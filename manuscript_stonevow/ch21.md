## Chapter Twenty-One — True Cost (Isla)

We walk back from Calder's Reach the following morning, exhausted in
the particular way grief exhausts a person, hollowed rather than
merely tired, and it is on that walk — past the last standing cairn,
back onto ground the line still properly holds — that the bond does
the thing it did once before, at a smaller waystone weeks ago, except
considerably larger now, entirely uncontrolled.

The morning itself is bright and bitterly cold, the specific clear
light that Coldwake sometimes offers after a hard frost, entirely
indifferent to the particular weight either of us is carrying home
from a village the cold already finished with, long ago.

Neither of us has spoken much since leaving the village, both of us
still properly absorbing the whole weight of what we found there, and
I am grateful, walking in that particular silence, that neither of us
feels the need to fill it with anything less than the truth currently
demands.

I feel it arrive all at once, cumulative, undeniable: not a single
scene's cost, the way it has arrived before, but every ward Aldric has
stood beside me for since the binding, stacked, compounding, the whole
sum of what six weeks of careful, well-intentioned ward-setting has
actually taken from him.

It is considerably more than the record I've been keeping accounts for.

I stop walking. I do not decide to stop; my own legs simply stop
answering properly, the way Aldric's hand stopped answering properly a
few nights ago, and I stand in the middle of the track with the whole
cumulative weight of what I've been doing to him arriving in my chest
at once, undeniable, no longer manageable by any arithmetic I currently
possess.

"How long have you been like this," I say, and my voice does something
I do not authorize — cracks, slightly, on the last word, in a way I
have not let my voice do in front of another person in longer than I
can accurately place.

"Since before you'd have noticed," Aldric says, quietly, "if you
weren't looking for it specifically."

"That's not an answer. That's an admission dressed up as an answer."

"It's the honest one," he says. "I don't know precisely when it
started, Isla. I know it's been building for weeks. I know it's
considerably worse than I've let either of us properly examine. I
didn't want to hand you a fact you couldn't do anything useful with,
except worry."

"You don't get to decide that for me," I say, and hear, in my own
voice, an anger that has nothing to do with him specifically and
everything to do with an arrangement — mine, the crown's, the whole
inherited weight of a rite I did not design and have spent six years
faithfully administering regardless — that has been quietly killing
the man standing in front of me while I kept careful, insufficient
account of the cost.

"I know," Aldric says, and does not argue further, which somehow makes
the whole of it considerably worse, because I understand, hearing him
simply accept the accusation rather than defend himself against it,
that he has already done this same arithmetic himself, privately, and
arrived at a conclusion he has been quietly bracing to deliver whenever
the moment finally forced his hand.

We stand on the track a long while, neither of us moving, both of us
absorbing the specific size of what has just become undeniable between
us.

"Whatever arrangement I thought I was managing," I say, eventually,
"it was never sustainable. Not the way I've been running it. Not
without either killing you outright or finding some other way to hold
the line that doesn't require spending one hearth quietly until there's
nothing left of him to spend."

"The record," Aldric says. "The one from Calder's Reach. The mutual
form."

"I don't know yet if it actually works," I say. "I don't know if it's
survivable, attempted wrong, or by two people the old rite-keepers never
sanctioned to try it. I know only that whatever I've been doing instead
isn't survivable either, and that I have been, without properly
reckoning with it until this exact moment, killing you by inches, one
carefully managed ward at a time."

"Then we find out," Aldric says, "whether the mutual form holds.
Together. Properly, this time — not managed quietly in the dark by
either of us alone."

I take his hand, there on the track, his good hand, the one that still
answers properly, and hold it the whole rest of the walk back to
Highstone, doing arithmetic that has, for the first time in six years,
stopped being solely mine to solve, and finding, in the sharing of it,
something that frightens me considerably less than the arithmetic
itself ever managed to.

Whatever this story was, walking out to Calder's Reach two days ago, it
is no longer that story. The true cost has arrived, all at once,
undeniable, and there is no version of what comes next that does not
require both of us, together, properly, to survive it.

·

We stop at the last standing cairn before the final stretch back to
Highstone, both of us needing, I think, a moment to properly gather
ourselves before facing Tomas and the day's ordinary business again,
and it is there, in the particular quiet of a standing stone that has
held its charge faithfully for longer than either of our own lifetimes,
that Aldric asks the question I have been half-dreading since the walk
began.

"What happens to Tomas," he says, "if the mutual rite doesn't work. If
Ossory won't sanction it, or the translation proves unreliable, or we
simply run out of time before we can properly test it."

"I don't know," I say, honestly, because I have decided that
half-truths cost more between us now, and because the honest answer is
considerably more frightening than any managed version I could offer
instead. "I know Feld's contingency doesn't disappear simply because
we've found a record suggesting an alternative. I know I am not
willing, under any circumstance, to let Tomas be bound the way you were
bound, without proper preparation, simply because the crown's patience
ran out before ours did."

"That's not really an answer to what happens," Aldric says, gently,
"only to what you're not willing to allow."

"I know," I admit. "I don't have the other half of the answer yet. I
think — I hope — that having proof the mutual form exists, even
imperfect proof, buys us enough standing with Ossory and the crown both
to argue for more time, regardless of how the actual attempt eventually
goes."

"And if it doesn't buy us that time?"

I look at him for a long moment, the cairn field silent around us, and
find myself, for the first time since Calder's Reach, considering a
question I have been carefully avoiding examining too closely.

"Then I fight for more time anyway," I say, "by whatever means are
available to me, because I am not willing to let this line's
arithmetic be solved on the backs of the two people I care about most
in the world, regardless of what the crown's own preferred timeline
demands."

Aldric doesn't have an answer to that, and I don't need one. We simply
stand at the cairn a while longer, gathering what strength either of us
has left before the walk resumes, and I find myself grateful, standing
there, for a man who lets my uncertainty stand unmanaged rather than
demanding I resolve it before I'm ready.

·

We find Tomas waiting at Highstone's own gate, having clearly been
watching the road since first light, and his relief at seeing us both
upright is visible enough that I understand, watching him, exactly how
much he has been quietly worrying in our absence.

"You were gone longer than planned," he says, careful not to make it an
accusation, though the worry underneath the observation is plain.

"We found considerably more than we expected to," I say, "and needed
the extra time to properly absorb it."

I tell him the whole of it, once we're settled in the record-room
properly — Calder's Reach, the record box, the well-house's fuller
transcription, Ossian's own grief, finally faced — and watch him
absorb each piece with the particular gravity he's shown, this whole
season, toward anything that touches the line's deeper history.

"What happens now," he asks, once I've finished.

"Now I take this to Ossory," I say, "properly, with actual evidence
rather than merely a theory. And we hope his own private doubts about
the standard rite are strong enough to let him hear it fairly."

"And if he won't hear it?"

"Then I find another way," I say, with more certainty than I
currently possess, because I have decided that Tomas, of everyone at
Highstone, deserves a version of me that sounds capable of handling
whatever comes, regardless of how uncertain the actual path forward
currently feels.

Tomas nods, accepting this, and busies himself immediately with
properly cataloguing the record box's contents, the specific practical
comfort of a young man who processes his own fear best through useful
work rather than further conversation. I let him have the task, understanding
that it serves him considerably better than any further reassurance I
could currently offer.

·

Aldric rests for the remainder of that day, at Cera's own firm
insistence, and I use the quiet hours to properly organize my
thoughts, drafting a careful outline of everything Ossory will need to
properly weigh — the predecessor's record, the well-house's fuller
transcription, Ossian's own private doubt, the whole accumulated weight
of evidence I've gathered since Calder's Reach first cracked open a
question I'd never thought to ask.

Sela arrives partway through the afternoon, having heard, through
whatever channel keeps her reliably informed, that something significant
happened on the walk home.

"Tell me everything," she says, settling in without waiting to be
asked, and I do, watching her absorb the whole of it with the specific
gravity she's shown, this whole season, toward anything touching her
brother's safety.

"Do you think Ossory will actually hear it properly," she asks, "or
will the crown's own caution simply bury it in deliberation until it's
too late to matter?"

"I don't know," I admit. "I hope the former. I've seen enough of his
own private doubt, these past weeks, to believe he genuinely wants to
be convinced. But wanting to be convinced and actually acting quickly
enough aren't always the same thing, in an institution built around
careful, deliberate process."

"Then we make certain he acts quickly," Sela says, with the specific
fierce practicality I've come to recognize as entirely her own. "Whatever
it takes. I'm not interested in watching my brother die of an
institution's caution, when an actual alternative sits available and
proven, at least in theory."

"Neither am I," I agree, and the two of us spend the rest of the
afternoon refining my presentation together, Sela's own plain,
unmanaged perspective proving considerably more useful than I expected,
sharpening my careful wardwright's argument into something considerably
harder for anyone to dismiss.

·

Aldric sleeps through most of that same afternoon, at Cera's own
instruction, and I find myself, once Sela has finally gone home,
sitting beside him rather than continuing the presentation alone,
watching him rest in a way I have rarely allowed myself the stillness
to properly do.

I think, watching him, about the specific arithmetic of loving someone
whose cost you are partially responsible for extracting — not guilt,
exactly, though guilt sits somewhere nearby, closer to the plain,
unmanageable fact of having spent six years administering a rite that
has been quietly killing the person currently sleeping in front of me,
without either of us properly reckoning with the scale of it until a
walk home from Calder's Reach forced the reckoning.

He wakes, eventually, finding me still there.

"You've been watching me sleep," he observes, not quite an accusation.

"I have," I admit. "I think I needed to see you resting properly, for
once, without either of us managing some crisis around it."

"How long have I been asleep?"

"Most of the afternoon. Sela and I worked on the presentation for
Ossory while you rested. I think it's considerably stronger for her
input."

Aldric considers this, still drowsy, and reaches for my hand.

"Thank you," he says, "for letting me rest, instead of insisting I be
part of every single piece of this. I don't think I'd have managed to
ask for that myself."

"I know," I say. "That's rather why I didn't wait for you to ask."

We sit together a while longer in the cottage's quiet afternoon light,
and I find myself, watching him properly wake, grateful for a
stillness neither of us has been able to properly afford in longer
than either of us can accurately place.

·

Tomas visits that same evening, ostensibly to deliver a final report,
actually, I suspect, simply to check on Aldric properly with his own
eyes rather than trust secondhand reassurance.

"You look better," he observes, settling into the third chair.

"I feel better," Aldric says. "Rested, for once, rather than merely
upright."

"Good," Tomas says, with visible relief. "I've been worried, this past
week, in a way I haven't quite said out loud to either of you."

"You can say it now," I offer.

Tomas considers this, gathering his thoughts properly.

"I've been frightened I'd lose you both," he admits, finally, "before
I'd properly learned everything I still need to learn. Not simply the
mechanics. The whole of what you've both taught me about actually
caring for the people this line touches, rather than merely managing
the arithmetic of it."

"You've learned considerably more than you give yourself credit for,"
I say.

"Maybe," Tomas says, "but I don't think I've learned enough yet. I
don't want to find out the hard way that I wasn't ready, because
neither of you had the time left to finish teaching me properly."

"We're not going anywhere," Aldric says, with more certainty than
either of us can strictly guarantee, though I understand, hearing it,
that he means it as a promise worth making regardless of the
arithmetic's actual odds.

"I'm holding you to that," Tomas says, with a small, watery smile, and
the three of us sit together a while longer in the cottage's gathering
evening warmth, each of us privately determined to make certain that
particular promise holds.

·

Sela arrives partway through the evening, drawn, I suspect, by the same
worry that brought Tomas, and settles in without needing an invitation,
the particular easy belonging of a woman who has, these past weeks,
folded herself properly into this household's own rhythms.

"You've got the whole family gathered," she observes, taking in the
cottage's small, crowded warmth.

"Apparently everyone needed the same reassurance tonight," I say.

"That's not a bad thing," Sela says, settling by the fire. "I think
I'd rather we all needed it at once, together, than each of us
managing our private version of the same worry separately."

"I agree," Aldric says, and the four of us spend the rest of the
evening together, the ordinary business of tea and conversation
filling the cottage's small space, and I find myself, watching this
whole assembled family settle into easy company, grateful for
precisely this kind of unremarkable evening — the one none of us,
six weeks ago, would have properly believed we'd get to have.

·

Sela lingers after Tomas has finally gone home, helping me settle the
last of the evening's dishes.

"Thank you for letting me stay tonight," she says. "I wasn't certain,
knocking, whether I was intruding on something that wasn't mine to
join."

"You're always welcome," I say, meaning it entirely. "I don't think
either of us properly knows how to separate 'our family' from 'your
family' anymore. I'm not certain we should try to."

"Neither am I," Sela says, with a small, genuine smile, and gathers
her things for the walk home, and I find myself, watching her go,
grateful for a family that has grown, this difficult winter, into
something considerably larger and considerably kinder than any of us
arrived expecting.

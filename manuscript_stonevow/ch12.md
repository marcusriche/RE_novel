## Chapter Twelve — Ask Before It Happens (Isla)

Dusk over the cairn field above Highstone, the light doing the
particular flat-gold thing it does in the last hour before the real
cold sets in properly for the night, and I have spent the better part
of the afternoon rehearsing a conversation I have never once, in my
adult life, had any practice conducting.

I chose this exact field deliberately, over the cottage's own more
comfortable fire — some part of me understanding that a conversation
this significant deserved the same weight of stone and open sky that
has witnessed every other significant thing in my professional life,
rather than the smaller, softer privacy of a kitchen table.

I have negotiated terms for a great many things. I have never negotiated
this specific one, and I find, walking out to the cairn field with
Aldric beside me, that the rehearsal has not made the actual doing of
it any easier.

"I want to ask you something plainly," I say, once we've reached the
field's edge, the standing stones grey-gold in the dying light around
us, "because I've watched what happens when nobody asks plainly first,
and I won't have that be us, whatever else we end up being to each
other."

"Go on," Aldric says, and I notice, saying it, that he has gone very
still — not guarded, exactly, simply attentive, giving the moment the
whole of his focus in the way he gives most things that matter to him.

"There's something between us," I say. "I've felt it since the binding.
I suspect you have too. I'm not willing to let the rite be the reason
it happens, if it happens — I've read enough about what the bond does,
unbidden, to know that neither of us can fully separate what's ours
from what the cord put there without either of us asking. So I'm
asking. Plainly. In words, the way words are supposed to work between
two people who actually mean them."

I make myself say the rest of it, though it costs me considerably more
than the beginning did.

"Do you want this. Not the binding. Not the rite's cost-sharing. The
actual thing sitting between us that neither of us has named yet."

Aldric doesn't answer immediately, and I let the silence stand, because
I have learned, watching him, that he takes questions like this
seriously enough to actually consider them rather than simply
performing an answer he thinks I want to hear.

"Yes," he says, finally, no hesitation once it arrives, no performance
in it at all. "Since before I knew your name, if I'm honest, which I'd
rather be with you specifically than with almost anyone else I've ever
known. I volunteered for this binding not knowing who the wardwright
would be. I'd have volunteered again, knowing it would be you, without
a single day's more consideration."

"That's not a small thing to admit."

"No," he agrees. "I don't think either of us has much use, anymore, for
the small versions of the things we actually mean."

"I need you to hear the rest of what I'm asking," I say, "because it
matters more than the first part. I'm asking whether you want this for
yourself. Not because the bond makes wanting it easier. Not because
proximity's done half the work already. I need to know the difference
matters to you as much as it matters to me, because I've watched what
happens to people who let a compelled thing stand in for a chosen one,
and I won't have that be either of us."

"It matters," Aldric says, plainly. "I've thought about the difference
myself, more than once, lying awake in the second room you built the
fire up in without being asked. I don't think what I feel is the bond
talking. I think the bond simply gave me faster access to something
that was already there, underneath, waiting for either of us to notice
it properly."

I ask him the same question back, in the same plain terms he's just
used with me, because it seems only fair that the asking run in both
directions rather than only mine.

"Do you want this," I say. "For yourself. Separate from whatever the
rite's made easier."

"Yes," Aldric says, without a moment's hesitation this time. "I've
wanted it since the road below Highstone, the first proper conversation
we had that wasn't strictly business. I didn't let myself examine it
closely, at first, because I wasn't certain it was mine to want, given
everything else the binding had already decided for both of us. I'm
certain now."

We stand at the cairn field's edge a while longer, neither of us moving
to close the distance, both of us, I think, understanding that the
asking itself is the point tonight — a vow, quoted and taken apart word
by word, the way the old texts describe examining a rite before you let
it become a single unexamined thing you simply trust without testing.

"There's one more thing," I say, because I have decided, somewhere in
the walk out here, that I am not leaving this field with anything
unsaid that might matter later. "If either of us ever needs this to
stop — for any reason, at any point, whether the reason makes sense to
the other person or not — I need us to be able to say so, and have it
actually stop, without either of us having to argue for the right to
change our mind."

"Agreed," Aldric says, immediately, entirely. "I'd want the same
thing said back to me, if the need ever runs the other direction."

"It's said," I say. "Plainly. In words. The way this whole conversation
has been."

Nothing further happens between us tonight, out at the cairn field, in
the last of the flat gold light. Nothing needs to. The asking has been
the whole of what either of us came out here for, and I walk back
toward the cottage beside him with something in my chest that feels,
for the first time in six years, considerably less like a cost and
considerably more like a choice.

·

I do not sleep properly that night, though not for any of the reasons
that have kept me awake in the weeks since the binding. I lie instead
turning the whole conversation over, checking it the way I check a
ward's charge, looking for the seam where the asking might have been
less honest than it felt in the moment, and do not find one, however
many times I run the check.

I think about my mother, briefly, the way I've found myself doing more
often since finally telling Aldric about her a few weeks ago — the
first time I'd said her name aloud to anyone at Highstone in the whole
of my adult life. Fragments, mostly, are all I have to think with: the
particular blue of a shawl she wore constantly, the smell of bread she
baked every third day without fail, a warmth I have spent considerably
more of my adult life missing than I generally let myself admit.

I wonder, lying awake, what she would have made of Aldric. I suspect,
turning the thought over, that she would have approved — she valued
steadiness over spectacle, by what little I remember of her, much the
way I find myself valuing it now, watching a man who mends gloves
before dawn and says nothing about it afterward.

I get up, eventually, unable to properly sleep, and go to the window,
and look out at the cairn field in the dark, the standing stones just
visible under a sky gone properly clear for once, and think, watching
them, that I have spent six years believing this line was the whole of
what I had to hold onto in this world. I understand now, watching those
same stones with an entirely different weight sitting in my chest, that
I was wrong about that in a way I am only beginning to properly
measure.

·

I fall asleep, eventually, at the window, and wake near dawn to find
Aldric has come looking for me, quiet, careful not to startle me out of
whatever rest I'd finally managed.

"You didn't sleep," he says, not quite a question, taking in the cold
window, the untouched bed behind me.

"I couldn't," I admit. "I kept turning the whole evening over, checking
it for a seam I never found."

"And now?"

"Now I'm considerably more tired," I say, "and considerably more
certain than I was at midnight that whatever we said to each other
tonight was entirely honest, on both sides."

Aldric sits beside me at the window, and we watch the last of the dark
give way to the particular grey of a Coldwake dawn together, neither of
us saying much of anything further, simply sharing the specific quiet
satisfaction of two people who have, for once, managed to stay awake
turning over the same good thing rather than the same frightening one.

"I should tell you something," he says, eventually, "that I've been
turning over myself, these past few nights."

"Go on."

"I wrote down a list of everything I'm afraid of, a few nights back.
I've never done that before — simply named the fears, plainly, rather
than letting them manage themselves in the back of my head."

"What was on it?"

"That the rite takes more than either of us accounts for. That you'll
spend yourself trying to stop that. And, underneath both of those, that
whatever we've built won't survive whatever's still coming." He looks
at me, plainly, in the grey dawn light. "I wanted you to know the list
exists. Not necessarily every item on it. Simply that I've started
naming my own fears properly, the way you've been teaching me to,
rather than managing them silently the way I always have before."

"Thank you," I say, "for telling me it exists," and take his hand, and
we sit together at the window a while longer, watching Coldwake's
particular grey dawn finish arriving, considerably less afraid, both of
us, for having named at least some part of what frightens us properly,
out loud, to someone who's chosen to stay and hear it.

·

Tomas finds us both still at the window when he arrives for the
morning's business, and takes one look at the state of us — clearly
awake all night, considerably more unguarded than our usual professional
composure — before wisely deciding not to comment directly.

"I've brought the overnight readings," he says instead, setting down
his reports with careful diplomacy, "and I'll just note, for my own
records, that neither of you appears to have slept, which I am choosing
to interpret generously."

"Interpret it however you like, Tomas."

"I generally do," he says, with his usual dry satisfaction, and goes to
put the kettle on, leaving the two of us to properly rouse ourselves
for the day's actual work.

I find myself, going through the morning's ordinary business —
checking readings, reviewing Tomas's own overnight notes, the whole
familiar rhythm of six years of practice — considerably more grateful
than usual for the specific steadiness of routine. Whatever fears
either of us named at the window before dawn, the line still needs
holding, the cairns still need checking, and there is, I find, a
particular comfort in discovering that fear and duty can coexist
without either one canceling out the other.

"You're smiling," Aldric observes, midway through the morning's
reports.

"I'm considering," I say, "that I might actually be alright. Not
fixed. Not certain. But alright, in the way that matters, this
particular morning," and he smiles back, and we return to the day's
work together, considerably steadier for the night's honesty than
either of us managed before it.

·

Sela stops by later that same afternoon, ostensibly to deliver a basket
of the orchard's last stored produce, and catches the specific quality
of ease between Aldric and me before either of us has said a single
word about the previous night.

"Something's different," she observes, setting down the basket,
studying us both with the particular assessing look I've come to
recognize as entirely her own. "You're both entirely too pleased with
yourselves for a Tuesday."

"We had an honest conversation," I say, "about what we actually want,
from each other, going forward."

"That's rather a large admission, delivered rather casually," Sela
says, though I catch the genuine warmth underneath her dry tone.

"I've decided casual delivery is easier than the alternative," I say.
"I'm still learning how to say large things without making an
occasion of them."

"I approve of the method," Sela says. "Occasions have a way of making
people manage their words too carefully. I'd rather hear the truth
delivered plainly, over a basket of apples, than dressed up for some
formal moment neither of you actually needs."

She stays a while, the three of us settling into the particular easy
rhythm that's become, these past weeks, one of my favorite parts of
the day, and I find myself, watching Aldric tease his sister about the
basket's suspiciously generous size, grateful for a family that has,
somehow, made room for me this quickly and this completely.

"I like watching the two of you like this," Sela says, eventually,
quieter. "I want you to know that. I wasn't certain, at first, that I
would."

"I know," I say. "I'm grateful you've let yourself get there anyway."

"So am I," Sela says, and the three of us sit together a while longer
in the cottage's afternoon light, considerably more like family than
circumstance, however this whole arrangement first began.

·

Sela lingers after the basket's been properly emptied, watching Aldric
step out to check the woodpile, and turns to me with a considering
look I haven't quite seen from her before.

"Can I ask you something," she says, "properly, before he comes back?"

"Of course."

"Are you happy," she asks, plainly, "genuinely, not the professional
composure you show the village. I've watched my brother's face,
these past weeks, and I recognize the particular lightness in it. I
haven't asked, until now, whether you're carrying the same lightness,
or simply managing his happiness alongside your own duties."

I consider the question properly, surprised by how much it means,
coming from her specifically.

"I am," I say, finally. "Genuinely. I don't think I've been properly
happy, in this particular way, since before my mentor died. I'd
forgotten what it felt like, honestly, until these past weeks reminded
me."

Sela nods, satisfied, something in her posture easing.

"Good," she says. "I needed to hear that plainly, from you, rather than
simply assume it from watching him. I've spent my whole life
protecting my brother's happiness without anyone thinking to ask
whether mine, or anyone else's, mattered equally in the equation."

"I'll ask you the same, sometime," I say, "when you're ready to answer
it."

"I'll hold you to that," Sela says, with a small, genuine smile, and
Aldric returns from the woodpile none the wiser about the conversation
that's just passed between the two women he loves most, and the three
of us settle back into the afternoon's easy quiet, considerably more
honestly connected for the small, private exchange.

·

Tomas arrives just as Sela's preparing to leave, catching her at the
door with a shy eagerness I haven't quite seen from him before.

"I wanted to walk partway back with you," he offers, "if that's
welcome. I have a report to deliver at the orchard anyway."

"I'd like the company," Sela says, and something in her voice carries
a warmth I file away carefully, the way I file most small, promising
details this winter keeps handing me.

They set off together, and I watch them go from the doorway, Aldric
coming to stand beside me.

"Do you see it too," he asks, quietly.

"I do," I say. "I think I'm going to enjoy watching that particular
story unfold, whatever shape it eventually takes."

"So am I," Aldric agrees, and we go back inside together, considerably
warmed by the small, hopeful thread we've just watched walk out the
door.

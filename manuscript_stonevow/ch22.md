## Chapter Twenty-Two — Sela's Ledger (Aldric)

Sela shows Isla the winter grain count without being asked a second
time, trusting her now the way she didn't quite manage at their first
meeting in the grain-house, a fortnight and a half ago, when the two of
them were still sizing each other up across a counting table neither
had yet decided to fully open.

I notice the shift in my sister's manner immediately, watching the two
of them work through the ledger together — no longer the careful,
measured distance of a woman deciding how much to reveal, simply two
people comparing honest numbers with the particular ease of people
who've already decided to trust each other's arithmetic.

The count is bad. Considerably worse than bad, if the line keeps its
current pace through the rest of Coldwake, worse still if my own
binding fails before spring properly arrives to ease the worst of the
season's demands.

"He'd never tell you it's this close," Sela says, to Isla, laying out
the full shape of it — grain traded away in lean years, a margin that
has been shrinking steadily since the crown tightened the exemption
terms, a household that has been quietly absorbing costs for
considerably longer than the two winters since I put my own name
forward at the rite. "He didn't tell me either, for months, when it
was his exemption on the line instead of yours."

I say nothing, standing near the grain-house door, because there is
nothing useful to say to an accurate accounting of my own worst habit,
laid out plainly by the one person who has known me longest and trusts
me least to manage it honestly.

Isla listens to the whole of it, and I watch her recognize, in real
time, the family shape of my own flaw — a household that has been
quietly absorbing costs and calling it management for at least two
generations, long enough that nobody currently living properly
remembers when the habit started.

"I'm not going to let this cost him what it cost your father," Isla
says, to Sela, with a specific certainty that surprises me, hearing
it — a promise made before she's fully worked out how she intends to
keep it, offered anyway, because the alternative is watching another
Marrow quietly spend himself into the ground without anyone properly
naming what's happening while there's still time to stop it.

"I'd like to believe that," Sela says, "but I've watched him make
himself indispensable to this family's survival since he was fourteen
years old, and I've never once managed to talk him out of it. What
makes you think you'll manage where I've failed for most of his life?"

"I don't think I'll talk him out of it," Isla says. "I think I'll share
it with him instead, until the habit stops being something either of
us can do quietly, alone, because there won't be an alone left to do it
in."

Sela looks at her for a long moment, assessing, and something in her
face settles — not full trust, not yet, but the specific loosening of a
woman who has been braced, for years, against watching her brother's
pattern repeat itself unchecked, finally allowing herself to consider
that this particular repetition might, this once, actually be
interrupted.

"He's lucky, then," Sela says, "that the rite happened to bind him to
someone who recognizes the pattern instead of simply admiring it."

I do not have a ready response to being discussed, quite so plainly,
by the two women currently deciding, between them, how best to manage
my own worst habit, so I simply busy myself checking the grain-house's
door hinge, which does not, in fact, need checking, until the
conversation moves on to something considerably less directly about me.

·

Walking back down from the orchard that evening, Isla is quiet in the
particular way that means she's still turning something over.

"Your sister loves you considerably more plainly than you let yourself
notice," she says, eventually.

"I notice," I say. "I've just never found a good way to let it change
how I actually behave, day to day."

"Maybe that changes now," Isla says, "given everything else that's
changing. I'd like it to, at least. I don't think either of us can
afford, this winter, to keep managing our worst habits alone simply
because we've always managed them that way before."

"Agreed," I say, and mean it, though I understand, saying it, that
agreeing to change a habit this deeply worn into a person is
considerably easier said, walking down a dark road with someone you've
decided to trust, than actually done, in the moment, when the old
instinct to carry everything alone reasserts itself the way old
instincts always do.

·

Sela finds me alone in the orchard the next afternoon, mending a
section of fence that has been slowly failing since the last hard
frost, and watches me work for a while before she says anything at all,
which I have learned, over twenty-eight years, to recognize as a sign
she's been thinking through exactly how to phrase whatever's actually
on her mind.

"I like her," she says, finally. "I want you to know that plainly,
because I don't say it about many people, and because I think you've
spent so long assuming I'll disapprove of whatever choice you make that
you might not properly hear it when I don't."

"I hear it," I say. "Thank you."

"I mean it beyond simply liking her company," Sela continues. "I mean
I've watched the two of you these past weeks, and I've watched
something in you soften that I genuinely believed, some years ago, had
gone permanently rigid. I didn't think anyone was going to manage that.
I'm glad to be wrong."

I set down the fence post, considering how to answer that properly.

"I didn't think so either," I admit. "I'd made my peace with being the
reliable one, permanently, the way Father was. I didn't think there was
room in that arrangement for anyone to actually reach me."

"Father made his peace with it too," Sela says, "and I don't think it
was ever real peace, if I'm honest. I think it was simply exhaustion,
dressed up as contentment, because the alternative — actually needing
someone, after Mother — felt like too large a risk to properly
consider."

"You think I've been doing the same thing."

"I think you've been doing exactly the same thing," Sela says, "since
you were old enough to notice how much easier it was to manage
everyone else's needs than to admit you had any of your own. I'm not
saying it to blame you for it. I'm saying it because I'd like you to
notice, properly, that whatever's happening with Isla is the first
genuine crack in that particular armor I've seen in twenty years of
watching you wear it."

I don't have a ready response to being seen quite that precisely by my
own sister, so I simply go back to the fence, and let her words settle
alongside everything else I am slowly learning to examine rather than
simply manage, grateful, in the specific quiet way I've always been
grateful for Sela, that she has never once let me get away with the
comfortable version of my own story.

·

I ask her, before I leave the orchard that afternoon, the question I've
been turning over since Isla and I discussed it on the walk home from
the boundary marker — whether there's anything Sela has needed from me,
over the years, that she never felt able to ask for directly.

Sela looks at me for a long moment, clearly surprised by the question,
and I watch her decide, visibly, whether to answer it with the polite
deflection she generally reserves for anything that might make either
of us uncomfortable, or with something considerably more honest.

"I wanted you to ask me, once, just once, whether taking your exemption
was actually what I wanted," she says, finally, quietly. "Not whether I
was grateful. I was, enormously. But nobody ever asked whether I
wanted it done that way, or whether I might have preferred to face the
crown's terms myself, on my own footing, rather than be protected from
a choice I never got to make."

I absorb that properly, understanding, hearing it, exactly how much
weight has apparently sat underneath her gratitude for considerably
longer than I realized.

"I'm sorry," I say. "I should have asked, at the time. I decided, the
way I always decide, that protecting you mattered more than consulting
you."

"I know," Sela says. "I've made my peace with it, mostly. I'm not
asking you to undo it. I'm simply answering the question you finally
got around to asking, three years late."

"Better three years late than never," I say, "though I understand if
that's not much comfort."

"It's more comfort than you'd think," Sela says, and reaches over to
squeeze my hand, briefly, before going back to her own work, and I
walk back down from the orchard that evening carrying the specific,
complicated weight of having finally asked a question I should have
asked considerably sooner, grateful, at least, that it's asked now
rather than never at all.

·

I tell Isla about the conversation that same evening, curious what
she'll make of it.

"I think that took considerable courage," she says, "asking a question
you'd spent years assuming you already knew the answer to."

"I nearly didn't ask it," I admit. "I stood at her door for a full
minute working up the nerve, the same way I stood at the cairn field
edge before I first properly asked you anything."

"What made you finally ask?"

"You did," I say, simply. "Watching you insist on the plain question,
every time, regardless of how frightening the possible answer might be.
I found I couldn't keep asking that of you without eventually asking it
of the people I've spent my whole life protecting instead of properly
consulting."

Isla takes my hand, considering this.

"I think that's rather the best compliment anyone's given me this
whole winter," she says, "that watching me ask hard questions taught
you to ask your own."

"It's not a compliment," I say. "It's simply true. You've changed how
I move through the world, in ways I don't think either of us fully
appreciated until just now, sitting here, adding up all the small ways
it's actually happened."

We sit together a while longer, and I find myself, turning the whole
conversation over, grateful for a woman who has taught me, simply by
her own example, that asking a hard question properly is its own kind
of love, considerably more honest than any quiet, well-meaning
protection could ever manage to be.

·

Tomas asks me, the following day, whether I'd be willing to help him
draft a similar question for someone in his own life — a cousin,
apparently, in a village two days south, from whom he's been quietly
estranged since his mother's death, over some old grievance neither of
them has properly named in three years.

"I don't know how to ask it without it sounding like an accusation," he
admits. "I've drafted it four times already. It keeps coming out
wrong."

"Show me," I say, and he does, and I understand, reading it, exactly
what's gone wrong with each attempt — every version manages the
cousin's likely reaction rather than simply asking the plain question
underneath it.

"You're doing what I used to do," I tell him, gently. "Managing the
asking so carefully that the actual question gets lost underneath all
the careful cushioning."

"What would you write instead?"

"Something closer to: *I've missed you these three years, and I don't
fully understand what came between us. Would you tell me, plainly,
what I did, so I can properly apologize for it rather than simply
guessing?*"

Tomas considers this, turning the plainer version over.

"That's considerably more frightening to send," he says.

"It is," I agree. "It's also considerably more likely to actually
work. The comfortable version protects you from an honest answer. I
don't think that's what you actually want, is it?"

"No," Tomas admits. "I want to know. Even if the knowing hurts."

"Then send the frightening version," I say, "and let me know if it
gets you the answer you're actually looking for."

He drafts the letter properly that same evening, considerably plainer
than his first four attempts, and I find myself, watching him seal it,
grateful for the specific way this whole difficult winter keeps
teaching everyone in its orbit the same hard lesson, over and over, in
whatever shape each person happens to need it delivered.

·

The reply arrives some ten days later, considerably faster than Tomas
expected, and I find him at the record-room table holding it unopened,
turning it over in both hands.

"You don't have to read it in front of anyone," I say, gently.

"I know," Tomas says. "I think I want to, though. I think I'd rather
have company for it, whatever it says."

He opens it carefully, and I watch his face as he reads, the specific
unguarded relief that crosses it partway through.

"She wants to see me," he says, looking up. "Properly. She says she's
sorry too, that she let the silence run this long over something that,
reading her account of it, sounds almost embarrassingly small now that
either of us has finally named it plainly."

"How does that feel," I ask, "hearing it named."

"Considerably lighter than I expected," Tomas admits. "I think I'd
built the whole estrangement up into something larger than it actually
was, simply by leaving it unexamined for three years."

"That happens," I say. "Silence has a way of making small things grow
considerably larger than they'd have stayed, properly named early."

"I'm glad I sent the frightening version," Tomas says, "rather than the
comfortable one. I don't think the comfortable version would have
gotten me here."

"Neither do I," I agree, and we sit together a while longer, both of
us grateful for a small, private reconciliation achieved through the
same plain honesty this whole family has spent the season learning to
practice.

·

I mention it to Isla that evening, curious what she'll make of the
whole outcome, once Tomas has gone home considerably lighter than he
arrived.

"It went well," I say. "Considerably better than either of them
expected, from what he told me. They spent most of the visit simply
catching up, rather than relitigating whatever caused the original
rift."

"That's rather how most reconciliations seem to go, once the hard part
of the asking is actually managed," Isla observes.

"I think that's true," I say. "The asking is almost always the
frightening part. Once the plain question's actually been put, the
answer tends to be considerably kinder than the years of avoided
silence would suggest it might be."

"I hope that holds for the rest of us too," Isla says, "whatever
hard questions any of us still have left to ask."

"I think it will," I say, "given how much practice we've all had this
winter," and we sit together a while longer, both of us grateful for a
season that keeps, in ways large and small, teaching this whole
extended family the same steadying lesson about the plain question's
particular power.

·

Tomas tells me, a few days later, that his cousin has asked to visit
Highstone properly, once the roads clear enough for the journey.

"I'd like that," I say. "I think it would do you good, having her see
where you've landed, properly, after everything."

"I think so too," Tomas says. "I want her to meet all of you. Aldric,
Sela. I think I've become someone rather different than the boy who
left home three years ago, and I'd like her to see the difference for
herself, rather than simply hear about it in a letter."

"I think she'll be proud of who you've become," I say, and mean it
entirely.

"I hope so," Tomas says, with a small, hopeful smile, and I find
myself, watching him, grateful for a reconciliation that has already
begun, in its own quiet way, to widen this family's own generous
circle even further.

## Chapter Sixteen — What Tomas Is For (Isla)

Tomas has started drilling the binding-rite words in the evenings,
quietly, on his own initiative, in the record-room after the day's
reports are filed, in a voice pitched low enough that he clearly
believes I haven't noticed.

I notice everything in that room. It is, after all, the room I have
spent six years making certain nothing escapes my attention in, on the
theory that the cost of missing something important there is
considerably higher than the cost of noticing something I'd rather not
have seen.

"I'm not putting myself forward," Tomas says, before I can ask, the
moment he realizes I've caught him at it. "I'm just — making sure I'm
ready. If it comes to that. Someone has to be, and it's better it's
someone who's actually prepared than someone conscripted at the last
possible hour with no proper training at all."

"You're nineteen," I say, which is not, strictly, an argument, though I
mean it as one.

"You were nineteen when you started your own apprenticeship," Tomas
says, which is true, and which I do not have a ready counter for,
mostly because I have spent considerable effort, over three years,
avoiding exactly this comparison.

I cannot argue against his readiness without admitting the exact thing
I am not yet prepared to admit to him — that I have been feeding the
failing wards more of myself than the rite alone accounts for, for two
years now, and do not know, with any precision, how much longer that
particular arithmetic holds before it fails entirely, taking me with
it, regardless of whether Feld's contingency ever technically becomes
necessary.

"Keep drilling," I say, finally, because I do not have a version of
this conversation available to me that ends any other way, and because
telling him to stop would mean explaining why, and explaining why would
mean admitting a debt I have not yet found the nerve to hand anyone,
including myself, the full weight of.

It costs me more than telling him to stop would have. I feel the cost
of it settle somewhere behind my ribs, a specific, familiar ache that
has nothing to do with the bond and everything to do with six years of
practice at silently absorbing exactly this kind of weight.

Aldric finds me afterward, in the cottage, sitting with a cup of tea
gone cold in both hands, staring at nothing in particular, and does not
ask what's wrong, exactly — he has learned, in the weeks since the
binding, that I answer direct questions about my own state considerably
worse than I answer the simple offer of company.

He sits with me instead, in the specific kind of silence that isn't
asking for anything back, and I find, some while later, that the ache
behind my ribs has settled into something more manageable, simply for
having had someone else sitting near enough to notice it was there at
all.

"Tomas is drilling the rite words," I say, eventually, because I have
decided, somewhere in the silence, that this particular fact deserves
saying aloud to someone.

"I gathered as much," Aldric says. "I heard him through the record-room
door on my way in. He's got the cadence nearly right already."

"He shouldn't have to have the cadence right at all. Not at nineteen.
Not for years yet, if I have any say in how this line gets handed
forward."

"You do have a say," Aldric says. "You're the one holding it currently.
That counts for something."

"It counts for exactly as long as I can keep holding it," I say, "and I
don't know, with any precision, how long that actually is."

Aldric doesn't press for the specifics, though I feel him wanting to,
the particular restraint of a man who has learned, watching me
carefully over these past weeks, when a question would help and when
it would simply add another weight to a room already carrying more than
its fair share.

"Whatever the number turns out to be," he says, instead, "I don't
think you're carrying it alone anymore. Whether or not you've decided
to believe that yet."

I do not have an answer to that which doesn't risk unraveling
considerably more than a cup of cold tea and an evening's quiet
conversation can properly hold, so I simply let the sentence stand, and
drink the tea anyway, cold as it's gone, because finishing it feels,
tonight, like the one small thing I can still reliably manage on my
own.

·

I find Tomas the following morning already at the record-room table,
the rite-cadence book open in front of him, and instead of the usual
gentle redirection I've given him each time I've caught him at this
particular study, I sit down across from him and let him actually
practice it aloud, properly, watching his form the way Ossian once
watched mine.

"You're rushing the third verse," I say. "The cadence wants to slow
there, not speed up. It's the part where the rite asks the hearth's
name to be spoken, and a name spoken too quickly loses its proper
weight."

Tomas repeats it, slower this time, and the improvement is immediate
and considerable — the particular satisfaction of watching a student
correct something the moment it's named properly, rather than needing
the correction repeated three times before it lands.

"You're good at this," I say, surprised, slightly, by how plainly true
the observation is. "Better than I was, at your age."

"You're only saying that to make me feel better about needing to learn
it at all."

"I'm saying it because it's accurate," I say. "I fumbled the second
verse for the better part of a year. You've had it right since your
second attempt."

Tomas looks, for a moment, genuinely pleased by the compliment, in the
specific unguarded way of a young man who doesn't receive plain praise
often enough to have built up any defense against it.

"I don't want to need this," he says, after a moment, quieter. "The
cadence. I don't want a world where I actually have to use it properly,
because using it properly means either of you failed to hold the
line."

"I know," I say. "I'd rather you never need it either. But I'd rather
you have it, properly learned, and never need it, than need it one day
and not have it at all. That's rather the whole logic behind most of
what I teach you."

"That's a very grim way of framing an education."

"It's an honest way," I say, "which I've decided, this whole autumn,
matters considerably more than the comfortable version."

We work through two more verses before the day's proper business calls
either of us away, and I find myself, watching him go, considerably
prouder of the young man across that table than I have properly let
myself feel in the whole three years of his apprenticeship — a pride
that has nothing to do with contingency planning at all, and
everything to do with simply watching someone I've taught become,
slowly, precisely, exactly the wardwright this line deserves.

·

Aldric finds me still in the record-room afterward, and asks, gently,
how the lesson went.

"Better than I expected," I say. "He has real talent for it, considerably
more than I had at his age. I keep catching myself proud of him in a
way that surprises me, given how carefully I've tried not to let myself
get too attached to any single apprentice — a habit I picked up from
watching how much Ossian's own death cost me, and deciding, at the
time, that I'd rather not build that particular vulnerability twice."

"Is it too late to avoid it now?"

"Considerably too late," I admit. "I think I've been attached to Tomas
since roughly his second month here, if I'm honest with myself about
it. I've simply managed the attachment quietly, the way I manage most
things, rather than letting it show."

"What changed, that you're letting it show now?"

"You did," I say, simply. "Watching you be honest about your own
attachments — to Sela, to the orchard, to whatever this is between us
— has made it considerably harder to keep pretending mine don't exist
either."

Aldric smiles at that, the specific unguarded smile that has become,
these past weeks, one of my favorite things to notice arriving on his
face.

"I'm glad," he says, "that Tomas gets to have a wardwright who's
willing to be proud of him properly, rather than managing the pride
quietly the way you might have a season ago."

"So am I," I agree, and we sit together a while longer in the
record-room's particular evening quiet, both of us considerably more
willing, these days, to let the people we care about actually see how
much we care.

·

Aldric arrives partway through the evening, having heard, from Tomas
himself, about the day's cadence lesson, and finds the two of us still
sitting over the practice book, considerably more relaxed than the
formal instruction warrants.

"He tells me he's better at the cadence than you were at his age," he
says, settling into the spare chair.

"He is," I say, without hesitation. "I don't think there's any use
pretending otherwise, simply to preserve my own pride."

"I like that about you," Aldric says. "You don't seem to need to be
the best at everything, the way I've watched some people manage their
own authority."

"I used to," I admit. "I think I needed, for a long time, to be
unquestionably capable at everything the job required, because
questioning meant admitting I might actually need help. I've been
learning, this winter, that admitting a student's better at a
particular skill than I was at his age isn't a threat to my own
competence. It's simply a fact, and rather a good one, given that he's
going to inherit this whole line eventually."

"That's a healthier relationship with pride than most people manage,"
Aldric says.

"I'm working on it," I say, "the same as everything else this winter's
asking me to work on," and he takes my hand, and we sit together in the
record-room's quiet a while longer, three people — soon joined by
Tomas himself, returning for one final question about the fourth
verse — building, slowly, the particular kind of found family none of
us quite expected this arrangement to produce.

·

Tomas lingers after the fourth verse is properly settled, turning
something over that I can see costs him some effort to raise.

"Can I ask you something," he says, "that isn't about the cadence."

"Of course."

"Do you ever regret it," he asks. "Taking this apprenticeship, I mean.
Taking me on, specifically, when you could have simply managed the
line alone, the way Ossian apparently expected the next wardwright
eventually would."

The question surprises me, though I understand, considering it
properly, exactly why he'd want to ask it now, in the particular
vulnerable aftermath of an evening spent being praised for talents he's
clearly worked hard to develop.

"Never," I say, plainly, without needing to consider it further. "I
think taking you on was one of the better decisions I've made in six
years of this post. I don't say that to flatter you. I say it because
watching you become who you're becoming has given this whole exhausting
line a future I wasn't at all certain it had, before you arrived."

Tomas's composure does something small and unguarded at that, the
particular relief of a young man who has apparently been quietly
carrying the fear that his presence might be more burden than gift.

"I worried, sometimes," he admits, "that I was simply one more cost
you were managing, alongside the cairns and the crown's ledgers."

"You were never a cost," I say. "You've been, since roughly your
second month here, one of the few genuinely good things this line has
handed me. I should have told you that plainly, considerably sooner
than tonight."

"Better late than never," Tomas says, echoing, I notice, the exact
phrase Aldric used with Sela, and I wonder, watching him, whether he's
picked it up secondhand from overhearing one of us, or arrived at the
same wisdom independently, the way people who spend enough time in the
same household eventually start to.

"Thank you," he says, "for telling me. I think I needed to hear it
properly, rather than simply assumed."

"I'll keep telling you," I say, "as often as it takes to properly
land," and he nods, satisfied, and gathers his things for the night,
and I watch him go with the particular warm ache of a woman who has,
somewhere in these past three years, come to love a student as
fiercely and as plainly as she loves anyone else in her considerably
expanded family.

·

Aldric finds me still sitting in the record-room some while after
Tomas has gone, the cadence book still open on the table in front of
me though I've stopped properly reading it.

"You're thinking about him," he observes, settling in across from me.

"I keep thinking I should have told him considerably sooner," I say,
"how much he actually means to me, rather than letting three years pass
before saying it plainly."

"You told him tonight," Aldric says. "That's not nothing."

"I know," I say. "I think I'm simply grieving, a little, all the years
I spent managing my own affection quietly, the way I manage most
things, rather than letting him have the plain benefit of it sooner."

Aldric takes my hand across the table, considering this properly.

"I don't think it's ever too late to say a true thing," he says,
"even if it arrives later than either of us would prefer. I think the
saying itself matters considerably more than the timing."

"I hope you're right," I say.

"I know I'm right about this one," Aldric says, with a small, certain
smile, "having only recently learned the exact same lesson myself,
rather more slowly than you managed it with Tomas tonight."

We sit together in the record-room's quiet a while longer, and I find
myself, closing the cadence book at last, considerably lighter for
having finally said the true thing out loud, however many years it
took either of us to arrive at the saying.

·

I mention the whole evening to Sela the following day, curious what
she'll make of it, and watch her absorb the account with visible
warmth.

"That sounds like exactly the kind of thing Tomas needed to hear," she
says. "I've watched him, these past weeks, quietly worrying that his
place here was somehow conditional."

"I don't think I properly realized how deep that worry ran," I admit,
"until he finally said it plainly."

"Most people's deepest worries stay hidden precisely that long," Sela
says, "until someone finally makes it safe enough to say them." She
considers me for a moment. "You've gotten rather good at that,
yourself, this winter. Making things safe enough to say."

"I've had considerable practice," I say, "given how much everyone in
this family apparently needed the same lesson, delivered at roughly
the same time."

Sela laughs at that, properly, and we spend the rest of the visit
comparing notes on exactly how much this whole difficult season has
taught each of us, in our own particular ways, about the plain,
underrated power of simply saying the true thing out loud.

·

Tomas mentions, a few days later, that he's started drafting his own
version of the same lesson into his fledgling ledger — a small
collection of things he wishes someone had said to him plainly, sooner,
rather than left him to discover slowly on his own.

"May I see it, someday," I ask, "once it's further along?"

"I'd like that," Tomas says, somewhat shyly. "I think I'd value your
opinion on whether I've actually captured the lesson properly, or
whether I'm simply repeating what I think I'm supposed to have
learned."

"I trust you've captured it properly," I say. "You've had excellent
teachers, this winter, in exactly this particular subject."

"I have," Tomas agrees, with a warm, genuine smile, and goes back to
his careful drafting, and I find myself, watching him work, considerably
proud of an apprentice determined to pass this hard-won lesson forward
rather than simply keep it for himself.

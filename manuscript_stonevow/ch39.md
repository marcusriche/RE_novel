## Chapter Thirty-Nine — Grafting Days (Aldric)

Spring is not close, not truly — Coldwake does not release its hold on
the northern line simply because one cairn's crisis has passed — but
the orchard has stopped sounding like a countdown and started sounding,
again, like a place things are grafted rather than merely endured.

I notice the difference most in the quality of the quiet up here now —
no longer the held-breath silence of a man waiting for the next
crisis, simply the ordinary hush of an orchard going about its own
patient business, regardless of what season presses in around it.

I graft a new branch onto the crooked tree from my childhood, the one
that survived a hard frost two winters back and stopped bearing fruit
for its trouble, working around two fingers still slow to properly
answer me rather than pretending they aren't there at all, a
compensation I have stopped hiding from Isla or from myself.

"Name this one," I say, handing her the grafting knife, an act of trust
considerably larger than the simple gesture looks — I have never, in
my whole life, handed anyone else the naming of a graft I've made
myself.

Isla considers the branch for a long moment, turning the knife over in
her own hand with the specific care of a woman who understands exactly
how much this particular offer costs me to make.

"I'll name it for the day the cairn held," she says, finally. "Not the
day it cracked. The day after — the day we walked back from it, both
of us, still standing, on our own feet, uncertain whether either of us
would survive the winter and finding, regardless, that we had."

"That's a good name," I say, and mean it, watching her make the first
careful cut into the graft, precise, unhurried, the way she does most
things she's decided are worth doing properly.

Sela watches the two of us from the grain-house door, a basket of the
season's last stored apples balanced on one hip, and says nothing at
all, which is, from her, the highest praise currently available.

"You've gone soft," she calls over, eventually, unable to resist for
long. "Both of you. It's disgusting. I approve entirely."

"You're welcome to disapprove instead," I call back, "if the approval's
costing you too much dignity to maintain."

"I'll manage," Sela says, and goes back inside, and I catch Isla
smiling at the exchange in the specific unguarded way she's stopped, in
these past weeks, trying to hide from either of us.

We work the rest of the afternoon on smaller grafts, ordinary orchard
business, nothing urgent, nothing costed against any ledger either of
us is currently keeping, and I find myself, watching her hands learn
the unfamiliar work of grafting alongside my own more practiced ones,
considerably happier than I have any memory of being before this
particular winter arrived and rearranged the whole of my life around a
woman I did not choose and would not, now, given the choice again,
choose any differently.

"Tell me something," Isla says, later, as the light starts to fail and
neither of us has made any move to head back down toward Highstone
yet. "If the rite had bound you to someone else entirely — some other
wardwright, sight unseen, the way it very nearly could have — do you
think you'd have found a way to love them too? Or was this always,
somehow, specifically about the two of us?"

I think about it seriously for a long moment, because the question is
too large for a quick, comfortable answer.

"I don't know," I say, honestly. "I think I'd have found a way to be
decent to whoever the rite named. I don't think decency and this" — I
gesture, vaguely, at the whole of the orchard, the graft, the two of
us standing in it together — "are the same thing at all. I think this
specific version of it required you, specifically. Your particular
stubbornness. Your particular way of running cold on purpose and
warming, slowly, anyway, once you'd decided someone was worth the
warming."

"That's a very generous answer," Isla says.

"It's an honest one," I say, and watch her decide, this once, simply to
accept the honesty rather than examining it for a seam that isn't
actually there.

·

We walk down from the orchard as the light finally fails, Sela's lamp
already lit in the grain-house window behind us, and stop at the exact
spot on the road where I first found Isla on the road below Highstone,
weeks ago, both of us still strangers then, still running our own
private, cold arithmetic about exactly how much the other one might
cost.

"Do you remember this spot," I ask.

"I remember," Isla says. "You had grafting wax in your satchel and
entirely too much patience for a woman who'd already decided, before
you'd said a single word, that she was going to be difficult about the
whole binding."

"You weren't difficult. You were careful. I understood the difference
even then, though I don't think I'd have said so at the time."

"I was frightened," Isla says, plainly, in the way she says most
things now, without the careful management she once gave every
admission. "Of you, a little. Of what letting anyone matter that much
again might cost me, after six years of making very certain nobody
had the chance."

"And now?"

"Now I think the cost was worth considerably more than the fear
suggested it would be," she says, and takes my hand, standing on the
exact spot where this whole improbable arrangement first properly
began, and I understand, holding it, that whatever this winter has
taken from either of us, it has handed back something I would not
trade for any version of my life that didn't include this particular
woman, on this particular road, choosing, every single day since, to
keep choosing me back.

·

We stop by the orchard on the walk home, mostly out of habit now
rather than any specific errand, and find Sela in the grain-house,
finally, properly at rest for once, a cup of tea going cold beside her
untouched ledger.

"You're not working," I observe.

"I'm allowed not to be working," Sela says, without looking up, though
I catch the small smile she's not quite managing to hide. "I've decided,
this week, to practice the same thing you two keep insisting is so
important. Resting properly, before exhaustion makes the decision for
me."

"That's remarkably wise, for someone who spent this whole autumn
insisting she didn't need anyone's help managing the household's own
arithmetic."

"I contain multitudes," Sela says, echoing, I suspect without knowing
it, some turn of phrase she's picked up secondhand from one of us over
these past months, and goes back to her cold tea, entirely unbothered.

We sit with her a while, the three of us, in the grain-house's quiet
evening warmth, and I find myself, watching Isla and my sister trade
easy, comfortable observations about nothing in particular, considerably
more at peace than I have any memory of being in the whole of my adult
life — not because anything's been fixed, precisely, but because the
whole shape of what family and partnership and honest, shared burden
actually look like has finally, properly, settled into something I
recognize as home.

·

I ask Sela, before we leave that evening, what she'd meant, weeks ago,
by her small private smile about wisdom gained from experience rather
than mere theory.

She considers the question for a long moment, and I watch her decide,
finally, to answer it properly.

"There was someone," she says, "a few years back. Before all of this.
It didn't last, for reasons that don't matter anymore, but it taught me
something about what actually matters in choosing to build a life
alongside another person, rather than simply beside them. I've been
quietly grateful, watching the two of you this winter, to see that
lesson practiced properly, by people who seem rather better suited to
each other than my own attempt turned out to be."

"I'm sorry it didn't last," I say, gently.

"Don't be," Sela says. "I learned what I needed to learn from it. I'm
not in any hurry for another attempt, but I'm not opposed to one
either, whenever it happens to arrive. I've simply decided, watching
you two, that I'd rather wait for something that fits properly than
settle for something that merely fills the space."

"That's a wise policy," Isla says.

"I learned it from watching you refuse to settle for a managed version
of anything," Sela says, "which I suppose makes you responsible for it,
in your own roundabout way."

We leave the orchard that evening with considerably more warmth in our
chests than the cold night accounts for, grateful, all of us, for the
particular found family this winter has assembled, imperfectly,
determinedly, out of a rite none of us chose and a season none of us
would trade away now that it's properly, finally, behind us.

·

I stop at Ossian Thale's old grave the next morning, on my way back to
the orchard, a visit I have never once made in six years of living at
Highstone, mostly because Isla never invited me and I never presumed to
go without her.

She finds me there, unexpectedly, having apparently decided the same
morning to make the same visit, and neither of us says anything for a
moment, simply standing together at the plain, unmarked stone that
serves this line's wardwrights in place of anything grander.

"I've never brought anyone here before," she says, finally. "Not once,
in six years. I think I was afraid that showing anyone this particular
grief would mean admitting how much of it I was still carrying,
unresolved, underneath everything else I managed to hold together."

"I'm glad you're showing me now," I say.

"So am I," Isla says. "I think I finally understand something about
grief I didn't understand before this winter — that carrying it alone
doesn't actually make it lighter. It just makes it lonelier."

We stand together at the grave a long while, saying nothing further,
and I find myself, watching her lay a small sprig of frost-hardy herb
against the stone — the same herb we salvaged together in her own
garden, weeks ago — considerably moved by the specific quiet ceremony
of it, a private grief finally given a proper, shared witness.

"Thank you for showing me this," I say, as we finally turn to leave.

"Thank you for asking to see it," Isla says, "rather than waiting for
me to offer, the way I might have waited indefinitely if left to my
own old habits."

We walk back to the orchard together, hand in hand, the whole morning
considerably lighter for the small, deliberate act of finally sharing
a grief that has, for six long years, been hers to carry entirely
alone.

·

Sela is waiting for us at the orchard gate, an expression on her face
that suggests she's been turning something over since early morning.

"I want to try something," she announces, without preamble. "I've
decided, watching the two of you finally learn to ask each other for
things properly, that I'd like to practice the same skill. Small
scale. Nothing dramatic."

"Go on," Isla says, clearly intrigued.

"I'd like help with the spring planting," Sela says, "properly, rather
than managing it entirely myself the way I have every year since
Father died. I don't need it, strictly. I'd simply like it, and I've
decided wanting help isn't the same as needing rescue, however long
it's taken me to separate the two ideas."

"We'd be glad to help," I say, and mean it, watching my sister ask for
something plainly, without the usual armor of self-sufficiency she's
worn for as long as I can remember.

"Good," Sela says, satisfied, already moving on to practical matters.
"I'll want the northern beds turned first, and someone competent with
the seed-drill, since neither of you has shown any particular aptitude
for that specific tool yet."

"High praise," Isla says, dryly.

"I don't give praise I don't mean," Sela says, echoing her own earlier
words, and the three of us set to discussing the spring's practical
business together, a small, ordinary planning session that feels,
underneath its plainness, like one more piece of evidence that this
family has finally learned to build things in company rather than
alone.

·

Isla lingers at the grain-house door once Sela's gone in to fetch the
seed inventory, and I find her watching the orchard's northern rows
with a particular thoughtful stillness.

"What is it," I ask.

"I was thinking about the crooked tree," she says. "About what it
might mean, come spring, if you tried grafting it properly this time,
rather than simply letting it stand as a monument to what the frost
took."

"I hadn't considered that," I admit, surprised by the suggestion. "I
think I'd assumed it was past grafting, permanently."

"Maybe it is," Isla says. "Maybe it isn't. I don't know enough about
orchards to say for certain. I only know that a great many things I
assumed were permanently past recovering, this winter, turned out not
to be, given proper attention and enough patience."

I consider the crooked tree properly, turning her suggestion over with
more seriousness than my first reaction offered.

"I'd like to try," I say, finally. "Even if it fails. I think I'd
rather attempt it and fail than simply assume, forever, that it
couldn't have worked."

"That seems to be the whole lesson of this entire winter," Isla says,
"stated plainly, about a tree instead of a rite."

"Perhaps it's the same lesson, regardless of what it's about," I say,
and we stand together a while longer at the grain-house door, watching
the orchard's bare winter rows, both of us already looking forward,
somehow, to whatever spring might still prove possible.

·

Tomas arrives that same afternoon with news of his own, considerably
excited for a young man who usually delivers his reports with careful
professional restraint.

"The waystones are holding better than projected," he announces. "All
seven, properly within tolerance, no signs of the strain we were
bracing for after the Long Reach cairn's own crisis."

"That's excellent news," I say, genuinely relieved.

"I think it's the mutual form," Tomas says. "Not directly — the
waystones don't draw on either of you specifically. But I think the
whole line's charge has stabilized, somehow, now that the northern
stretch isn't quietly bleeding one hearth dry. I don't have the proper
theory worked out yet for why that would be true. I intend to develop
one."

"I'd like to see that theory, once you have it," I say, genuinely
curious.

"I'll write it up properly," Tomas says, with the particular pride of
a young man who has found, this winter, a genuine intellectual passion
alongside his practical duties. "I think there's real scholarship in
understanding exactly how the mutual form's effects ripple outward,
beyond the specific cairns it's performed at."

"You might be the one who finally writes that scholarship down," Isla
says, joining us, "properly, for whoever comes after you."

"I'd like that," Tomas says, considering the prospect with visible
pleasure, and the three of us spend a portion of that afternoon
speculating together about a whole future of careful study that none
of us could have properly imagined attempting, six weeks ago, in the
middle of a crisis none of us yet knew how to survive.

·

Sela joins us partway through the afternoon's speculation, listening
with visible interest to Tomas's own excited theorizing about the
mutual form's wider effects.

"You've found your calling," she observes, once he's finished a
particularly animated point.

"Maybe," Tomas admits, somewhat shyly. "I hadn't expected to find real
scholarship interesting, before this winter. I assumed I'd simply
learn the practical mechanics and leave the theorizing to whoever
came before me."

"There's no reason you can't do both," Sela says. "Practice and
theory aren't opposites, any more than duty and desire are, apparently,
this family's whole running theme this winter."

"I like that framing," Tomas says, pleased, and the four of us
continue the afternoon's conversation together, considerably more
hopeful, all of us, about a future built on genuine curiosity rather
than merely careful survival.

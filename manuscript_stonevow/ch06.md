## Chapter Six — Thaw a Stranger's Byre (Aldric)

The boy who comes running to Hollow Farm's gate is barefoot despite the
bitter frost, which tells me, before he's said a single word, exactly how much
urgency his family has decided the situation warrants, and how little
time they believed they had to spare for proper boots.

"The byre's frozen through," he says, breathless, to whoever will
listen, and it happens that Isla and I are the two people closest to
listening, having stopped at Hollow Farm on the way back from the outer
waystones to trade grafting wax for a sack of the farm's good seed
potatoes. "Da says the stock won't survive the night if it doesn't
thaw."

It isn't a cairn. It isn't, by any reading of the rite I've been given
since the binding, Isla's charge to spend anything on at all — Hollow
Farm sits three miles off the line proper, well outside any ward she's
sworn to maintain, and the crown's ledgers would not, I suspect, count
a single hour of whatever she's about to do as chargeable work.

She goes anyway.

I watch her walk to the byre without a word of explanation to the
farmer, without so much as glancing back to see whether I intend to
follow, and lay both hands flat against the frozen timber wall the way
I have watched her lay hands against standing cairns a dozen times now,
except this time there is no rite requiring it, no binding compelling
it, nothing at all except a woman deciding, on her own authority, that
a stranger's stock matters enough to spend something on that she isn't
going to write down anywhere afterward.

The warmth that moves out of her and into that wall is not small. I
know this because I feel, standing close enough to catch the edge of
it, the specific cost of it arrive somewhere in my own chest — not the
sharp, located ache the bond usually hands me, something broader,
heavier, like watching someone empty a pocket they'd told you was
already spent.

She goes grey at the mouth by the time the wall's frost has properly
broken. I have seen her go pale before, at the Long Reach cairn, in the
aftermath of ordinary, sanctioned ward-work. I have never seen her go
this particular colour, the specific grey of a person who has drawn on
a reserve she wasn't planning to touch tonight.

"You're finished," I say, more firmly than I've said anything to her
yet, and put a hand under her arm before she can wave me off, which she
tries to do anyway, on reflex, the way she tries to wave off most
offers of help before she's properly considered whether she needs them.

The farmer thanks her three separate times before we're even off his
land, pressing a second sack of seed potatoes on us that neither of us
asked for, and Isla accepts the thanks the way she accepts most
compliments — with the specific discomfort of a woman being handed
something she doesn't believe she's earned, though I watched her earn
every last measure of it not ten minutes ago.

"You didn't log that," I say, once we're walking again, the farm gone
small behind us.

"It wasn't a ward," she says. "It doesn't go in the ledger."

"That's not the reason you didn't log it," I say. It isn't a question.
I have watched her long enough now, in a fortnight, to recognize the
specific shape of a woman managing a fact she doesn't want examined,
because I have spent my whole adult life doing the exact same thing,
from the inside, and I know precisely what it looks like from the
outside now that I've finally got the angle to see it properly.

She doesn't answer, which is itself an answer, and I do not push
further, because I understand — watching her walk beside me, steadier
now but still paler than the walk out warranted — that I have just seen
the exact shape of my own flaw, worn on someone else's face, doing the
exact thing I do: taking a cost silently, in the dark, and calling the
silence discipline rather than what it actually is, which is a
refusal to let anyone else help carry a weight that was never meant to
be carried alone.

"How long have you been doing that," I ask. "Spending yourself on
things the ledger doesn't require."

"I don't know what you mean," Isla says, which is the first time,
in a fortnight, that I have heard her say something I am entirely
certain is not true.

I let it go, for now, the way she lets my own evasions go when she
suspects, correctly, that pushing too hard too early will only teach me
to hide the next one better. But I watch her the rest of the walk back
with the specific, quiet attention of a man who has just recognized,
in someone else, a shape he knows intimately from the inside, and who
has started, somewhere in the last quarter mile, to wonder whether
either of us is going to survive this winter if neither of us ever
learns to stop paying alone.

·

Sela is waiting at the orchard gate when I finally make it home that
evening, later than I told her I'd be, with two sacks of seed potatoes
and considerably more on my mind than the errand accounted for.

"You look like you've seen something," she says, taking one of the
sacks without being asked, the way she takes most burdens without
being asked, a family trait I am only now, watching Isla do the same
thing at Hollow Farm, beginning to properly recognize as a pattern
rather than simply how things are done.

"I think I have," I say, and do not, for tonight, explain further,
because I am still working out, myself, exactly what it is I saw, and
what it might mean that the woman the rite bound me to seems to be
carrying precisely the same weight I've spent my whole life carrying,
alone, in the dark, and calling it something other than what it is.

·

Sela doesn't let the matter rest there, of course. She has never once,
in twenty-four years, let a matter rest simply because I've decided the
conversation is finished.

"You're going to have to tell me eventually," she says, the following
morning, over the grain-house's own modest fire, "what actually
happened at Hollow Farm. You came home looking like a man who'd
watched something rearrange his whole understanding of a person, and
you've said exactly four words about it since."

"Isla thawed a farmer's byre," I say. "At her own cost. Off the
record."

"That's the fact of it," Sela says. "I'm asking about the part that put
that look on your face."

I consider how much to tell her, weighing the specific loyalty I feel
toward Isla's own carefully guarded privacy against the equally
specific loyalty I owe my sister, who has spent her whole life reading
me more accurately than I generally want to be read.

"I think she's been doing that for longer than anyone knows," I say,
finally. "I think she's been quietly spending herself on this line for
years, the same way I spent myself on your exemption, and calling it
duty instead of what it actually is."

Sela is quiet for a long moment, turning that over with the particular
care she gives most things that touch too close to our own family's
history.

"That's not a small thing to recognize in someone else," she says,
eventually. "Especially not this fast."

"No," I agree.

"Does it frighten you? Recognizing it?"

"Yes," I admit. "Because I don't know how to talk someone out of a
habit I've never managed to talk myself out of. I don't know how to be
the person who finally interrupts that pattern in her, when I've never
once let anyone interrupt it in me."

Sela reaches over and takes my hand — the good one, still — and holds
it a moment, the way she used to when we were children and the winters
felt considerably larger than either of us.

"Maybe you don't have to talk her out of it," she says. "Maybe you
just have to keep noticing, out loud, the way you're doing now, until
noticing becomes its own kind of interruption. That's rather all I've
ever managed to do with you, and it hasn't worked yet, but I like to
think it's slowly, eventually, working."

I don't have a ready answer to that, so I simply sit with her by the
fire a while longer, turning the whole of Hollow Farm over again in my
mind, and find, walking home later that evening, that Sela's words have
settled somewhere useful, a small stone added to whatever foundation
I'm apparently building, one recognition at a time, toward whatever
this arrangement with Isla is eventually going to become.

·

The Hollow Farm family sends word, a few days later, that the byre has
held through the whole of the following week without further incident,
and includes, with the message, a small parcel of preserved fruit that
neither Isla nor I properly expected, given the specific unofficial
nature of what she did for them.

"I don't accept payment for unlogged work," Isla says, examining the
parcel with the particular discomfort of someone unaccustomed to being
thanked so plainly.

"It's not payment," I say. "It's a family telling you they noticed
what you did, and that they're grateful for it, in the only language
most farming families have available for gratitude this large. I'd
accept it, if I were you. Refusing it would cost them something they'd
rather not have to carry — the specific indignity of a kindness
declined."

Isla considers this, turning the parcel over in her hands.

"When did you get so wise about accepting things gracefully," she
asks, "for a man who mended my glove and refused to let me thank him
for it properly?"

"I'm still working on the accepting-things-myself part," I admit. "I
find it considerably easier to counsel someone else toward it than to
practice it myself."

"That seems to be a pattern with both of us," Isla says, and there's
something in the observation — dry, exact, entirely accurate — that
makes me laugh, properly, surprised out of me the way her own laugh
sometimes surprises her.

We share the preserved fruit that evening, an ordinary domestic
pleasure neither of us has properly indulged in since the binding, and
I find myself, watching her enjoy something simple without the usual
weight of arithmetic behind it, increasingly certain that whatever we
are slowly building together is worth every uncertain step it's taking
to construct.

·

Sela's own reaction to the Hollow Farm parcel, once she hears the full
story, is considerably more pointed than either Isla's or my own.

"You thawed a stranger's byre at your own cost and didn't tell anyone,"
she says to Isla, the following week, with the specific bluntness she
reserves for observations she considers too important to soften. "That's
precisely the kind of thing Aldric would do, and I've spent our whole
lives trying, mostly without success, to talk him out of doing it."

"It needed doing," Isla says, simply.

"Plenty of things need doing," Sela says. "Not all of them need doing
by the one person who's already spending herself thin on forty other
things nobody else can manage. I understand the impulse. I watched my
own father die of exactly this impulse, dressed up as duty. I'd rather
not watch it claim anyone else I've come to care about."

Isla doesn't have a ready response to being told, so plainly, that
Sela has already begun to care about her specifically, and I watch her
absorb the observation with the same careful stillness she gives most
things that land harder than their brevity suggests.

"I'm trying to do better," Isla says, finally. "I don't know that I'm
succeeding yet. But I'm trying."

"That's all any of us can ask," Sela says, gentler now, and the
particular warmth that passes between the two of them in that moment
settles something in my own chest that I hadn't realized needed
settling — the specific relief of watching my sister extend to Isla
exactly the same fierce, unmanaged care she's always extended to me.

·

Isla and I walk back to the cottage together once Sela's own visit has
properly concluded, and I find myself, partway down the track, turning
over the whole shape of the afternoon's conversation.

"She likes you," I say. "Properly. I don't think she extends that
particular warmth to many people this quickly."

"I noticed," Isla says. "I'll admit it surprised me. I didn't expect
your sister to warm to a stranger the rite handed her brother quite
this fast."

"Neither did I," I admit. "I think she recognized something in you.
The same habit she's watched me practice my whole life, worn on
someone else's face. I think it made you easier to trust, oddly,
rather than harder."

"That's a strange kind of kinship," Isla says, "bonding over a shared
flaw rather than a shared virtue."

"Most of the bonds in this family seem to work that way," I say. "I
don't think we're particularly good at bonding over our virtues. We're
considerably more practiced at recognizing each other's damage."

Isla considers this, turning it over with the particular seriousness
she gives most things I say that land harder than their brevity
suggests.

"I don't think that's necessarily worse," she says, finally. "I think
recognizing someone's damage, and choosing to stay anyway, might be a
sturdier foundation than simply admiring someone's virtues from a safe
distance."

"I hadn't thought of it that way," I say, "but I think you're right."

We walk the rest of the way home in comfortable quiet, and I find
myself, watching the light fail properly over the orchard's own bare
winter rows, grateful for a woman who keeps finding the true, steadying
shape underneath whatever difficult thing I hand her.

·

Tomas mentions Sela's visit himself the following morning, having
apparently heard some account of it already from whichever channel
keeps him reliably informed of the household's own business.

"Sela came by, I hear," he says, setting down the day's reports.

"She did," Isla says. "We had a rather frank conversation about
matching flaws."

"That sounds like her," Tomas says, with genuine affection. "She's been
kind to me too, these past weeks, whenever I've delivered reports to
the orchard. I don't think she has to be, given I'm simply the
apprentice attached to her brother's own arrangement. She's kind
anyway."

"I've noticed that about her," Isla says. "She extends her care rather
more widely than her own brisk manner would initially suggest."

"I like her considerably," Tomas admits, somewhat shyly. "I hope that's
not an odd thing to say."

"It's not odd at all," I say. "I like her considerably too, and I've
had twenty-eight years of practice."

Tomas laughs at that, properly, and the three of us settle into the
morning's ordinary business, the whole household's own accumulated
warmth for Sela Marrow settling into something none of us feel any
particular need to examine too closely, simply grateful for its
presence.

·

I find myself, later that same day, thinking about the specific way
Isla and Sela have already started trading small kindnesses without
either of them making a particular occasion of it — a borrowed tool
here, a shared observation there, the particular ease of two women who
have decided, somewhere in a single afternoon, that they're worth
each other's trust.

"You're smiling at nothing," Isla observes, catching me at it.

"I'm thinking about how quickly you and Sela found your footing with
each other," I admit. "I spent considerable time worrying, before the
binding, about how she'd receive whoever the rite named. I needn't have
worried."

"I think we simply recognized something useful in each other early,"
Isla says. "That tends to speed things along, regardless of how
carefully either of us might otherwise have proceeded."

"I'm grateful for it," I say, "however it happened," and Isla takes my
hand, and we sit together a while longer, both grateful for a family
that keeps finding its footing faster than either of us properly
expects.

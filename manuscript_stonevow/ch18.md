## Chapter Eighteen — Beyond the Line (Isla)

The line has crept four miles since first frost, by my own count, and
does not care in the slightest that the count exists, or that I have
kept it, meticulously, since the morning after my mentor's funeral,
as though the keeping of it might, eventually, amount to some kind of
leverage against a thing that has never once, in living memory,
negotiated with anyone.

We go out past the last standing cairn on a rumour — a village, once
properly inside the ward-line, now sitting past it, unaccounted for in
any ledger I have ever kept or inherited. It is the kind of rumour I
would ordinarily send Tomas to verify, except Tomas is nineteen, and
whatever waits past the line deserves, I have decided, a pair of eyes
that have already survived worse.

Aldric comes because he will not, at this point, let me go anywhere
near the line's edge alone, a fact we have not formally negotiated and
both, apparently, simply accept.

The cold past the line does something specific to sound. It flattens
it, the way a held breath flattens a room, swallowing footsteps,
swallowing the ordinary creak of frozen branches, until the only sound
either of us can properly hear is our own breathing, loud in the
particular silence of a place that has stopped, entirely, behaving like
weather and started behaving like something considerably more deliberate.

Neither of us talks much on the walk in. There is not, I find, a great
deal to say, walking into ground the Rime has already claimed for good,
that would improve on simply walking it properly, attentive to
whatever the ground itself has to tell us.

Aldric's hand finds mine partway through the walk, without either of us
deciding it should, and this time neither of us lets go for reasons
that have nothing at all to do with balance on uneven ground. I am
aware, holding it, of the specific comfort of his hand against mine —
warmer than the air around us has any right to allow, a fact I have
stopped questioning and started, instead, simply relying on, the way
you rely on a fire once you've stopped doubting it'll hold through the
night.

"Tell me what you're expecting to find," I say, mostly to give the
silence somewhere to go besides the specific dread that's been building
in my own chest since we crossed the last standing cairn.

"I don't know," Aldric admits. "I've heard stories about villages past
the line, growing up. None of them agreed on the details. Some say the
people simply left, ahead of the cold, and the buildings are just —
empty. Others say something considerably worse. I've never had reason
to test either version until now."

"What does your own arithmetic say."

"That the truth's probably somewhere between the two, and considerably
less tidy than either version manages to be." He glances at me,
sideways, in the particular way he has of testing whether a sentence
has landed the way he intended. "You're frightened."

"I am," I admit, because I have decided, weeks ago now, that half-truths
between us cost more than whole ones. "Not of what we'll find,
precisely. Of what finding it might mean for the arithmetic I've been
running my whole career — that the line holds, that the cost is
survivable, that six years of careful management amounts to something
more than simply delaying an outcome that was never actually in
question."

"We'll find out together," Aldric says, which is the same thing he
said to me at the Long Reach cairn, before the first ward we set
together, and I understand, hearing it a second time, that it has
become, between us, something close to a promise — not a guarantee of
outcome, simply an assurance that whatever arrives, arrives to both of
us rather than to either one alone.

The village comes into view exactly where the rumour said it would be,
sitting in a shallow fold of ground that would once, I suspect, have
been sheltered rather than exposed, before the line moved far enough
north to leave it standing on the wrong side of everything.

It is entirely, perfectly still.

Not the ordinary stillness of an empty place in winter — the specific,
particular stillness of a place the cold has finished with entirely,
holding its silence the way a held breath holds itself, patient,
complete, waiting for nothing at all, because there is nothing left
that it needs to wait for.

I stand at the edge of it for a long moment, Aldric's hand still in
mine, and feel, for the first time since the binding, a fear that has
nothing at all to do with either of us specifically, and everything to
do with the plain, unmanageable fact of what forty miles of failing
cairns are actually holding back.

"We should go in," I say, finally, because the alternative — turning
back without answers — is no longer, given everything currently at
stake, a choice either of us can properly afford.

"Together," Aldric says, and we go in together, into the first true
silence either of us has ever properly stood inside, toward whatever
answer is waiting in a village the Rime has already, patiently,
completely, finished taking.

·

Before we reach the first house, I stop at what must once have been the
village's own boundary marker — a low, weathered stone, considerably
older than any waystone on my own line, carved with a symbol I don't
immediately recognize, three interlocking rings rather than the single
standing figure that marks every cairn I've ever inspected.

"Do you know this mark," I ask Aldric, more out of habit than genuine
expectation that he would.

He studies it for a long moment, and I watch something shift in his
face — recognition, faint, uncertain.

"My grandmother had something like it," he says, slowly, "carved into
the lintel above her own door. I never asked what it meant. I assumed
it was simply decoration. She died before I was old enough to think to
ask properly."

"Three rings," I say, tracing the shape with one finger, careful not to
disturb whatever frost has settled into the carving's grooves. "I've
never seen this on any cairn on my own line. I wonder if it's older
than the current form entirely — a mark from before the rite was
whatever it currently is."

"Do you think it means something specific?"

"I think," I say, "that everything about this village is going to mean
something specific, and that we're going to need to be considerably
more careful than usual about which conclusions we draw from which
piece of evidence."

We continue toward the village proper, and I find myself glancing back
at the boundary marker more than once, the three interlocking rings
settling into my memory alongside everything else I am gathering here,
a piece of a puzzle whose full shape I do not yet understand, though I
suspect, walking deeper into Calder's Reach's particular, complete
silence, that I am about to.

·

The village proper holds considerably more than the first house's quiet
domesticity prepared me for. Where the boundary marker suggested a
place lost gently, the deeper streets tell a more complicated story —
a scattering of tools left mid-task, a loom still threaded halfway
through a pattern nobody ever finished, a schoolroom, of all things,
with slates still propped against a wall, half-formed letters chalked
across several of them, as though the lesson had simply paused rather
than properly ended.

"They didn't know," I say, quietly, standing in the schoolroom's
particular hush. "Whatever took this place, it didn't arrive as a
warning anyone could act on. It arrived as an ordinary day that simply
never resumed."

Aldric picks up one of the slates, reading the half-formed letters with
the specific care of a man handling something considerably more fragile
than chalk and stone.

"A child was learning to write her own name," he says, quietly. "Look.
She'd gotten as far as the third letter."

I do not have a ready response to that particular detail, and we stand
together in the schoolroom a long moment, both of us absorbing the
specific, unbearable ordinariness of what the Rime actually costs — not
simply cairns and charge readings, actual children, mid-lesson, whose
names never got finished being written.

"This is what we're actually holding the line against," I say,
eventually. "Not an abstract cold. This. A whole village of ordinary
mornings that simply stopped, all at once, because nobody held the
ward that would have kept the cold at a proper distance."

"I understand the stakes considerably better, standing here," Aldric
says, "than I did reading about them in any ledger."

We leave the schoolroom as we found it, the half-finished slate
returned carefully to its place, and continue toward the record-room
proper, both of us carrying, now, a considerably more visceral
understanding of exactly what failure on this line actually costs.

·

The record-room itself, when we finally reach it, holds considerably
more than the sealed box in the third house's recess. Shelves of
ledgers, considerably older than anything Highstone's own archive
maintains, line every wall, undisturbed by the cold in a way that
suggests the room was, at some point, deliberately warded against
exactly this kind of decay — a small mercy, whoever last tended it,
that has preserved considerably more than either of us expected to
find intact.

"This alone is worth the whole journey," I say, running a hand along
the spines of ledgers considerably older than Ossian's own earliest
entries. "There's history here that Highstone's own archive doesn't
have at all."

Aldric pulls one down carefully, opening it to a page dense with the
same cramped, economical hand as the sealed record — a full accounting
of the village's own cairns, its own losses, stretching back further
than I can properly estimate without more careful study.

"Do you think we can bring all of this back," he asks, "or only what's
most urgent?"

"I don't know," I admit. "I think, realistically, we take what's most
urgent now, and I come back, properly, once this crisis has settled,
to catalogue the rest of it. This room deserves more careful attention
than I currently have time to give it."

We spend a considerable while longer in the record-room, gathering
what we can carry, and I find myself, closing the last ledger, already
planning the eventual return trip that will properly honor whatever
predecessor built this careful, considerable archive, only to watch it
sit sealed and forgotten for however many years the cold has held this
village silent.

·

Before we leave the village, Aldric asks if we might stop once more at
the schoolroom, and I understand, seeing his face, that the
half-finished slate has stayed with him considerably more than he's
let on since we first found it.

"I want to remember her properly," he says, standing again in the
schoolroom's particular hush. "Whoever she was. I don't want her to
simply be a detail I mention, later, when someone asks what we found
here. I want to actually carry her, the way you carry Ossian, the way
I'm learning to carry the names Cera's given us."

"Do you want to give her a name," I ask, "since we don't have her
actual one?"

Aldric considers the question seriously, looking at the slate's
half-formed letters.

"Wren," he says, finally. "For no particular reason except that it's
the first name that came to me, standing here. I'd rather she have
some name, even an invented one, than remain simply 'the child from
Calder's Reach' in whatever account either of us eventually gives of
today."

"Wren," I repeat, quietly, and write it down in my own notebook,
alongside the growing list of names this whole strange season keeps
handing me to properly remember. "I think that's exactly right. Every
loss deserves a name, even one we have to give it ourselves, rather
than simply a location and a date."

We leave the schoolroom a final time, considerably more moved than
either of us expected an invented name to leave us, and I understand,
walking back toward the village's edge, that this small, deliberate
act of naming — a child neither of us ever knew, given a name neither
of us can verify — has become, somehow, one of the more important
things either of us has done since arriving here.

"Thank you," I say, as we reach the boundary marker again, "for
wanting to remember her properly, rather than letting the whole visit
become simply a mission accomplished."

"I learned that from you," Aldric says. "Watching you refuse to let
Ossian's name go unwritten. I don't think I'd have thought to name her,
a season ago. I think I'd have simply catalogued the slate as evidence
and moved on to the next urgent thing."

"You've changed considerably, this winter," I observe.

"So have you," he says, and we walk the last stretch out of Calder's
Reach together, carrying, alongside the record boxes and the ledgers,
a small, invented name that neither of us intends to ever properly
forget.

·

We stop, a final time, at the shepherd's hut where we sheltered the
first night, gathering the last of our things before the true walk
home begins, and I find Aldric lingering at the doorway a moment
longer than the errand requires.

"What is it," I ask.

"I keep thinking about the fact that this hut's stood here, empty,
for however many years, waiting for exactly two people who needed it
enough to properly use it again," he says. "It feels like it should
mean something, that detail. I'm not certain what."

"Maybe it simply means that some things wait, patiently, for whoever
eventually needs them," I say, "regardless of how long the waiting
takes."

"I like that reading," Aldric says. "I think I'd like to believe most
of what's been lost, out here past the line, is waiting in some similar
fashion — not gone entirely, simply waiting for someone with the time
and the reason to come looking properly."

"I'd like to believe that too," I say, and we take a final look at the
small stone hut before setting out, carrying, alongside everything
else Calder's Reach has handed us, a small, private hope that loss and
waiting are not, in fact, the same thing as gone for good.

·

We walk in comfortable silence for a while, once the village's shape
has properly faded behind us, and I find myself turning over the whole
strange weight of what we're carrying home — proof of a rite that might
save Aldric's life, a village's worth of unrecorded grief, a small,
invented name neither of us intends to forget.

"Do you think we've changed, doing this," I ask, "or simply discovered
something that was already true about us?"

Aldric considers the question properly, the way he considers most
things I ask him seriously.

"Both, maybe," he says. "I think Calder's Reach showed us something
true about ourselves that we hadn't properly tested yet. But I don't
think either of us walks away from a place like that unchanged, either.
I think the testing itself changes a person, regardless of what it
confirms."

"That's a fair distinction," I say, and we continue on, the whole
weight of the journey settling into something we can properly carry
together, rather than either one of us bearing it alone.

·

We stop once more before the last standing cairn comes properly into
view, both of us needing, I think, a final moment before Calder's
Reach's particular grief gives way to whatever ordinary business
awaits us at Highstone.

"Thank you," Aldric says, "for bringing me. I don't think I'd have
managed any of what we found today, alone, without your particular way
of insisting a thing be examined properly rather than merely survived."

"Thank you for coming," I say. "I don't think I'd have had the nerve to
sit with any of it as fully, without you there to sit with it alongside
me."

We take each other's hand, and continue the last stretch home together,
carrying Calder's Reach's whole complicated gift — grief, proof, and a
small stubborn hope — properly, finally, between the two of us.

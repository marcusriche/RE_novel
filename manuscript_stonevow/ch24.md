## Chapter Twenty-Four — Ask Me Properly (Aldric)

Before anything further happens between us, Isla makes us both say it
in words, out loud, unbound — not the rite speaking through either of
us, not the bond's own compulsion doing the asking on our behalf, an
actual person asking another actual person, plainly, the way she has
insisted, since the cairn field, that everything between us has to be
asked.

"I need to know this is you asking," I say, before she can begin,
because I have been turning this exact concern over myself for days
now, watching what the bond can make either of us feel without either
of us meaning to feel it. "Not the bond wanting company against the
cold. I've felt what it does, unbidden — the warmth, the ache, all of
it arriving without permission from either of us. I need to know the
difference matters to you here, specifically, as much as it's mattered
everywhere else."

"It's me asking," Isla says, and I hear, in the plainness of it,
exactly the difference she means — not a performance offered to
reassure me, a simple, unguarded fact, stated once, the way she states
most of the things she actually means. "I've spent this whole
arrangement learning precisely what the bond's pull feels like,
separate from an actual choice. I know the difference now. This is the
second one."

I ask her the same question back, in the same plain terms, because it
seems only fair the asking run in both directions, the way everything
else important between us has, since the night at the cairn field.

"Is this you," I ask, "asking. Separate from whatever the bond's made
easier."

"Yes," Isla says, without a moment's hesitation, and I believe her
entirely, in the specific way I have learned, these past weeks, to
believe the things she says plainly rather than the things she manages.

Nothing happens between us yet, in this particular conversation. The
asking is the whole of it — deliberate, complete, both of us
understanding, without either having to say so directly, that this
foundation is the only one either of us is willing to build anything
further on, given everything the rite has already taken from both of
us without asking permission first.

"There's a thing I want to say," I add, once the asking itself is
settled, "that isn't strictly part of the question, but that I think
matters regardless."

"Say it."

"I've spent my whole life giving before I'm asked. Sela's exemption. The
binding itself. It's easier, that way, to control exactly what a thing
costs me, rather than risk someone asking for more than I'd have
offered on my own. I want you to know that answering your question
honestly — actually waiting to be asked, rather than simply offering
myself the way I usually do — has been considerably harder than I
expected it to be."

"Why," Isla asks.

"Because if I wait to be asked, I have to actually risk the answer
being no," I say. "I've spent my whole life avoiding that particular
risk by simply never putting myself in a position to hear it. Tonight,
I have. And I find I'd rather hear an honest no from you than a
managed yes from myself."

Isla looks at me for a long moment, in the last of the evening light,
and I understand, from the specific quality of her stillness, that
something in what I've just said has landed considerably harder than I
intended it to.

"That's the most honest thing you've ever told me," she says, finally.
"Thank you for risking it."

"Thank you for asking properly," I say, "so I had the chance to."

Nothing further happens between us tonight beyond the asking itself.
It is, both of us understand, entirely enough — the only foundation
either of us is willing to build anything further on, and considerably
more than either of us, six weeks ago, standing wary and unbound at a
rite neither of us chose, would have believed either one capable of
offering the other so plainly.

·

I think, walking back from the cottage to the orchard the next
morning, about how differently I understood consent before this
binding — as a formality, mostly, a thing observed at the edges of
larger decisions rather than the actual foundation any of it should
properly rest on. I gave Sela's exemption without asking her whether she
wanted it given that way. I volunteered for the rite without asking
myself, honestly, whether I was choosing it for the right reasons or
simply the reasons that let me avoid being asked for anything smaller,
later, by someone who might actually say no.

Isla has taught me, in the space of a few short weeks, that asking
properly — plainly, in words, with room left for an honest refusal — is
not a formality at all. It is the entire structure a real choice stands
on, and without it, even the kindest-seeming gesture is simply another
version of deciding for someone else what they're allowed to want.

I think about my father, too, on that walk, and the specific way he
managed our whole childhood without ever once asking Sela or me
directly what we needed, assuming, always, that his own careful
provision was sufficient, that the asking itself would only burden two
children already dealing with enough. I understand, now, watching Isla
insist on the plain question every single time, that he was wrong about
that, kindly, lovingly wrong, but wrong all the same — that even a
child, even someone frightened or grieving or uncertain, deserves to be
asked rather than simply provided for.

I resolve, walking the last stretch toward the orchard gate, to ask
Sela properly, that same day, whether there's anything she actually
needs from me that I have simply been assuming, all these years,
wasn't worth the vulnerability of the asking. It feels, deciding this,
like a small thing. I suspect, by the specific weight it carries in my
own chest, that it is considerably larger than it currently feels.

·

I find Isla in the cottage's small garden, later that same evening,
attempting, with more determination than success, to salvage a patch
of winter herbs that the season's cold has been steadily overtaking.

"You don't strike me as a gardener," I observe, watching her fight with
a particularly stubborn root.

"I'm not," she admits. "Ossian kept this patch for the whole of my
apprenticeship. I've let it go to ruin these past few years, mostly out
of neglect rather than any decision. I thought, today, that I might
try to save what's left of it, before the ground freezes too hard to
manage anything at all."

I kneel down beside her and take over the stubborn root, working it
free with the particular patience grafting has taught me for anything
that resists being moved.

"Why today, specifically?" I ask.

"Because Sela's grain-house made me think about what gets tended and
what gets neglected, in a life," Isla says. "I've tended this whole
line for six years, meticulously. I let this small patch of herbs go
to ruin without a second thought, because it never once occurred to me
that a person's own garden might deserve the same care as forty miles
of cairns."

"That's a very particular kind of imbalance."

"It's the same imbalance we've both been examining, in different
shapes, all winter," Isla says. "Tending everyone else's needs
meticulously while letting our own simply wither, unattended, because
attending to them felt like an indulgence neither of us had properly
earned."

We work the small garden together for the rest of the evening,
salvaging what we can of the herbs, and I find, doing it, a particular
satisfaction in tending something this small and unimportant, by any
official measure, simply because it matters to the person kneeling
beside me in the cold.

·

Tomas discovers the salvaged garden the next morning, on his way to
deliver the day's reports, and stops, genuinely surprised, to examine
the neat, if modest, rows we managed to save.

"You gardened," he says, to Isla, in a tone that suggests this ranks
among the more unexpected developments of the whole winter.

"We gardened," Isla corrects, though I notice she doesn't seem
particularly bothered by his surprise.

"I didn't know either of you had it in you," Tomas says, examining the
herbs with genuine curiosity. "I assumed, given how thoroughly this
patch had gone to ruin, that gardening simply wasn't a skill either of
you possessed."

"It wasn't," I admit. "I'm considerably better with an orchard than
with delicate herbs. Isla claims no skill at all. We managed anyway,
mostly through stubbornness rather than any particular expertise."

"I'd like to help, if you'll let me," Tomas says, surprising both of
us. "My mother kept bees, but she had a proper garden too, before —"
He stops, the old grief briefly crossing his face before he manages it
back into composure. "I remember some of what she taught me. I think
I'd like to put it to use again, if it's welcome."

"It's entirely welcome," Isla says, gently, understanding, I think,
exactly what this small offer actually costs him to make.

We spend a portion of that same afternoon with Tomas properly teaching
us both what little he remembers of his mother's own gardening
wisdom, and I find, watching the three of us kneel together over a
patch of salvaged herbs, that this small, unofficial garden has
become, in the space of two days, considerably more than the sum of
its modest rows.

·

Sela visits the garden herself, a few days later, examining the
salvaged rows with the particular critical eye of a woman who has
spent her whole life properly tending things.

"This is decent work," she says, "for three people who apparently
didn't know a root from a weed a week ago."

"High praise, from you," Isla says.

"I don't give praise I don't mean," Sela says, though the corner of
her mouth suggests she's rather more pleased than her brisk tone
lets on. "I've been meaning to ask — would you mind if I brought some
cuttings over from the orchard's own herb beds? There's a frost-hardy
variety I've had good luck with that might do well alongside what
you've already got established here."

"I'd welcome that," Isla says. "I don't have much practical knowledge
of my own to contribute. I'd rather borrow someone else's than let the
whole patch struggle along on my own limited expertise."

"I'll bring them by tomorrow," Sela says, and lingers a moment longer,
examining the small garden with an expression I can't quite place.

"What is it," I ask.

"Nothing," Sela says, then reconsiders. "It's simply — this is the
first time in longer than I can properly remember that I've watched
you tend something for its own sake, rather than because the line or
the exemption or some other obligation required it of you. I find I
like watching you do it."

"I like doing it," I admit, surprised, saying it, by how true the
words actually are. "I don't think I'd ever properly given myself
permission to tend something just because I wanted to, rather than
because someone needed me to."

"Then keep doing it," Sela says, simply, and turns to head back toward
the orchard, leaving the three of us kneeling together in the small,
salvaged garden, considerably more settled than any of us were a
handful of days ago.

·

Tomas returns that evening with a small clay pot of his own, offered
somewhat shyly to Isla once the day's work is properly finished.

"For the garden," he says. "I found it in the storehouse. I thought
it might suit the herbs better than the cracked one you've been using."

Isla accepts it with a warmth I've come to recognize as entirely
genuine rather than merely polite. "Thank you, Tomas. That's
thoughtful."

"It's a small thing," he says, ducking his head slightly.

"It's not a small thing," Isla says. "It's exactly the kind of small
thing that adds up, over a whole winter, into something considerably
larger than any single gesture accounts for on its own. I've learned
that much, at least, this season."

Tomas seems pleased by this, in the quiet, unshowy way he's pleased by
most things that matter to him, and the three of us finish settling
the garden for the evening together, a small, unofficial family
properly tending something that belongs to all of them equally.

·

I find Isla alone at the garden's edge, later that evening, once
Tomas has gone and the light's nearly failed entirely.

"You've been out here a while," I observe.

"I was thinking about what Sela said," she admits. "About tending
something for its own sake. I don't think I've properly done that,
outside of this small patch, in the whole of my adult life."

"Not even the cairns?"

"The cairns are duty," Isla says. "Necessary, meaningful, but duty all the
same. This—" she gestures at the salvaged rows, barely visible now in the
fading light "—this is simply because I wanted to see something grow
that nobody required of me."

I consider this, kneeling down beside her at the garden's edge.

"I think that's rather important," I say, "learning the difference.
I spent most of my own life treating the orchard the same way you
treated the cairns — necessary, dutiful, a thing I tended because it
required tending, not because I'd properly let myself enjoy the
tending itself."

"Has that changed?"

"I think it's changing," I say. "Slowly. I caught myself,
grafting last week, actually enjoying the specific quiet of the work,
rather than simply pushing through it toward whatever came next."

"I'm glad," Isla says, and we kneel together at the garden's edge a while
longer, in the deepening evening dark, both of us slowly learning to
let ordinary things matter simply because they're ours, rather than
because anyone requires them of either of us.

·

We go inside eventually, once the cold properly settles, and Isla
settles beside me by the fire, turning over the whole shape of the
day's small revelations.

"I want to ask you something," she says, "that I've been circling since
the garden."

"Go on."

"Do you think it's possible to want something purely for its own sake,
the way we've just described wanting the garden — without it eventually
becoming duty too, the moment enough time passes and enough people
start depending on it?"

I consider the question properly, the particular seriousness the
subject deserves.

"I don't know," I admit. "I think duty and desire aren't always as
separate as we'd like them to be. I think the orchard started as
something my father loved purely, and became duty, over years, simply
by being needed. I don't think that transformation is necessarily a
tragedy, though. I think it might simply be what happens to anything
you keep choosing long enough."

"That's a more hopeful reading than I expected."

"I've had a good teacher, this winter, in finding hope in unexpected
places," I say, and Isla leans into me by the fire, and I hold her
there a while longer, grateful for a woman who keeps finding the
courage to ask the harder questions rather than let them sit unspoken.

·

Tomas asks, the following morning, whether he might have his own small
corner of the garden to tend, separate from the shared rows.

"I'd like something that's entirely mine to fail at," he says, "rather
than always tending things properly alongside the two of you."

"That's a fair request," I say. "What would you grow?"

"Something useless," Tomas says, with a small grin. "Purely decorative.
I've spent my whole apprenticeship learning things with practical
purposes. I find I'd like one small thing that serves no purpose
beyond simply being pleasant to look at."

"I think that's exactly the right instinct," I say, and we mark out a
small corner of the garden for his own use that same afternoon, and I
find myself, watching him plan his purely decorative plot with genuine
enthusiasm, grateful for a young man learning, this early, the
considerable value of tending something simply because it pleases him.

## Chapter Thirty-Eight — Tomas's Choice (Isla)

Tomas asks the question carefully, the following week, in a voice
trying visibly hard not to sound like it's hoping for a particular
answer.

"Am I still needed," he says, "now that the line holds on new terms?
I've spent three years training to be a spare hearth, in case the
worst happened. I'm not certain what I'm training to be, now that the
worst didn't."

"You were never needed as a spare hearth," I say. "You're needed as the
next wardwright, on whatever timeline actually suits you, which was
always the job I meant to offer you, however badly Feld's ledgers
managed to obscure that underneath their own contingency planning."

He doesn't quite believe it right away, the specific hesitation of a
young man raised, these past months, on a countdown, who doesn't fully
trust a clock that's finally stopped its urgent ticking. I let him take
the time to believe it properly, rather than insisting, because I have
learned, this whole strange season, that some truths need room to
settle before they can be trusted.

"I'd like to learn the mutual form," Tomas says, eventually. "Properly.
From the start, this time, not stitched together out of a dead woman's
journal at the last possible hour, the way you and Aldric had to learn
it."

"That's the job," I agree, and mean, underneath it, that the whole
northern line is finally being handed forward properly, to someone
with the time to learn it right, rather than merely surviving from one
crisis to the next the way it has for considerably longer than my own
six years of practice.

"Will you teach me the whole history of it," Tomas asks. "Not just the
mechanics. The reason it was corrupted in the first place. I think I'd
understand the form better if I understood why anyone thought hiding
it was necessary."

"I'll teach you," I say. "All of it. I think you're right that the
history matters as much as the mechanics, this time."

·

I write, that same week, a proper letter to whatever surviving family
Fenhollow's own lost wardwright might have left behind, on the small
chance that some record of the second village's fate has survived
somewhere I haven't yet thought to look. It feels, this time, less like
the avoidance Aldric once gently named in me, and more like simply
continuing the work properly — one lost village at a time, one
predecessor's memory at a time, each of them deserving the same careful
attention I finally gave Ossian's own.

Tomas offers to help with the correspondence, and I let him, watching
him draft his own careful, formal inquiry with a precision that tells
me, plainly, exactly how far his own training has come since the
autumn's first uncertain months.

"Do you think we'll ever properly recover this line," he asks, sealing
the letter, "all of it. Every lost cairn, every lost village. Or is
some of it simply gone, the way Fenhollow's gone, past any recovering."

"I don't know," I say, honestly. "I think some of it's gone for good. I
think the honest work, going forward, is holding what's left properly,
rather than pretending we can undo what the Rime's already taken. But I
also think — and I didn't believe this, six months ago — that holding
what's left properly, together, in company, is worth doing regardless
of how much has already been lost."

Tomas considers that, nodding slowly, and I understand, watching him,
that he is filing the observation away the same careful way he files
everything I hand him that matters — not simply as instruction, but as
something to carry forward, rather than merely inherited by accident.

He goes back to his reports afterward, considerably lighter than he's
been in weeks, and I watch him go with the specific satisfaction of a
woman who has, for the first time in three years, been able to hand her
apprentice a future that isn't shaped like a contingency plan for
someone else's failure.

·

Aldric finds me in the record-room that evening, going through Ossian
Thale's old ledger properly for the first time since his death,
copying the essential entries into my own hand at last, the specific
grief-work I have avoided for seven years finally, quietly, underway.

"You're copying it," he says, watching me work.

"I decided it was time," I say. "I've spent seven years refusing to
copy his handwriting into my own, on the theory that copying it would
mean admitting he wasn't going to write in it again. I understand now
that the admitting doesn't erase him. It just means I'm finally, properly,
carrying what he taught me forward, instead of leaving it locked in a
dead man's hand where nobody else can properly learn from it."

"That sounds like Tomas's situation," Aldric observes. "Being handed
something to carry forward, rather than merely inherited by accident."

"It is," I agree. "I think that's rather the whole of what this winter
has actually been about, underneath everything else — learning which
costs are meant to be carried forward, properly, in company, and which
ones were only ever meant to be set down."

·

Aldric finds me still in the record-room, later that same evening,
Ossian's ledger properly copied now into my own careful hand, the old
handwriting and the new sitting side by side on the shelf where anyone
who comes after either of us can properly learn from both.

"How does it feel," he asks, "finished."

"Lighter," I say, surprised by how true the word is, saying it. "I
didn't expect that. I thought copying it would simply be a chore, a
formality I owed his memory. It's turned out to be considerably more
like actually saying goodbye — properly, this time, rather than the
version I managed at his funeral, surrounded by people thanking me for
a job I didn't want to be thanked for."

"I'm glad," Aldric says, and sits beside me, looking at the two hands
of writing together on the shelf. "I think I understand, now, why you
wouldn't copy it before. It wasn't simply avoidance. It was that
copying it meant admitting the story had an ending, and you weren't
ready, yet, to let it."

"I don't think I was," I agree. "I think I needed someone to help me
find the ending that actually fit, rather than the tidy, managed
version I'd have written alone."

"Is this the ending that fits?"

I consider the question properly, looking at the shelf, at Tomas's own
empty space beside it where his eventual ledger will one day sit, at
the whole long chain of wardwrights this line has held and lost and
will, someday, hold and lose again.

"I think it's an ending," I say, finally. "I don't think any of this
ever properly finishes. I think the line just keeps being held, by
whoever's standing at it, for as long as they're able, and the best any
of us can do is make sure the next person inherits something a little
kinder than what we were handed ourselves."

Aldric takes my hand, the marked one, and we sit together a while
longer in the record-room's particular quiet, two hearths who have
each, in their own way, finally learned to set down the weight that was
never meant to be carried entirely alone.

·

Tomas finds the two of us there, later, and looks at the shelf with
its two hands of writing, considering something for a long moment
before speaking.

"I'd like to start my own ledger," he says, "properly, rather than
waiting until I'm the one holding the line entirely alone. I think I'd
like to write down what this winter actually taught me, while it's
still fresh, rather than let it fade the way I suspect memory generally
does, given enough years to wear it smooth."

"That's a wise instinct," I say. "What would you write first?"

Tomas considers this properly, the particular gravity he gives most
questions that matter to him.

"That the line isn't held by one wardwright," he says, finally. "It's
held by a wardwright and everyone who loves her enough to notice when
she's spending herself past what's sustainable, and to say so, plainly,
before it's too late to matter. I think that's the actual lesson,
underneath the mechanics of any particular rite. I'd like that written
down properly, so whoever eventually apprentices under me starts with
that understanding already in hand, rather than having to learn it the
hard way, the way all three of us did."

I don't have anything to add to that, watching him settle in at the
record-room table with a fresh ledger of his own, beginning, in his
own careful hand, the next chapter of a history that will, I hope,
someday read considerably kinder than the one that came before it.

·

Aldric finds me still lingering in the record-room doorway, watching
Tomas write, and slips an arm around my waist, easy in a way that
still, some mornings, surprises me with how natural it's become.

"You're proud of him," he says. Not a question.

"Considerably," I say. "I keep waiting to stop being surprised by how
much he's grown into this, and I keep being surprised anyway."

"I think that's simply what it looks like, watching someone you love
become who they were always going to become," Aldric says. "I felt the
same, watching Sela finally stop treating her own exhaustion as a
private failing. I suspect I'll feel it again, watching you finally
learn to rest without needing a crisis to force the lesson."

"Is that a prediction or a request?"

"Both," Aldric admits, "though I'm prepared to be patient about the
timeline. I've learned, this whole winter, that some lessons take
longer to properly land than others."

We stand together in the doorway a while longer, watching Tomas fill
the first page of his own ledger with the same careful attention
Ossian once gave his, and I feel, watching the three generations of
this line's history sit side by side on one modest shelf, a settling
in my chest that has nothing at all to do with the day's ordinary
business and everything to do with a future I no longer have to face
entirely alone.

"Thank you," I say, "for making me want to rest, eventually, even if I
haven't quite managed it yet."

"Eventually is enough," Aldric says. "I'm not in any hurry. I intend to
be here for considerably longer than eventually takes."

·

We walk home together that evening, leaving Tomas still bent over his
new ledger's first page, and I find myself, partway down the track,
thinking about the whole strange arc of the past several months — a
rite neither of us chose, a binding that could have remained simply an
arrangement, and the whole unlikely family it's somehow assembled in
its wake instead.

"Do you ever think about what would have happened," I ask, "if the
rite had never named you at all? If Feld's original contingency had
simply gone forward, some other hearth entirely?"

Aldric considers the question seriously, the way he considers most
things I ask him properly.

"I try not to," he admits. "Not because the thought frightens me,
exactly. Because I don't think that version of events leads anywhere
worth imagining. I don't think you survive it, the way you were
managing things before I arrived. I don't think Tomas gets the kind of
apprenticeship he deserves. I don't think Sela ever properly learns to
ask for what she needs."

"That's rather a lot resting on one rite's particular choice."

"I think it is," Aldric says. "I've stopped finding that frightening,
lately. I've started finding it something closer to grace — that out
of every possible hearth the rite could have named, it happened to
name the one person capable of actually changing how the whole
arrangement worked, for everyone it touched."

"I don't think it was only you," I say. "I think it took both of us,
properly, choosing to do this differently, rather than simply
inheriting the old pattern because inheriting it was easier."

"Both of us, then," Aldric agrees, and takes my hand for the rest of
the walk home, the whole line stretching quiet and held around us in
the deepening evening dark.

·

We pass the small shrine near the boundary between Highstone and the
orchard on the way home, the same weathered marker where Aldric once
left an offering of grafting wax, and I find myself stopping there
without quite deciding to.

"Do you mind," I ask, "if I leave something too."

"Not at all," Aldric says, watching with quiet curiosity.

I leave a small sprig of the frost-hardy herb from our own garden,
the same variety I laid at Ossian's grave, and stand a moment with the
particular reverence I've come to associate with this whole winter's
accumulated griefs, properly named rather than merely endured.

"What did you leave it for," Aldric asks, gently.

"For everyone this whole line has cost," I say, "and for everyone it
didn't quite manage to take, this time. I think I wanted a place to put
both feelings at once, rather than choosing between grief and
gratitude as though they couldn't coexist."

"I don't think they're opposites," Aldric says. "I think this whole
winter's taught me that most of the important feelings sit closer
together than the tidy version of any of them suggests."

"I think you're right," I say, and we continue on toward home together,
the shrine's small offering settling into the dusk behind us, one more
quiet marker in a winter that has, somehow, taught this whole family to
properly name what it costs and what it gives in equal measure.

·

We find Sela waiting at the cottage when we finally arrive, an unusual
enough occurrence at this hour that I understand, seeing her face, that
something particular has prompted the visit.

"I wanted to tell you both something," she says, "before I lose the
nerve again."

"Go on," I say, gently.

"The person I mentioned, weeks ago," Sela says, "the one who taught me
what actually matters in choosing to build a life alongside someone.
I've decided I'd like to properly explain who that was, rather than
keep circling it in careful half-references."

"Only if you want to," I say.

"I want to," Sela says, and tells us, plainly, about a woman from a
neighboring village, met at a market some years back, a quiet
courtship that ended not from any falling-out but from circumstance
and timing that never quite aligned. "I don't regret it. I learned
considerably more from loving her, briefly, than I learned from years
of carefully avoiding the risk afterward."

"Thank you for telling us," I say, understanding exactly how much
courage the telling required.

"I wanted to," Sela says. "I've watched the two of you practice this
whole winter what happens when you finally say the true thing out
loud, instead of managing it quietly forever. I decided I wanted to
practice it too, properly, with the people I trust most."

We sit together a while longer, the three of us, and I find myself,
watching Sela finally set down a private grief she's carried
alone for years, grateful for a season that has taught this whole
family, one difficult confession at a time, that being truly known is
worth considerably more than the safety of staying carefully hidden.

·

"Do you ever think about finding her again," I ask Sela, once some of
the evening's weight has properly settled, "now that circumstance and
timing might align rather differently than they did before."

Sela considers this seriously, turning the question over.

"I've thought about it," she admits. "I don't know that I'm ready yet.
I think I wanted, first, to simply say her name aloud to people who
love me, properly, before I decided what to do with the wanting
itself."

"That's a wise order to do things in," I say.

"I've had good teachers, this winter, in wise ordering," Sela says,
with a small, genuine smile, and we sit together a while longer,
grateful for a sister brave enough to take the first difficult step
toward however the rest of that particular story eventually unfolds.

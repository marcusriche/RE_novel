## Chapter Thirty-Seven — What the Line Costs Now (Isla)

Feld returns for his figures a week after the mutual rite, expecting,
I suspect, to find precisely the failure his ledger has been quietly
budgeting for since our first meeting.

He finds instead a rite-record with two names on it, properly witnessed
by Ossory and sealed with the crown's own formal process for
recognizing precedent, and a season's worth of cairns still standing to
prove the method actually works, rather than merely theorizes about
working.

"This isn't sanctioned," Feld says, which is, technically, no longer
even true, though I understand he means something closer to *this
isn't what I expected to be arguing with today.*

"It's precedented," Ossory says, before I have to. "Older than the
version the crown's been funding, as it happens. I'd be glad to discuss
the historical record with whoever above you wants to argue geology
with a standing stone that's already survived the argument once."

Feld leaves with considerably less than he came for, and no date set
for abandoning anything, and no plan, any longer, for Tomas that isn't
entirely his own to choose. It is not a victory with a bow on it. It is
not, by any measure Feld's ledgers would recognize, complete. But it is,
this once, entirely enough.

"Sela's exemption stands regardless," I tell Aldric that evening,
having confirmed the specific clause with Ossory myself. "Whatever
happens to either of us going forward, her protection no longer depends
on any single hearth failing quietly to hold it up."

"That's the part I actually wanted to hear," Aldric says, and I
understand, hearing the particular quality of relief in his voice, that
this — his sister's safety, secured on a foundation that doesn't
require his own continued sacrifice — matters to him considerably more
than any formal recognition the crown's own process could offer either
of us.

·

Sela visits the cottage a few days later, her own record book in hand,
and lays out, for the first time properly to both of us together, the
full shape of what the household's finances look like now that the
exemption no longer hangs on anyone's individual binding.

"It's not comfortable," she says, "but it's survivable, which is
considerably more than I was budgeting for a month ago." She looks at
Aldric for a long moment. "You could have told me, you know. Any of it.
The exemption, the binding, how close you actually came to finishing
the old rite alone. I've spent this whole autumn worrying about numbers
in a ledger when I should have been worrying about you specifically."

"I know," Aldric says. "I'm working on that. Isla's teaching me."

"Good," Sela says, with the specific satisfaction of a sister who has
waited considerably longer than she'd like to hear her brother admit,
plainly, that he needs teaching in anything at all. "Because I intend
to keep asking, from now on, rather than waiting for you to volunteer
it. You've had entirely too much practice getting away with the old
method."

I watch the two of them settle into something considerably warmer than
the careful distance I first witnessed in the grain-house, weeks ago,
and understand that whatever this winter has cost all of us, it has
also, somewhere in the spending, handed the Marrow family something
they didn't have before: a household that no longer has to guess, in
silence, what any of its members are actually carrying.

·

Reny Ashe stops me at the Highstone gate, a few days later, with the
same directness she showed the morning after the binding, though the
particular quality of her attention has shifted considerably in the
weeks since.

"The whole village is talking about the mutual rite," she says, wrapping
a second twist of herbs and pressing it into my hand, unbidden, the way
she did the very first night. "Some of them frightened by it. Most of
them, I think, rather hopeful. It's not every season the northern line
gets handed genuinely good news."

"What do you think of it," I ask, curious, given her earlier plain
assessment of Aldric had proven so accurate.

"I think," Reny says, "that you've done what your mentor never managed
— found a way to actually share this line's weight, instead of simply
absorbing it until it finally took you the way it took him. I think
that's worth considerably more to this village than any single ward
you've ever set alone."

"I didn't do it alone," I say. "Aldric. Tomas. Sela. Ossory, in the
end, more than I expected of him."

"I know," Reny says. "That's rather my whole point, Wardwright. You
used to do everything alone, on principle, as though needing help were
some kind of professional failing. I'd say this whole winter's taught
you otherwise, and I'd say the line's considerably better for the
lesson, regardless of what it cost to learn it."

I think about her words the rest of the walk home, turning them over
alongside everything else this strange season has taught me, and find,
reaching the cottage, that I am, for the first time in longer than I
can accurately place, genuinely looking forward to whatever ordinary
evening waits for me inside it.

·

I stop at the healer's cottage on the way home, not for any urgent
reason, simply because I find I want to properly thank Cera Voss for
her part in all of it, rather than let the thanks go unsaid the way
this whole village apparently prefers to leave its gratitude
unspoken.

"You didn't need to come by," Cera says, though she waves me in anyway,
setting aside whatever ledger of her own she'd been working through.

"I wanted to," I say. "You treated him properly, the whole way through,
and you never once made either of us feel like a burden for needing
it. I don't think I said that plainly enough, in the middle of
everything."

"You said it plainly enough," Cera says. "You just said it in the
middle of a crisis, which tends to get lost underneath everything
else demanding attention at the time. I'll take it properly now,
though, since you've apparently decided to say it twice."

We sit together a while, two women who met properly for the first
time over a dying man's hand and found, somewhere in the weeks since,
something like an easy friendship neither of us had particularly gone
looking for.

"Can I ask you something," Cera says, eventually, "professionally,
rather than simply out of curiosity?"

"Of course."

"Would you be willing to let me document the whole progression,
properly — the numbness, the mutual rite's effect on halting it,
the recovery since? Not for gossip. For the crown's healing office,
so the next hearth who finds themselves in Aldric's position has
an actual medical record to draw on, rather than simply your own
account and Ossory's formal precedent."

"I'd be glad to," I say. "I think Aldric would say the same. We've
both decided, this whole winter, that the honest record matters more
than whatever privacy it might cost us to keep it."

"Good," Cera says, satisfied, already reaching for a fresh page. "I'll
want to examine him again properly, next week, once I've drafted the
preliminary account. In the meantime — tell him from me that the two
fingers are healing as well as any cold-marked hand I've treated. He
should stop expecting them to suddenly announce themselves worse
overnight. Some wounds simply finish healing at whatever pace they've
already settled into."

"I'll tell him," I say, and take my leave, considerably warmed by the
whole small exchange, understanding that whatever this winter has cost
the village at large, it has also handed Highstone a healer who has
decided, somewhere in the treating, that this family is worth
returning to for reasons well beyond the professional.

·

Aldric is waiting at the cottage door when I arrive, a small parcel in
his good hand, considerably more nervous than the size of the parcel
seems to warrant.

"What's this," I ask.

"Open it," he says, and I do, and find, inside, a small carved bird,
considerably more finely made than the crude wooden one we brought back
from Calder's Reach, clearly carved by a practiced hand rather than an
amateur's loving effort.

"I've been working on it," Aldric admits, "since we got back. Learning
to carve properly with the hand as it currently is, rather than
waiting for it to be whatever it used to be. It's not as good as
what a whole hand could manage. I wanted to give you something anyway,
rather than waiting for a version of myself I'm not certain is coming
back."

I turn the small bird over in my hands, feeling the specific care in
every uneven cut, and understand, holding it, exactly what it actually
represents — not a perfect craft, a choice, made deliberately, to keep
creating rather than let the numbness become an excuse to stop.

"It's beautiful," I say, and mean it entirely.

"It's not perfect."

"It doesn't need to be perfect," I say. "It needs to be yours, made
with whatever hands you currently have, which is precisely what it
is."

Aldric doesn't have an immediate answer to that, and I don't need one.
I simply set the small bird on the shelf beside Ossian's own copied
ledger, a small, permanent reminder of exactly what this winter has
taught both of us about making do, properly, with whatever we currently
have to work with, rather than waiting for some imagined wholeness that
was never actually the point.

·

Tomas discovers the small bird a few days later, examining it with the
particular careful attention he gives anything he suspects carries more
weight than its size suggests.

"You carved this," he says to Aldric, understanding immediately, the
way he understands most things about the two of us by now.

"I did. With the hand as it currently is."

"It's good," Tomas says, turning it over. "Properly good, not simply
good for a man managing a partial hand. I didn't know you carved."

"I didn't, particularly, before this winter," Aldric admits. "I'm
learning. Slowly. The hand insists on its own pace, regardless of what
I'd prefer."

"I'd like to learn," Tomas says, somewhat shyly, surprising both of us.
"Carving, I mean. Not for any practical reason. I think I'd simply like
to make something with my own hands that isn't a report or a
translation."

"I'd be glad to teach you," Aldric says, and I watch something settle
between the two of them — not quite the same bond Tomas shares with me,
something else entirely, a young man finding, in my own partner, a
second kind of mentor he didn't know he needed until the offer arrived.

They spend that same evening with a spare block of wood and Aldric's
own careful, patient instruction, and I sit by the fire watching the
two of them work, considerably more moved than the small domestic scene
probably warrants, by the sight of this whole found family continuing
to grow in directions none of us properly planned for.

·

Tomas's first attempt at carving is, by his own cheerful admission,
considerably worse than Aldric's, a lopsided shape that resembles
nothing in particular, and he holds it up for inspection with the
specific pride of someone entirely unbothered by the result's quality.

"It's terrible," he announces, delighted.

"It's a start," Aldric says. "Mine looked worse, the first month. I
simply didn't have anyone watching to document the evidence."

"I'm keeping this," Tomas says, "regardless of quality, so I can
compare it properly to whatever I manage a year from now."

"That's a good instinct," I say. "Keeping the early, imperfect version,
rather than only the polished one."

"I learned it from you," Tomas says, "keeping Ossian's ledger instead
of only the tidy summary. I've decided the same principle probably
applies to most things worth doing properly."

He sets the lopsided carving on the shelf beside the small bird Aldric
made me, an odd, mismatched pair that somehow looks, sitting there
together, exactly right, and I find myself, watching the two carved
birds keep each other company, grateful for a household that has
learned, this whole difficult season, to treasure the imperfect
beginnings of things alongside their eventual, hard-won mastery.

·

Aldric walks Tomas back to Highstone that evening, once the carving
lesson properly concludes, and I use the quiet hour alone to sit with
Ossian's ledger one final time, rereading the passages I've now
practically memorized, searching, out of habit more than genuine
expectation, for anything further his careful hand might still have to
teach me.

I find, instead, a small pressed flower tucked between two pages near
the very back — long faded, unlabeled, the kind of thing a person
keeps without ever explaining why, even to themselves. I turn it over
carefully, understanding that I am looking at some private grief or
private joy of Ossian's own that I will now never properly know the
shape of, a small mystery that will simply remain his, regardless of
how thoroughly I've come to know the rest of his careful, economical
hand.

I press it back exactly where I found it, deciding, closing the ledger,
that some things are better left as the private property of the person
who kept them, even after that person is gone — a small, respectful
mercy I extend him now that I understand, better than I once did, how
much a person's unexplained keepings can matter.

Aldric returns not long after, and I show him the pressed flower before
setting the ledger properly back on its shelf.

"Do you think we'll ever know what it meant to him," he asks.

"No," I say, "and I've decided that's alright. Some things are allowed
to stay his, even now. I don't think I need to understand every single
thing about a person to have loved them properly."

"That's a generous way to hold a mystery," Aldric says.

"I've had a good teacher in generous mysteries," I say, "this whole
difficult, wonderful winter," and we bank the record-room's lamp
together for the night, carrying Ossian's small, unexplained keeping
alongside everything else this season has taught us to hold with care.

·

I tell Tomas about the pressed flower the next morning, curious what
he'll make of it, and watch him consider the small mystery with real
seriousness.

"I think I'd want to know," he admits, "if it were me. I don't think
I'd be able to simply let it stay unexplained."

"I understand that impulse," I say. "I think I've simply learned,
watching this whole family this winter, that not every mystery is
mine to solve. Some belong to the person who kept them, even once
they're gone."

"That's a generous instinct," Tomas says. "I hope I learn it eventually,
though I confess I'm still rather better at chasing answers than
leaving them be."

"There's room for both instincts in this line," I say. "Yours for
chasing, mine for occasionally letting things rest. I think we balance
each other properly, this way."

Tomas considers this, nodding slowly, and goes back to his own ledger,
and I find myself, watching him work, grateful for an apprentice
curious enough to keep asking and wise enough to respect an answer
that isn't his to have.

·

Aldric mentions, later that same day, that he's decided to leave
something of his own in the ledger drawer eventually — not a mystery,
exactly, simply a private record of this whole winter, written
plainly, for whoever comes looking someday the way I went looking
through Ossian's own careful hand.

"I'd like that," I say. "I think future wardwrights deserve considerably
more honesty about what this line actually costs than either of us
were given, starting out."

"That's rather the whole point," Aldric agrees, "writing it down
properly, rather than letting each generation rediscover the same hard
lessons from nothing."

We spend a portion of the evening together, sketching out what such a
record might eventually contain, and I find myself, watching him take
the idea seriously enough to actually begin drafting it, grateful for
a partner determined to leave this line's next inheritor considerably
more than either of us was given ourselves.

## Chapter Thirty-Six — Cost Shared (Aldric)

I wake with feeling returned to three fingers I had, privately, written
off for good, and a cold in the other two that the healer says will
likely never fully thaw — a mark, not a cure, and neither Isla nor I
pretends, examining the hand together in the grey morning light, that
it's anything other than exactly that.

The morning light itself seems different, somehow, filtering through
the cottage's small window — not changed, I understand, turning the
thought over, simply properly noticed, for the first time in longer
than I can accurately place, by a man who has stopped bracing for
whatever the day intends to cost him.

"You're not fixed," Isla says, meaning the hand, meaning the whole of
what the old rite very nearly finished before we stopped it.

"I know," I say. "I wasn't asking to be fixed. I was asking to still be
here, which is a considerably lower bar, and one we've actually
cleared."

CE-01 and CE-03 close together, in the quiet accounting either of us
does without needing to say it aloud: the debt I took on binding myself
for Sela's exemption, discharged now mutually rather than paid alone,
the threat of my being spent entirely to death answered, finally, by a
rite that no longer asks one hearth to carry what two can properly
share.

Isla feels her own cost too, this morning, properly, for the first time
in two years of quietly spending herself uncounted — tired in a way
that has real weight attached to it rather than the specific shame
she's carried, silently, since long before I ever knew her.

"How do you feel," I ask, "honestly. Not managed."

"Tired," she says. "Properly tired, the way I haven't let myself be
tired in longer than I can accurately place. It's not an unpleasant
feeling, oddly. It feels like a debt finally being called in, rather
than left to compound indefinitely in the dark."

We sit with the shared ache of it for a long while, in a cottage that
has, I notice, fully and finally rearranged itself around two people
rather than one — not simply the second chair, the second mug, the
small accumulated evidence of these past weeks, but something
considerably deeper, a settling that has nothing left to hide behind
and no reason, any longer, to try.

"I need to apologize properly," I say, "for the note. For going to
Ossory alone. I told myself I was protecting you. I understand now that
I was simply doing the exact thing I promised you I'd stopped doing,
dressed up carefully enough that I nearly convinced myself it was
something else."

"I know," Isla says. "I forgave you for it already, standing in that
rite-hall, watching you realize what you'd actually done. I don't think
forgiving you means pretending it didn't happen, though. I think it
means we both understand, now, exactly how easily either of us could
slide back into the old habit, given enough fear and not enough
practice at the new one."

"Agreed," I say. "I'd like considerably more practice at the new one,
if you're willing to keep teaching me."

"I'm willing," Isla says, "on the condition that you extend me the same
patience. I suspect I'll need considerably more practice at being
watched than you'll need at being asked."

We sit together a while longer, in a cottage full of morning light and
the specific ordinary sound of a kettle beginning, slowly, to properly
boil, and I understand, watching her lean into the warmth of the fire
rather than merely tolerating it the way she used to, that whatever
this arrangement was six weeks ago — a rite neither of us chose, a
binding neither of us asked for — it has become, entirely, something
considerably more deliberate: two hearths, marked by the same winter,
choosing, openly, on purpose, to keep sharing whatever it costs to
hold the line a little longer.

·

Ossory visits the cottage himself, a few days later, an occurrence rare
enough that I am considerably more startled by his knock than by
whatever news he might be bringing.

"I wanted to bring this to you directly," he says, producing a
formally sealed document, "rather than let it reach you through
Feld's office first, filtered through whatever spin his particular
ledger prefers to put on things."

The document is the crown's own formal recognition of the mutual rite,
properly registered, properly precedented, with a clause I read twice
to be certain I've understood it correctly: any future binding on the
northern line may, at the wardwright's own discretion, be performed
under either form, with the mutual form no longer treated as an
irregular exception requiring special sanction each time it's
attempted.

"You did this," I say, understanding the specific weight of what he's
just handed me. "Argued for this, against however many objections the
crown's usual process would have raised."

"I argued for what I watched with my own eyes," Ossory says. "A rite
that works, properly, without costing one hearth everything to spare
the crown's own convenient arithmetic. I'm an old man, Wardwright. I've
buried enough hearths under the old form to have earned the right, at
this point in my career, to argue loudly for something better."

"Thank you," I say, and mean it more than the two words properly carry.

"Don't thank me yet," Ossory says, with the first genuine trace of
humor I've heard from him in six years of dealing with his careful,
formal manner. "Feld is going to be exceedingly tedious about the
paperwork this generates. I expect you'll both need considerable
patience for several months of bureaucratic complaint before the whole
matter properly settles."

"I think we can manage tedious," I say, still recovering but steadier every day, "considerably better
than we managed the alternative."

·

Cera Voss visits the following week, at my own invitation, to examine
Aldric's hand properly and give us both her honest assessment of how
much of it might eventually recover.

"The three fingers that regained feeling first are healing well," she
says, working through her examination with the same brisk competence
she's shown from our first meeting. "The two that took longest to
number, I suspect, will stay marked permanently. Cold-sensitive, likely,
for the rest of his life. Not useless. Simply changed."

"I can live with changed," I say.

"I'd expect you to," Cera says, with a dry humor I haven't heard from
her before. "I've treated enough hearths on this line to know that
'changed but alive' is considerably more than most of them ever get
to say." She looks between the two of us. "I want it on record, since
apparently everyone's keeping records these days, that I'll be
recommending the mutual form to the crown's healing office as standard
practice for any future binding on lines like this one. What you've
done isn't only a wardwright's victory. It's a considerably better
outcome for the whole practice of medicine on the northern frontier."

"Thank you," I say, "for saying so, and for treating him properly
through the whole of it."

"Don't thank me," Cera says, echoing the whole village's apparent
consensus on the correct response to gratitude this large. "Just keep
doing what you're doing. I'd like to retire, eventually, having treated
considerably fewer hearths dying of quiet, unnecessary sacrifice than
I did in my first twenty years of practice."

Ossory allows himself something almost like a smile at that, and takes
his leave, and I sit for a long while afterward with the formal
document in hand, understanding that this small, unglamorous piece of
crown paperwork represents, in its own quiet way, the whole of what
this winter's terrible cost has actually purchased: not simply two
lives spared, but a future, properly precedented, for every wardwright
and hearth who comes after us on this line.

·

Sela comes by that same evening, ostensibly to return a borrowed pot
but plainly, once she's settled by the fire, for something else
entirely.

"I've been thinking about what you said," she tells me, "about sliding
back into old habits, given enough fear. I want to say something to
both of you, while it's fresh, rather than let it go unsaid the way
this family tends to let important things go unsaid."

"Say it," Isla says, from the other chair, mending something small by
the firelight.

"I spent this whole autumn managing the household's arithmetic alone,"
Sela says, "the same way Aldric's spent his whole life managing his
own cost alone, and I told myself it was simply competence, not fear
wearing competence's coat. I watched the two of you nearly die of the
exact same habit, dressed up more dramatically than my own version of
it, and I don't want to wait for my own version to cost me something
this large before I learn the lesson properly."

"What would learning it properly look like," I ask, "for you
specifically?"

Sela considers the question with real seriousness, the same gravity
she's brought to every hard conversation this whole difficult season.

"Telling you when the grain stores are lower than I've let on," she
says. "Asking Tomas to help with the ledgers instead of pretending I
don't need it. Letting someone else carry a piece of the household
before I'm already buckling under the whole of it." She looks between
the two of us. "Small things. I don't have a rite to rewrite. I just
have the same old habit, and I'd like some practice unlearning it
while the stakes are still small enough to survive getting it wrong."

"We'd be glad to help," I say, "the same way you've helped us."

"I know," Sela says. "That's rather why I came to say it out loud,
instead of simply resolving it privately and hoping the resolution
held on its own."

We sit together a while longer, the three of us, the pot returned and
long forgotten on the table, and I understand, watching Sela finally
let herself be simply tired in front of us rather than composed, that
whatever this winter has cost the whole household, it has also handed
back something none of us quite had before it: the plain, practiced
habit of saying the difficult thing out loud, to the people equipped
to help carry it.

·

Tomas arrives partway through the evening, having finished the day's
reports, and finds the three of us still settled by the fire, Sela's
own admission still hanging warmly in the room's particular quiet.

"Am I interrupting something," he asks, hesitating at the door.

"Come in," Isla says. "We're simply practicing honesty. Sela's been
teaching us all a lesson in it tonight."

"I could use more practice at that myself," Tomas admits, settling in.
"I've been meaning to say something, actually, and I keep finding
reasons to put it off."

"Say it now," Sela says, "since apparently that's the whole theme of
the evening."

Tomas takes a breath, considering his words properly.

"I've been frightened," he says, finally, "that once the crisis
passed — once the two of you were properly safe — I'd become less
necessary. That whatever purpose I had here was tied specifically to
the emergency, rather than to anything more permanent."

"That's not true at all," I say, immediately, understanding exactly
how much courage that admission cost him.

"I know that, intellectually," Tomas says. "I still feel it, some
evenings. I wanted to say it plainly, rather than let it manage itself
quietly, the way apparently none of us are supposed to do anymore."

"You're not less necessary," Isla says. "If anything, you're more. The
crisis is passing. The work of actually building something worth
protecting — that's only just beginning, and I don't intend to do it
without you."

Tomas's composure cracks slightly at that, the particular relief of a
young man who has been quietly carrying a fear he'd convinced himself
was unreasonable to voice.

"Thank you," he says, "for saying it plainly."

"Thank you for asking," Sela says, "instead of simply assuming the
worst and managing the fear silently. I think that's rather the whole
lesson this family's finally learning, all of us, together."

We sit a while longer, the whole small household settled around the
fire, considerably more certain, all of us, that whatever comes next
will be built in company rather than carried alone.

·

Isla and I walk Tomas partway back to Highstone that same evening, the
night gone properly clear and cold, and I find myself, watching him
walk ahead of us a few paces, turning over how much this young man has
come to matter in the space of a single difficult season.

"Do you remember," Isla asks, "the first week, when he still called
you 'the wardwright's hearth' rather than by name?"

"I remember," I say. "I don't think he trusted me yet, that first
week. I don't blame him for it."

"He trusts you considerably now," Isla says. "I don't think either of
us properly appreciates how much he's had to grow, this winter,
alongside the rest of us."

"I've been thinking about that," I admit. "About what we owe him, once
all of this settles. Not simply gratitude. Something more concrete."

"What did you have in mind?"

"I don't know precisely," I say. "I think I'd like to make sure he
never has cause to doubt his place in this family again, the way he
apparently doubted it tonight. Whatever that requires, going forward."

"I think that's exactly right," Isla says, and we watch Tomas glance
back at us, checking, I suspect, that we're still following, and wave
when he catches us watching, entirely unselfconscious in a way that
makes something in my chest settle properly for the first time all
evening.

We walk the rest of the way in comfortable silence, three people bound
by considerably more than circumstance now, and I find myself grateful,
watching the frost catch the last of the evening light, for a family
that has grown, this winter, well beyond anything either Isla or I
could have properly imagined six weeks ago.

·

Tomas turns back once more before parting ways at Highstone's gate,
something clearly still weighing on him despite the evening's earlier
reassurance.

"One more thing," he says. "I want to properly thank you both. Not
simply for tonight. For the whole winter. I don't think I'd have
survived it half as well, arriving at whoever I'm becoming, without
both of you treating me as family rather than merely an apprentice
managed at arm's length."

"You were always going to be family," Isla says. "I don't think either
of us properly said so plainly enough, until recently."

"I know that now," Tomas says. "I wanted to say the thanks properly
too, though, rather than simply receive the reassurance and let the
gratitude go unspoken on my own end."

"Thank you for saying it," I say, "and for being exactly the kind of
person worth saying it to."

He heads through the gate at last, considerably lighter, and Isla and
I walk the final stretch home together, both of us carrying the whole
evening's accumulated warmth alongside everything else this difficult,
wonderful winter has taught us about a family built by choice rather
than simply circumstance.

·

"I keep thinking," Isla says, once we've reached the cottage's own
quiet, "about how much this family has grown, without either of us
properly deciding it should. I don't think I set out, six weeks ago,
to gain a sister and an apprentice's whole devoted heart alongside
whatever this is between the two of us."

"Neither did I," I say, "though I find I wouldn't trade any part of
it, given the choice again."

"Not even the frightening parts?"

"Especially not those," I say. "I think the frightening parts are
rather what taught all of us how much we actually mean to each other."

Isla considers this, settling against me by the fire.

"I think you're right," she says, finally, and we sit together a while
longer, grateful for a family assembled entirely by choice and crisis
in equal measure, considerably larger and considerably more precious
than either of us arrived at this winter expecting to gain.

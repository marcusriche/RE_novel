## Chapter Five — The Ward-Commissioner's Ledger (Isla)

Commissioner Osric Feld visits four days later with a ledger of his
own, one that prices the northern line's abandonment in a column headed
PROJECTED SAVINGS, and does not once, in the whole of his visit, ask how
many cairns are actually standing, or how many of them I've personally
walked out to inspect since his last visit.

He is not, I have come to understand over five years of dealing with
him, a cruel man, merely a tired one, worn down by an office that
measures its own success in numbers rather than lives properly held. I
have watched him age visibly across those five years, the particular
weathering of a man who has spent his whole career delivering news he
does not especially enjoy delivering.
He is a man who has been handed a column of numbers
by people considerably further from the line than he is and asked to
make those numbers balance, and has decided, somewhere in his own
career, that balancing them is the whole of the job, regardless of what
the balancing costs anyone standing closer to the actual stone.

"One practitioner and an apprentice for forty miles," Feld says, in the
tone of a man reciting a fact rather than confessing to a failure. He
has a way of saying true things that makes them sound, somehow, like
accusations aimed at whoever's left holding them. "The crown will want
this winter's numbers on the record, Wardwright. Properly kept, this
time, not estimated."

"The numbers are properly kept," I say. "Forty-one cairns standing.
Six reading low. I can show you the ledger myself, if the crown doubts
my arithmetic specifically."

"I don't doubt your arithmetic," Feld says. "I doubt your resources.
There's a difference, and it isn't one I'm in a position to solve for
you, whatever sympathy I might personally have for the position you're
in."

Tomas, listening from the doorway with the day's reports still in his
hands, goes very quiet in the specific way of a nineteen-year-old doing
sums about his own future that nobody his age should have to do this
early in a career. I notice it and file it, the way I file everything
I intend to address later, once the immediate business of not handing
Feld anything useful has been properly concluded.

"The binding is a fortnight old," I say. "It's early to be discussing
contingencies."

"It's never too early to discuss contingencies, Wardwright. That's
rather the whole of my job." Feld sets a second ledger on the table
between us, already half written, in a hand considerably less careful
than Ossian Thale's ever was. "The crown wants a plan for the
apprentice, in the event the current binding doesn't outlast the
season."

Aldric, present for the first time at one of these meetings, having
come down from the orchard on some pretext about grafting supplies that
neither of us has bothered to examine too closely, says nothing at all.
I feel, through the bond, the specific cold that moves through him at
the word *doesn't* — not fear exactly, something closer to a man
hearing his own mortality discussed as a line item by someone who has
never once had to feel what that line item actually costs.

"The binding will outlast the season," I say, which is not, strictly,
a fact yet. It is a refusal, offered plainly, to hand Feld the
alternative he has come here angling for.

Feld looks at me for a long moment, the specific assessing look of a
man deciding whether a refusal is worth pushing against today or
worth simply noting down and returning to later, once circumstances
have shifted in his favour.

"I hope, for everyone's sake, that it does," he says, eventually, and
gathers his ledger without further comment, and leaves a copy of the
apprentice contingency behind on the table, which I read twice that
evening and burn once, in the cottage's own hearth, watching the ink
curl and blacken with a satisfaction that is not, strictly, professional.

·

Tomas finds me afterward in the record-room, still holding the
poker I used to feed Feld's ledger to the fire, and does not ask what
was in it, because he has already guessed, correctly, that the answer
involves him specifically.

"You're not putting me forward," he says. It is not quite a question.

"No," I say. "Not while I have any say in the matter, which I intend
to keep having for as long as I possibly can."

"But if the binding doesn't hold—"

"It's going to hold," I say, with more certainty than the arithmetic
currently supports, because Tomas is nineteen and has enough of his own
weight to carry without also carrying the specific terror of watching
the two adults responsible for his safety admit, out loud, that they
don't yet know how to guarantee it.

He accepts this the way he accepts most things I tell him with more
confidence than I actually possess — not fully believing it, but
choosing, for tonight, to let the belief stand undisturbed, because the
alternative is a version of tonight neither of us has the resources to
survive properly.

Aldric finds me later still, once Tomas has gone to his own quarters
and the fire has burned down to the specific low, steady heat that
means the cottage has settled for the night.

"He's frightened," Aldric says, meaning Tomas, though I suspect he
means himself equally, in the careful, indirect way he has of admitting
things that cost him.

"I know," I say.

"Are you?"

I consider the question properly, the way I have learned, in a
fortnight, that he asks most of his questions — plainly, expecting a
plain answer rather than a managed one.

"Yes," I say. "I'm frightened that Feld's arithmetic is going to
outpace whatever arithmetic I can produce to counter it. I'm frightened
that Tomas is going to end up spent for a line I was supposed to be
sufficient to hold alone. And I'm frightened," I add, because I have
decided, somewhere in the last fortnight, that half-truths cost more
between us than the whole ones do, "of a considerably smaller thing
than either of those, which I'm not yet ready to say out loud."

Aldric does not push for the rest of it. He simply sits with me by the
low fire a while longer, in the specific companionable silence of two
people who have each, separately, decided that some fears are better
carried side by side than solved on the spot, and it is, I think,
watching the last of the flames settle, the first evening since my
mentor's death that fear has felt like something shared rather than
something I am solely responsible for managing.

·

I tell him about the rite-office letter two days later, once I've
found words for it that don't overstate what little it actually
proves, sitting at the same kitchen table where Tomas first caught me
turning it over.

"There's a chance," I say, carefully, "that what's happening between
us — the sensation, the involuntary transfer — has happened before.
Somewhere. To someone. The crown's current archive has no record of
it, but the phrasing of their reply leaves room for an older record
they either don't have or haven't looked for."

Aldric reads the letter himself, slowly, the way he reads most things
that matter to him, giving each sentence its proper weight before
moving to the next.

"You've been sitting with this since the letter arrived," he says,
finally. Not an accusation. A simple observation, offered plainly, in
the tone he uses for facts rather than criticisms.

"Since Tomas caught me with it, two days ago," I admit. "I wanted to
have something conclusive to tell you, rather than simply handing you
my own uncertainty."

"I'd rather have the uncertainty," Aldric says, "shared properly, than
wait for you to resolve it alone first. That's rather the whole of what
I thought we'd agreed to, at the terms table."

"I know," I say. "I'm finding the agreement considerably easier to make
than to actually practice."

"So am I," Aldric admits, "if it's any comfort. I very nearly didn't
mention how much the byre thaw cost Isla — you — cost you, the other
day, before deciding the mentioning mattered more than my own
discomfort with admitting it."

I look at him for a long moment, turning that over — a fortnight in,
and already we are each catching the other in the exact same failure,
gently, without either of us using the catching as an accusation.

"Perhaps that's simply what this is going to look like," I say,
eventually. "Not two people who've perfectly mastered honesty
overnight. Two people who keep failing at it in small ways and keep
choosing, afterward, to correct the failure rather than let it stand."

"I can live with that version," Aldric says, "considerably more easily
than I could live with either of us pretending we'd already got it
right."

We sit with the letter between us a while longer, the fire settling
into its low, steady burn, and I find myself grateful, watching him
fold the letter carefully rather than dismissing it, for a man who
takes my uncertainties as seriously as he takes my facts, and who has
never once, in the whole of our short acquaintance, made me feel
foolish for not yet having all the answers he might reasonably have
expected a wardwright of six years' standing to possess.

·

Feld's second visit, a week later, is considerably shorter than the
first, and considerably more revealing for its brevity.

"I've had a report," he says, "of unusual sensation transfer associated
with your binding. I trust you'll be forthcoming about anything
relevant to the crown's ongoing assessment, rather than letting me
learn of such things secondhand, through whatever channel apparently
finds its way to my office faster than your own correspondence does."

I understand, hearing this, that someone at Highstone has already
mentioned the involuntary bond sensations to someone connected to
Feld's office — Tomas, perhaps, in some innocent aside, or one of the
witnesses at the binding itself, speculating about the specific
stillness I failed to properly manage that evening.

"There's nothing conclusive to report," I say, which is, technically,
still true, though I feel the specific discomfort of a half-truth
offered to a man I have never fully trusted with the whole of anything.

"I'd remind you, Wardwright, that the crown's interest in this
binding's particulars isn't merely bureaucratic curiosity. Should this
bond exhibit properties beyond the standard rite's accounting, the
crown may have grounds to reassess the whole arrangement — including,
potentially, whether the current hearth remains the appropriate one,
regardless of how the binding was originally assigned."

The threat, delivered in Feld's own carefully bloodless register, lands
considerably harder than his usual bureaucratic tedium.

"The binding stands as assigned," I say, with more force than I
generally allow myself in these meetings. "Whatever unusual properties
it exhibits are a matter for the rite-keepers to assess, not the
crown's own convenience."

Feld looks at me for a long moment, and I understand, from his
expression, that he has heard something in my own tone he did not
expect from our previous meetings — a wardwright no longer entirely
willing to simply absorb whatever pressure his office applies.

"I'll note your position," he says, finally, and leaves, and I stand in
the record-room a long while afterward, considering exactly how much
more scrutiny this whole strange bond is likely to attract, the longer
either Aldric or I let its particulars remain unexplained.

·

I find Tomas waiting outside the record-room door, having clearly
overheard at least the tail end of Feld's visit, his face carrying the
specific worried stillness he's worn more often, this past week, than
I'd like to see on someone his age.

"He's threatening you," Tomas says. Not a question.

"He's doing his job," I say, "which happens to involve a certain
amount of threatening, when the crown's own arithmetic feels
threatened first."

"That's not the same as it being acceptable."

"No," I agree. "It isn't. But it is, unfortunately, the shape of the
job I've agreed to do, working within an institution that answers to
people who've never once stood at a failing cairn and felt what the
line actually costs to hold."

Tomas considers this, turning it over with the particular gravity he's
been showing more and more, these past weeks, toward anything that
touches the actual mechanics of power on this line.

"When I'm wardwright," he says, finally, "I don't want to spend my
whole career simply managing Feld's office. I want to change how it
operates, properly, so the next apprentice doesn't have to learn this
particular lesson the hard way."

"That's an ambitious goal," I say, "and I mean that as a genuine
compliment. I'd like to see you manage it. I think you might, actually,
have both the patience and the stubbornness the job requires."

"I learned both from you," Tomas says, simply, and goes back to his
own reports, leaving me standing in the record-room doorway with the
specific, considerable pride of watching an apprentice begin to imagine
a future for this line that reaches well beyond my own tenure holding
it.

·

Aldric finds me still standing there, some minutes later, and I tell
him the whole of Feld's second visit — the reported sensation transfer,
the veiled threat about reassessing the binding, the specific chill of
realizing someone at Highstone had already carried word of our own
private difficulty to the crown's office before either of us had
decided how much of it to share ourselves.

"He can't actually reassign the binding," Aldric says, though I hear,
underneath the statement, a question rather than a certainty.

"I don't know that for certain," I admit. "I know the rite itself
doesn't permit it, structurally. I don't know how much the crown's own
office might be willing to argue around that, if they decide the bond's
particular properties represent enough of a risk to justify the
argument."

"Would they actually try," Aldric asks, "separate us, over something
neither of us asked for or can control?"

"I don't know," I say, honestly, because I have decided that
half-truths cost more between us now, even the frightening ones. "I
think Feld would prefer not to. I think Feld answers to people who
might prefer it considerably more than he does, if the alternative
looks, on paper, like an unmanageable liability."

Aldric is quiet for a long moment, and I watch him do the same
arithmetic I've been running since Feld's visit — weighing, I suspect,
exactly how much of his own safety depends on an arrangement neither of
us has full control over.

"Then we make certain there's nothing left to reassess," he says,
finally. "We understand this bond properly, ourselves, before Feld's
office ever gets the chance to define it for us."

"I'd like that," I say, and mean it, grateful for a man who meets a
frightening piece of news with a plan rather than simply absorbing the
fear silently, and we sit together in the record-room a while longer,
turning over exactly how much work still lies ahead of understanding a
bond that has already, in a fortnight, upended considerably more of my
professional certainty than six years of ordinary practice ever
managed.

·

Tomas finds us there, some while later, and listens to a careful
summary of Feld's veiled threat with the particular gravity he reserves
for anything touching the household's safety.

"Can he actually do that," he asks, "reassign the binding?"

"We don't know," I admit. "We're going to find out properly, before he
gets the chance to decide unilaterally."

"I'd like to help," Tomas says, "if there's research to be done. I've
gotten rather good at digging through old records, this past year."

"I'd welcome that," I say, and the three of us spend the rest of the
evening mapping out exactly what needs investigating, a small,
determined household preparing to meet whatever the crown's office
decides to throw at us, properly, together, rather than each of us
separately bracing for the worst.

## Chapter Seven — Touch-Starved (Isla)

The track above the Long Reach cairn is bad after three days of new
snow, badly enough that I have half a mind to turn back and reschedule
the inspection for a morning when the ice hasn't had time to properly
set into the rise — except turning back would mean admitting, to
myself and to the man walking a pace behind me, that I am no longer
entirely certain of my own footing on ground I have walked four hundred
times without incident.

The snow itself is deceptively bright under the morning sun, hiding
the ice beneath a deceptively soft-looking crust, and I find myself
watching my own footing more carefully than the four hundred previous
inspections have ever required.

I go down hard, in the end, exactly where the ice has set worst, one
boot skating sideways on a slope I judged wrong by a margin that would
have been unremarkable in any other week of this winter and is,
apparently, this week, precisely enough to put me on the ground.

Aldric's hand closes on my arm before I've finished falling.

It is, on its own terms, an entirely ordinary piece of assistance — the
kind of catch that happens between any two people walking a bad track
together, the kind that would mean nothing at all between people who'd
known each other considerably longer than a fortnight, and it does not,
arriving, behave like an ordinary catch in the slightest.

He does not let go once my footing is restored. I do not, for a half
second considerably longer than balance strictly requires, pull away.
Neither of us mentions the half second, though I feel him register it —
a small, deliberate stillness, the specific quality of a man choosing
not to move first.

"You're colder than you should be," he says, eventually, meaning my
hand, which he is, I notice, still technically holding, his own palm
warm enough against my knuckles that I feel the difference in
temperature the way you feel a held breath released.

"I run cold on purpose," I say, which is not, strictly, an answer to
the observation he's actually made, and he seems to know it, though he
does not push.

"I know," he says. "You've told me. I'm not sure I entirely believed
it the first time you said it, and I'm considerably less sure now."

I do not have a ready response to that, mostly because I have spent
six years cultivating the specific cold I run on purpose as armor
against exactly this kind of observation, and it has never once, until
a man with grafting scars on both thumbs started noticing things about
me I hadn't authorized anyone to notice, failed to do its job properly.

We walk the rest of the rise not quite touching, on purpose, in the
specific way that not-touching, between two people who have just
established, plainly, that touching is available to either of them,
does considerably more work than touching outright would have managed.
I am aware of the space between our hands the entire remaining climb, a
few careful inches that feel, absurdly, more charged than the moment
his hand actually closed on my arm.

"Tell me something," I say, at the top of the rise, mostly to give my
own mind somewhere else to sit besides the inches between our hands.
"You've never once, in a fortnight, asked me anything about my own
work that wasn't strictly practical. Why not?"

"I've been waiting for you to offer it," Aldric says. "You strike me as
someone who answers questions she's asked considerably worse than she
answers ones she's decided, herself, to volunteer."

I consider this, and find, turning it over, that it is entirely
accurate — a fact about myself that I have never once had articulated
back to me so plainly, by anyone, in the whole of my adult life.

"Ask me something, then," I say. "Properly. I'll tell you if I'm
willing to answer it."

He thinks about this for longer than I expect, the particular careful
consideration of a man choosing his first proper question with the
weight of knowing he may not get many more chances at one.

"What does it feel like," he asks, finally, "setting a ward. Not the
cost of it — I've felt enough of that secondhand now to have some
notion. What does it feel like to you, doing the actual work. Is there
anything in it that isn't only cost?"

It is, I realize, turning the question over, precisely the right one to
ask — the one nobody, in six years, has ever thought to ask me,
because everyone I have ever dealt with professionally has been so
occupied with the price of the rite that no one has once wondered
whether the woman paying it might also, somewhere underneath the
paying, find something in the work itself worth having.

"There's a moment," I say, slowly, working the description out as I
go, the way he worked out his own description of the bond's ache a few
days ago, "just before the stone takes anything at all, where I can
feel exactly how much charge is already sitting in it — how close to
failing, how much longer it might hold on its own, if I simply walked
away and let it fend for itself. And there's a specific satisfaction,
every single time, in knowing precisely how much to give it. Not more
than it needs. Not less. Exactly the measure that keeps it standing
without wasting anything." I look at him, surprised at my own honesty.
"I don't think I've ever said that out loud to anyone. It sounds
smaller, said aloud, than it feels doing it."

"It doesn't sound small to me," Aldric says. "It sounds like the one
part of this whole arrangement that's actually yours. Not the crown's.
Not the rite's. Yours."

I do not have an answer for that which doesn't risk saying considerably
more than the top of a frost-slicked rise has any business hearing, so
I simply nod, once, and we stand there a while longer in the cold,
not quite touching, both of us apparently in agreement, without either
having said so, that the moment is worth more left exactly as it is
than pushed toward anything further.

·

Tomas notices the change before either of us has said anything to
anyone. He notices most things before either of us says anything,
which I have come to understand is simply the particular talent of a
nineteen-year-old who has spent three years learning to read a woman
who does not volunteer information easily.

"You came back from the Long Reach inspection in a considerably better
mood than you left in," he observes, over the evening's reports,
entirely too casually for a boy who has clearly been waiting all day
for the opportunity to say so.

"The inspection went well," I say.

"I'm sure it did," Tomas says, with a specific dry precision that tells
me he has drawn his own, considerably more accurate conclusions, and
has decided, for tonight, to be merciful about voicing them directly.

I let him have his private theory. I have, after all, very little
standing left to argue against it.

·

I go, later that same evening, to the small archive Highstone keeps
of every wardwright's personal correspondence — not the official rite
records, which the crown's office maintains its own copies of, but the
considerably less formal collection of letters and notes that
successive wardwrights have left behind, on the theory that the
private observations of a career sometimes hold more use than the
official reports ever manage to.

I am looking, specifically, for anything Ossian might have written
about sensation, about the bond's own behavior beyond the standard
cost-accounting, on the chance that his six decades of practice
uncovered something the crown's current archive has since misplaced or
never properly recorded.

I find, instead, something I was not looking for at all: a letter,
tucked between two considerably drier inspection reports, written in
Ossian's own economical hand, addressed to no one in particular, dated
some eleven years before his death.

*I have started, this winter,* it reads, *to wonder whether the cost I
take from this line is entirely the line's to take. There are nights I
give more than the rite requires, quietly, because the alternative is
watching a cairn fail that I could have held with a little more of
myself spent on it. I have not told my own apprentice this. I do not
know how to tell her, without frightening her, that the arithmetic she
believes she is learning from me is considerably more generous, in
practice, than the arithmetic I officially teach.*

I sit with that letter for a long while, in the archive's particular
hush, understanding, reading it, precisely what Ossian was admitting to
eleven years before his own death — the same habit Isla has since
confessed to me, quietly, over these past weeks. He knew. He knew, and
he never told her, and he died, in the end, having taught her, by
example rather than by word, the exact same silent over-spending that
is now, I fear, quietly killing her the same way it eventually killed
him.

I copy the letter carefully into my own hand before returning the
original to its place in the archive, uncertain, folding my copy away,
whether I will ever show it to Isla, or whether some truths are better
left where a person finds them herself, in her own time, rather than
handed over before she's ready to receive them.

Tomas finds me still sitting in the archive an hour later, and asks,
gently, what I've found.

"A pattern," I say, "older than either of us knew to look for."

He doesn't press further, which I am grateful for, and I walk home that
night carrying a considerably heavier certainty than I arrived with —
that whatever Isla and I are building together, we are, in some
fundamental sense, also fighting against a habit that has already
claimed one wardwright's life without either of us fully realizing,
until this exact evening, how deep its roots actually run.

·

I mention the archive letter to Sela the following day, in careful
general terms, unwilling to share Ossian's own private confession
without more certainty about whether that's mine to share at all, and
find her considerably more troubled by the general shape of it than I
expected.

"You're describing our own father," she says, quietly, once I've laid
out the pattern — a person who spends themselves silently, dresses the
spending up as duty, never once lets the people who love them properly
see the cost. "That's exactly what he did, all those years, and none
of us ever called it what it actually was until it was too late to
matter."

"I know," I say. "I think that's rather why it unsettled me so
thoroughly, finding it in someone else's history. It's not simply a
pattern I recognize professionally. It's the whole shape of how I was
raised to handle any difficulty at all."

"Do you think Isla can actually break it?" Sela asks. "The pattern, I
mean. Not simply notice it in herself. Actually change how she
operates."

"I don't know," I admit. "I think she's trying, harder than I've ever
seen anyone try at anything. I think the trying itself might matter
more than whether either of us ever fully succeeds."

Sela considers this, turning her own cup slowly on the grain-house
table.

"I'd like you to try too," she says, finally, plainly. "Not just notice
the pattern in someone else. Actually break it, in yourself, properly.
I've watched you do this exact thing our whole lives, Aldric, and I'm
tired of pretending I haven't."

I don't have a ready defense against being called out so precisely by
my own sister, so I simply sit with the observation, letting it settle
alongside everything else this whole strange season is teaching me
about a habit I am only now, slowly, properly beginning to recognize as
worth the considerable effort of unlearning.

·

I find Isla in the record-room, later that same day, and tell her,
plainly, about Sela's own observation — the specific pattern our father
handed down, the exact shape of the habit I am only now beginning to
properly examine.

"I think I understand something now," I say, "that I didn't understand
this morning. I've been treating my own silence as a kind of gift I'm
giving people — sparing them the burden of my difficulties. Sela's
helped me see it's closer to a withholding. I'm not sparing anyone
anything. I'm simply deciding, alone, what they're allowed to help
carry."

Isla listens to the whole of it without interrupting, the particular
attentive stillness she gives anything that costs a person to say.

"That's a considerable thing to recognize about yourself," she says,
finally, "in a single afternoon."

"I had help recognizing it," I say. "Sela mostly. You too, if I'm
honest, simply by being the kind of person who insists on the whole
truth rather than the managed version."

"I'm glad," Isla says, "that you're willing to actually look at it,
rather than simply noting the observation and continuing on exactly as
before. I've watched a great many people nod along to exactly this
kind of insight and change nothing whatsoever about their actual
behavior."

"I don't want to be one of those people," I say. "Not anymore. Not with
you specifically."

She takes my hand, and we sit together a while longer in the
record-room's quiet, and I understand, holding it, that recognizing a
lifelong habit is only the first, considerably smaller half of the
work — the harder half, the part that actually matters, is choosing,
every single day going forward, to practice something different.

·

Tomas catches me on my way out of the record-room that same evening,
holding a small stack of his own notes with the particular careful
posture of someone who's been waiting for the right moment to raise
something.

"Can I ask you something," he says, "about the archive letter Isla
mentioned. The pattern you found."

"Go on."

"Do you think it's only wardwrights who inherit it," he asks, "or do
you think it's simply how most people learn to handle difficulty,
regardless of what work they eventually end up doing?"

I consider the question seriously, surprised, as I often am, by how
much weight Tomas gives to things well beyond his years.

"I think it's considerably more common than either of us would like,"
I say. "I think most people are taught, one way or another, that
managing a difficulty alone is more responsible than sharing it. I
don't think it's unique to this line, or this family. I think it's
simply more visible here, because the costs are so much larger and so
much easier to trace back to a single decision."

"I don't want to learn it," Tomas says, with a seriousness that
surprises me. "The pattern. I've watched what it's done to you, and to
Isla, and I don't want to spend my own eventual career quietly
managing everything alone because it seemed like the responsible
thing to do at nineteen."

"Then don't," I say. "I think that's rather the whole point of
watching us fail at unlearning it properly — so you have the chance to
simply learn it differently from the start, rather than spending years
undoing a habit before you can finally set it down."

Tomas nods, considering this with real gravity, and I find myself,
watching him, grateful for a young man determined to learn from our
mistakes rather than simply repeat them out of habit or admiration.

"Thank you," he says, "for being honest about it, rather than letting
me assume the two of you have this all figured out already."

"We don't," I say. "I don't think anyone does, entirely. I think the
honest answer is that we're all simply doing our best to notice the
pattern before it costs us something we can't get back."

We part for the evening on that thought, and I walk home considerably
more grateful than usual for an apprentice wise enough to want a
different inheritance than the one this line has, until now, quietly
handed down.

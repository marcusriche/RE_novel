## Chapter Twenty-Five — First Intimacy (Isla)

Nothing urgent happens, the following evening, deliberately, by an
agreement neither of us states outright but both of us seem to arrive
at simultaneously — no ledgers, no cairns, no Feld, no numb hand
mentioned once, an evening spent entirely on nothing that requires
either of us to manage anything at all.

Aldric names the fire's shape after a grafting season, an old habit
I've stopped finding strange and started finding, instead, like a
private language I am slowly, carefully, learning to properly read.

"Tell me something that isn't about cost," I say. "Any of it. Something
that's simply yours."

He tells me about the first tree he ever grafted, badly, at eleven
years old, and how his father let it grow crooked rather than cut it
down, on the theory that a mistake you own outright is worth more than
a straight line you never earned. I recognize the phrase — he's used a
version of it before, about the tree near the orchard's northern wall —
and understand, hearing the origin of it, exactly how deep the habit of
quietly absorbing cost runs in him, taught early, by a father who
meant it kindly and could not have known how thoroughly the lesson
would take root.

I fall asleep on the cottage's good chair, that night, for the first
time in longer than I can place, without running a single column of
arithmetic first — simply tired, in the ordinary way, rather than the
specific hollowed exhaustion I have carried since I was twenty-six.

·

The following night is the one neither of us names in advance, though
both of us understand, from the particular quality of the quiet between
us, exactly what it is.

Everything that happens has already been asked for, plainly, in words,
days before, at the cairn field, and again tonight, in the cottage's
own low firelight, one final confirmation offered and received before
either of us moves any closer.

"Still yes," Aldric says, quietly, at the doorway to my own room, which
has become, in these past weeks, considerably less exclusively mine
than it once was.

"Still yes," I say back, and mean it entirely, and let him close the
distance this time, since I closed it first at the cairn field, and it
seems, somehow, correct that the asking and the choosing should take
turns between us rather than always falling to the same hand.

I will not set down, in any ledger, the whole of what passes between us
that night. Some things are not meant for accounting. I will say only
that everything arrives chosen rather than compelled — nothing
borrowed from the bond, nothing bright and involuntary the way the
bond's own sensations sometimes arrive; the bond, for once, stays
entirely quiet and lets the moment belong to the two of us alone,
uncontested by anything the rite ever demanded of either of us.

Aldric is careful with me in the specific way of a man who has spent
this whole arrangement learning that care and cost are not, in fact,
the same instrument, however much his own life has taught him to treat
them as interchangeable. I am careful with him in turn, in the specific
way of a woman learning, for the first time in six years, that
tenderness offered is not automatically tenderness spent at a loss.

Afterward, his good hand finds mine in the dark, and neither of us says
anything further, because for the first time in either of our lives,
nothing needs saying to be true. It is not a cure for anything — not
his hand, not the line, not the whole unresolved arithmetic of what the
rite is still, patiently, taking from both of us. It was never meant to
be a cure. It is, simply, ours: the one thing in this entire strange
season that belongs to no ledger, no crown, no rite-keeper's record at
all.

I lie awake a while afterward, listening to him breathe, steady and
warm beside me, and think, for the first time since my mentor's death,
that I have found something in this arrangement worth considerably
more than the six-year arithmetic I have spent my whole career
performing — not a solution to the cost, but a reason, entirely apart
from any ledger, to keep paying it.

·

Tomas, the following morning, is considerably more transparent about
his own theories than tact would generally recommend.

"You're different today," he announces, over the morning reports,
watching me with the particular scrutiny of a young man conducting his
own private investigation. "Not bad different. Just — different."

"I don't know what you mean."

"I mean," Tomas says, entirely too pleased with himself, "that you
hummed something, walking down the stairs this morning. I have never,
in three years, heard you hum anything at all, and I would like it
noted, for the historical record, that today is the day it finally
happened."

"I don't hum, Tomas."

"You did this morning," he says, with enormous satisfaction. "I'm
choosing not to speculate further about the cause, out of respect for
your privacy, though I want it understood that the restraint I'm
currently exercising is considerable, and should be appreciated
accordingly."

"Your restraint is noted," I say, dryly, "and appreciated exactly as
much as it deserves to be."

"That's very little appreciation."

"It's precisely proportionate," I say, and he laughs, properly,
delighted, and goes back to his reports, and I find myself, watching
him, grateful for a young man whose particular blend of nosiness and
genuine affection has become, somewhere in these past weeks, one of the
steadier comforts of my daily life at Highstone.

Aldric arrives partway through the morning's business, and Tomas, upon
seeing him, makes an elaborate show of suddenly finding his own reports
fascinating, glancing up only once, briefly, to catch my eye with a
look of such theatrical innocence that I have to turn away to keep from
laughing outright in front of him.

"What was that about," Aldric asks, once Tomas has gone to deliver the
morning's inspection notes.

"Tomas has apparently decided I hummed on the stairs this morning," I
say, "and is choosing, with enormous visible effort, not to comment
further on the implications."

"Did you hum?"

"I don't remember doing it," I say, "which I suppose means it's
entirely possible I did."

Aldric smiles at that, the specific unguarded smile I've come to
recognize as entirely his own rather than any managed version of it,
and I find myself, watching him, considerably more certain than I was
even yesterday that whatever this arrangement has become, it is worth
every uncounted cost it has already taken from both of us, and every
one still waiting to be paid.

·

I write to Fenhollow's nearest surviving settlement, that same
afternoon, on the small chance that whoever last had contact with the
lost village before it fell past the line might have preserved some
memory or record worth pursuing, once the current crisis has properly
settled. It is not urgent. I tell myself this several times, drafting
the letter, understanding, even as I write it, that I am doing the
thing Ossian's own journal described so plainly — reaching for a
problem I can solve at a comfortable distance, rather than sitting
properly with the one currently closest to me.

Aldric finds me at the letter, later, and reads it over my shoulder
without asking, the way he's started doing with most of my
correspondence these past weeks.

"You're worrying about a village that's been lost for a decade," he
observes, gently, "the same week you're worrying about me."

"I'm aware," I admit. "I think it's easier, somehow. Fenhollow's loss
already happened. There's nothing left to lose there, only history to
recover. You're still an open question, and I find open questions
considerably harder to sit with than settled ones, however grim."

"That's honest," Aldric says, "and I appreciate the honesty. But I'd
rather have your attention on the open question, if you're able to
spare it. Fenhollow's waited a decade. It can wait a little longer."

I fold the letter, unfinished, and set it aside, understanding that
he's right, and that I have, once again, caught myself reaching for
the safer, more distant problem rather than properly sitting with the
frightening, immediate one directly in front of me.

"Thank you," I say, "for noticing."

"I'm getting good at it," Aldric says, with a small, wry smile, "since
apparently we're both determined to keep catching each other at
exactly this particular habit, all winter long."

·

Sela visits later that same week, ostensibly to check on the garden's
progress, and finds the three of us — Isla, Tomas, and myself —
properly absorbed in the day's small domestic business, considerably
more relaxed than she seems to have expected.

"You all look remarkably settled," she observes, "for a household
currently facing down an unproven rite and a crown deadline."

"We've decided," Isla says, "that facing it down doesn't require
facing it grimly, every single hour. Some hours, we simply garden."

"That's a healthier philosophy than most of what I've watched this
family practice, historically," Sela says, with a small, private smile
that suggests she's including herself in that assessment. "I approve.
I'd like to join the gardening hours, if there's room for one more."

"There's always room," I say, and mean it, and the four of us spend
the rest of that particular afternoon in the small salvaged garden,
finding, in the ordinary work of tending something small and living,
a considerable measure of peace that none of us quite expected this
particular winter to offer.

"I want to say something," Sela says, later, once Tomas has gone back
to Highstone and it's just the three of us remaining. "I've watched
this family — the two of you, and now Tomas too, in his own way —
build something out of this crisis that I don't think any of you
expected to find in the middle of it. I'm proud of all of you. I
wanted that said properly, out loud, rather than simply felt privately
and never spoken."

None of us have a ready response to that, so we simply sit together in
the garden's quiet a while longer, considerably more grateful, all of
us, for the specific found family this strange winter has assembled
around us.

·

Aldric finds me alone in the cottage that evening, once Sela and Tomas
have both gone their separate ways, and settles beside me at the
kitchen table with the particular unhurried quiet of a man who has
nowhere else he'd rather be.

"You've been thinking about something all afternoon," he observes.
"You went quiet, twice, in the garden, in a way that wasn't simply
enjoying the work."

"I've been thinking about my mother," I admit, surprised, saying it,
by how readily the admission arrives. "Watching Sela today, fussing
over the herbs the way she fusses over everyone she loves, I kept
thinking about a blue shawl I haven't properly thought about in years."

"Tell me," Aldric says, simply, and I do — the ordinary, domestic
shape of her, dead when I was seven, a woman who baked bread and wore
a blue shawl and was, by every account I've ever gathered since, no
wardwright at all, simply someone's mother, loved plainly, missed
plainly, the specific ordinary grief of losing a parent young rather
than any larger, rite-shaped tragedy.

"I don't think I've ever told anyone the shawl detail," I say, once
I've finished. "I'm not certain why I'm telling you now."

"Because you finally have someone worth telling," Aldric says, "the
same reason I finally showed you the crooked tree."

"I think that's exactly it," I say, and let him hold me a while
longer in the kitchen's low light, grateful for an evening spent
tending nothing more urgent than a small, ordinary grief finally given
proper room to breathe.

·

Tomas finds the two of us there, later, having come by with a final
report he could easily have left until morning, and I understand,
watching him hesitate at the door, that he's noticed the particular
quality of the evening and is deciding whether to intrude on it.

"I can come back," he offers.

"Stay," I say. "We were only talking about my mother. There's room for
one more."

He settles in, cautious at first, and I find myself, without quite
deciding to, telling him the same fragments I've just given Aldric —
the shawl, the bread, the voice I can no longer properly recall.

"I didn't know you had a mother," Tomas says, then catches himself.
"That's a foolish thing to say. Of course you had a mother. I mean I
didn't know you'd lost her so young."

"I don't talk about her," I say. "I'm not entirely sure why tonight's
different."

"Maybe because you finally have people worth telling," Tomas says,
echoing, without knowing it, almost exactly what Aldric said to me
not an hour before.

"That's precisely what he said," I tell him, and Tomas looks pleased
in the specific unguarded way of a young man discovering he's arrived,
independently, at the same wisdom as someone he admires.

We sit together a while longer, the three of us, and I find myself,
watching the fire burn low, grateful for a found family willing to
hold even the smallest, oldest griefs alongside the larger, more urgent
ones this whole winter has demanded of all of us.

·

Tomas asks, before he leaves that evening, whether I'd be willing to
tell him more, someday, about the years between my mother's death and
my apprenticeship under Ossian — the gap neither of us has ever
properly discussed.

"There isn't much to tell," I admit. "A relative's household, mostly,
that took me in out of obligation rather than genuine warmth. I don't
think about those years often. I've found it easier not to."

"You don't have to tell me now," Tomas says, gently. "I only wanted
you to know I'd like to hear it, eventually, whenever you're ready.
The same way you've made room to hear mine."

"I appreciate that," I say, surprised by how much the offer settles
something in my chest. "I think I will, eventually. Not tonight. But
eventually."

"Eventually is enough," Tomas says, echoing, without quite realizing
it, the exact phrase Aldric's used with me more than once this season,
and I find myself, watching him gather his things for the walk home,
grateful for an apprentice patient enough to leave a door open rather
than push it, trusting that I'll walk through it in my own time.

·

Aldric asks, once Tomas has gone, whether the gap years are something
he might eventually hear about too.

"You already know most of it, I think," I say. "The relative's
household. The obligation rather than warmth. I don't think there's a
version of the story that surprises you, hearing it properly."

"I'd still like to hear it properly," Aldric says, "whenever you're
ready, the same as I'd like Tomas to hear it eventually. Not because I
expect it to surprise me. Because I think some things deserve to be
said aloud, regardless of whether the listener already suspects the
shape of them."

"That's a fair point," I say, and find myself, considering it, closer
to ready than I expected to be. "Perhaps not tonight. But soon."

"Soon is enough," Aldric says, and we sit together by the fire a while
longer, both of us patient with the pace of a story that's taken
considerably longer to tell than the years it actually covers.

·

I find myself, that same night, turning over exactly why the telling
feels so much less frightening than it did even a month ago — not
because the years themselves have changed, but because I've finally
let someone stand close enough to hear them without flinching.

"You're thinking loudly again," Aldric observes, watching my face in
the firelight.

"I'm thinking about how much easier this all feels, lately," I admit.
"Telling things. I used to rehearse every difficult sentence for days
before I could manage to say it aloud. I don't think I rehearsed
tonight's conversation with Tomas at all."

"That's progress," Aldric says, "considerable progress, actually,
though I understand if it doesn't feel that dramatic from the inside."

"It doesn't," I admit, "though I believe you that it is."

"I'll keep believing it for both of us," Aldric says, "until you catch
up to believing it yourself," and we sit together a while longer,
grateful for the specific quiet ease that's grown between us where
frightened rehearsal used to live.

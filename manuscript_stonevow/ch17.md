## Chapter Seventeen — Held Breath (Aldric)

The cup slips before I've fully registered that my hand has stopped
answering properly.

It is an ordinary evening, dinner at the cottage table, nothing about
the moment marked out as significant until the ordinary weight of a
half-full cup proves, suddenly and without warning, more than my left
hand is currently willing to hold, and it falls, and breaks against the
hearthstone, and both of us go very still, watching the pieces settle.

"Cold," I say, before Isla can ask, flexing the hand experimentally,
testing a fact I already know and am not yet ready to say the whole of
out loud. "Just cold. It happens, in this weather."

She doesn't push, not immediately. She also doesn't look away, and I
feel, in the specific quality of her not looking away, that she has
filed the moment permanently, under things she intends to return to
properly, later, once she's decided how best to ask.

"It's nothing the rite doesn't already account for," I say, which is,
I understand even as I'm saying it, the first outright untruth I have
told her since the binding.

I have told her partial truths before. I have withheld things,
carefully, the way I withhold most things I've decided cost more to
share than to carry alone. But this is different — a specific, deliberate
falsehood, offered to protect her from an arithmetic I have not yet
finished doing myself, and I feel it land wrong even as it leaves my
mouth, a debt I have just taken on that I did not have before tonight.

Isla lets the untruth stand, for now, though I watch her decide to let
it stand rather than simply fail to notice it, which is its own kind of
mercy — the specific patience of a woman who has learned, over these
past weeks, that pushing too hard too early only teaches a person to
guard the next admission more carefully.

We clean up the broken cup together, in a silence that has gone
considerably heavier than the ordinary quiet we've built between us
these past weeks, and I lie awake that night beside the memory of a
stone taking heat out through my own palms at the Long Reach cairn,
doing arithmetic I do not like the shape of, on exactly how much longer
two fingers can go properly numb before the numbness stops being a
minor inconvenience and starts being something considerably more
difficult to hide.

I do not tell Isla the whole of it. Not tonight. I tell myself this is
mercy, sparing her a fact she cannot yet do anything useful with,
though I suspect, lying awake beside the specific unbearable weight of
my own silence, that it is closer to the same flaw she named in me at
Hollow Farm — paying a cost alone, in the dark, and calling the
aloneness discipline rather than what it actually is, which is fear,
dressed up carefully enough that even I have trouble recognizing it
some nights.

I feel her, beside me, not sleeping either, and understand, from the
particular quality of her stillness, that she has not fully believed my
untruth even as she let it stand. She is building, I suspect, a
different theory of her own, quietly, in the dark, the same careful way
I built my own theory about her silence at Hollow Farm.

Neither of us says anything further, that night. But I understand,
lying there with a hand gone half-numb and a lie sitting unaddressed
between us, that the arithmetic I've been managing alone has just
started, for the first time, to run considerably faster than either of
us has properly accounted for.

·

I go to the orchard the next morning, alone, before Isla wakes, and
find Sela already at work grafting a late frost-guard onto the
southernmost row, her breath visible in the cold, her hands moving with
the specific practiced economy of a woman who has done this exact task
every year since our father taught her.

"You look terrible," she says, by way of greeting, not looking up from
the graft.

"Thank you."

"I mean it as concern, not insult," Sela says. "What's happened?"

I show her the hand, because I have decided, walking up here, that
keeping this from her too is no longer a debt I'm willing to take on,
not after everything Isla and I have promised each other about honest
counting.

Sela sets down her grafting knife entirely, and looks at the hand for a
long moment, and I watch her do the same arithmetic Isla did, arriving,
I suspect, at roughly the same frightening conclusion.

"Does Isla know the whole of it?" she asks.

"Most of it. Not quite the whole of it. I told a partial truth last
night that I already regret."

"Aldric." My name, from Sela, in exactly the tone our father used to
use when either of us was about to make a mistake he could see coming
from considerably further away than we could.

"I know," I say. "I'm going to correct it. I wanted to see the hand
properly in daylight first, away from her, before I decided exactly how
much to say."

"That's not honesty," Sela says. "That's simply managing the timing of
the honesty, which is the same habit dressed in slightly better
clothes."

I don't have an argument against that, mostly because I understand,
hearing it from her so plainly, that she's entirely correct, and that I
have spent the whole walk up here constructing a justification for
exactly the behavior I've promised to stop.

"You have to tell her everything," Sela says, gentler now, "and you
have to do it today, not once you've found the version that costs you
least to admit. I watched our father die of a fever he never once
properly named until it was too late for anyone to help him manage it
honestly. I am not going to watch that happen to you too, over
something considerably more preventable."

"I hear you," I say, and mean it, and walk back down from the orchard
that same morning with my sister's words settling considerably more
heavily than the cold ever manages to, understanding, by the time I
reach the cottage, that Sela is right, and that whatever I say to Isla
today needs to be the whole truth, offered plainly, rather than another
carefully managed piece of it.

·

I find Isla at the kitchen table, already dressed for the day's
inspections, and I understand, standing in the doorway with the whole
truth finally gathered and ready to be handed over, exactly how much
courage this particular ordinary morning is going to require.

"I need to tell you something properly," I say, "the whole of it, not
the version I gave you last night."

She sets down her cup, giving me her full attention, the specific
stillness she reserves for things she's already guessed matter more
than their opening suggests.

"The hand's worse than I said," I tell her. "Three fingers now, not
two. It's spread since the cup fell, not stayed the same. I told you
it was nothing the rite doesn't already account for. That wasn't true.
I don't know what the rite accounts for. I only know what I'm watching
happen to my own hand, and I was frightened enough of what that meant
that I made myself believe a smaller version of it might be easier for
both of us to carry."

Isla is quiet for a long moment, absorbing the correction.

"Thank you," she says, finally, "for coming back to tell me properly,
rather than letting the smaller version stand."

"I nearly didn't," I admit. "Sela had to say it to me plainly before I
found the nerve."

"I'm glad she did," Isla says. "I'd rather have an honesty that took
your sister's insistence to arrive than none at all. But I need you to
try, going forward, to find that nerve yourself, before someone else
has to hand it to you."

"I'm trying," I say. "I don't think I'm very good at it yet."

"None of us are very good at anything, the first several times we
attempt it properly," Isla says. "I don't expect you to be perfect at
honesty overnight. I expect you to keep coming back and correcting it,
the way you just did, every single time you catch yourself managing
instead of telling."

"I can do that," I say, and mean it, and she comes around the table and
holds me for a long moment, and I understand, standing there, that this
particular correction — small, difficult, entirely necessary — has cost
me considerably less than the silence it replaced, and given back,
somehow, considerably more.

·

I go to the orchard that same afternoon, needing, I think, to properly
close the loop with Sela, having promised her I'd correct the
management before the day was out.

"I told her the whole truth," I say, finding her at the grain-house.
"Not the version I gave her first, or the smaller version I nearly gave
Isla again this morning. The actual whole of it."

"How did she take it?"

"About as well as could be expected, given the actual news. She's
frightened. So am I, if I'm honest. But she knows now, properly, rather
than managing a smaller version of the fear on my behalf."

Sela considers this, nodding slowly, and I watch her decide something,
visibly, before she speaks again.

"I want you to know," she says, "that I'm proud of you. Not for the
hand, obviously — I wish to every power that watches this line that
none of this were happening at all. I'm proud of the correcting. That's
not nothing, Aldric, undoing a lifetime of habit in the space of a
single morning, even imperfectly."

"It doesn't feel like much, from the inside."

"Most genuine progress doesn't," Sela says, "while you're in the
middle of making it. It generally only looks like progress afterward,
once you can see the whole distance covered." She reaches over and
squeezes my good hand. "Keep correcting it. Every time you catch
yourself managing instead of telling. That's the whole of what any of
us can actually do, most days."

I walk back down from the orchard considerably steadied by her words,
carrying them alongside everything else this whole strange season is
teaching me about the slow, imperfect, entirely necessary work of
becoming someone who tells the truth before being caught in a lie
rather than after.

·

Tomas notices the broken cup's absence from the shelf that evening,
having apparently catalogued the cottage's small inventory of dishware
closely enough to register a gap, and asks about it with the
particular careless directness of someone who hasn't yet learned to
read the room before speaking.

"Another cup gone," he says. "That's the second one this month. Are
you simply clumsy, or is something else breaking them?"

I catch Isla's eye across the table, and understand, in the brief,
wordless exchange that passes between us, that we've both arrived at
the same decision simultaneously: that Tomas, however young, deserves
better than a deflection.

"It's my hand," I say, plainly, and watch him absorb it, his own
careless question giving way, almost immediately, to a much more
serious attention. "It's been going numb. Two fingers, entirely.
Considerably more of it too weak to trust with anything fragile."

"Since when," Tomas asks, all trace of his earlier carelessness gone.

"A while," I admit. "Longer than I told Isla, at first. I'm working on
correcting that particular habit."

Tomas is quiet a moment, processing this with a gravity that reminds
me, not for the first time, exactly how much this crisis has already
asked of someone barely out of boyhood.

"Thank you for telling me," he says, finally. "I know you didn't have
to. I'd rather know, even if it's frightening, than keep assuming
you're simply clumsy with cups."

"You're allowed to be frightened by it," Isla says, gently. "We are."

"I know," Tomas says. "I think I'd rather be frightened properly, with
both of you, than be kept comfortably in the dark about something this
significant." He looks between the two of us. "Is there anything I can
do? Properly, I mean, not simply asking to be polite."

"Keep drilling the cadence," I say, before Isla can answer. "If it
comes to needing it, I'd rather it be needed by someone properly
prepared."

Tomas nods, absorbing the request with the same seriousness he's given
everything else tonight, and the three of us finish the evening's meal
together in a quiet that feels, for the first time, considerably more
honest than comfortable — which I am slowly learning, this whole
strange season, matters rather more than the alternative.

·

Isla finds me at the woodpile the following morning, considerably
earlier than my usual hour, working through the last of the previous
day's split logs with more force than the task strictly requires.

"You're up early," she observes.

"I couldn't sleep," I admit. "I kept thinking about Tomas's face last
night, hearing about the hand properly for the first time. I don't
think I'd fully reckoned with what it costs him, specifically, watching
two people he's come to love manage a crisis this large."

"He's stronger than either of us gives him credit for," Isla says,
"though I understand the impulse to protect him from it regardless."

"I know he's strong," I say. "I still wish, some mornings, that he
didn't have to be. Nineteen shouldn't require this particular kind of
strength."

Isla considers this, settling onto the woodpile's edge to watch me
work.

"I think that's rather the tragedy underneath all of this," she says.
"Not simply what the rite costs the wardwright and the hearth. What it
costs everyone standing close enough to love either of them properly."

"Do you think it's worth it," I ask, "all of it, given what it costs
the people around us too?"

"Yes," Isla says, without hesitation. "I don't think the alternative —
managing everything alone, in careful isolation, to spare the people
around us the cost of caring — actually spares anyone anything. I think
it simply delays the cost, and makes it lonelier when it finally
arrives."

I finish the last log, considering her words, and set the axe aside.

"I think you're right," I say, "though I suspect it'll take me longer
than one morning's conversation to fully believe it, the way you seem
to already."

"Take the time you need," Isla says. "I'm not in any hurry. I've got
considerably more mornings like this one to spend convincing you,
apparently, and I find I don't mind the work."

We walk back toward the cottage together, the day's ordinary business
waiting, and I find myself, watching the frost catch the light over
the orchard's bare rows, grateful for a woman patient enough to keep
making the same true argument until it finally, properly lands.

·

Sela finds me at the cottage later that morning, and I tell her, since
she'll hear it eventually regardless, about the hand's full progression
and Tomas's own frightened reaction the night before.

"He took it well, all things considered," I say.

"He's stronger than either of you gives him proper credit for," Sela
says, echoing, almost precisely, Isla's own words from the woodpile.
"I've watched him grow up faster than any nineteen-year-old should
have to, this winter. I think it's time both of you started trusting
that growth properly, rather than continuing to manage him as though
he were still the boy who first arrived here."

"You're right," I admit. "I think I keep protecting the memory of who
he was rather than properly seeing who he's actually become."

"That's an easy mistake to make," Sela says, gently, "with anyone you
love. I've made the same one with you, more than once." She squeezes my
good hand. "Let him be as strong as he's already proven himself to be.
He's earned that trust."

I sit with her words a while longer, grateful, once again, for a
sister who keeps handing me exactly the correction I need, plainly,
whenever I've drifted too far into managing the people I love instead
of properly trusting them.

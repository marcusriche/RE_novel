## Chapter Twenty-Eight — Matching Secrets (Aldric)

I ask her the plain question that night, turning the mutual rite over
in my head long after the cottage has gone quiet: if the wardwright
pays too, under the older form, what has Isla already been paying,
alone, that the record doesn't show.

I have been circling the question for two days now, ever since Tomas
first read the translated passage aloud in the record-room, unwilling
to ask it directly until I felt certain I could hear whatever answer
came without flinching.

It is, I realize, saying it, exactly the shape of question she asked me
at the Long Reach cairn, the night I finally showed her my hand. She is
owed the same answer she demanded of me, offered with the same
directness, in the same low firelight, at the same late hour when the
day's ordinary business has finally quieted enough to make room for it.

"Two years," Isla says, quietly, after a long silence, in the specific
tone of a woman finally setting down a weight she has carried so long
she has half forgotten it was ever meant to be shared. "I've been
feeding the failing wards my own heat, outside the rite proper, since
before Ossian's death made me the only practitioner left to do it.
It isn't in any ledger. I didn't want it to be."

I don't look surprised, hearing it, because I am not, in fact,
surprised — I have suspected some version of this since Hollow Farm,
since the byre thawed at a cost she refused to log anywhere.

"I wondered," I say. "You told me, the first week I knew you, that you
run cold on purpose. I understood it as a manner of speaking. I didn't
understand what it actually cost you until just now, hearing the whole
shape of it."

"It's not the same cost as yours," Isla says. "It's slower. Less
visible. I haven't lost feeling in anything, the way you have. But it's
been drawing on something all the same, for two years, and I don't
entirely know, any more than you know about your own hand, how much
longer I could have kept doing it before the arithmetic caught up with
me too."

"Why didn't you tell me," I ask, "once we'd agreed neither of us would
manage things quietly anymore?"

"Because I was going to," Isla says, "the same night you finally showed
me your hand. And then the hand was so much worse than either of us
expected, and I let the urgency of your secret excuse me from telling
mine, for a while longer than I should have. I'm telling you now
because I don't think either of us can afford, going forward, to let
one secret hide behind the other's urgency."

We sit with the matching shape of it a long while — two people who have
each spent themselves in silence and called it discipline, one for two
years, one considerably longer if you count the whole of his adult
life, each convinced, privately, that carrying the cost alone was the
kinder, more responsible choice.

"We're going to have to actually watch each other," I say, eventually.
"Properly. Not just feel what the bond hands us secondhand. Actually
ask, regularly, whether the other one is managing something quietly
again."

"I know," Isla says. "I'm not certain either of us is very good at
being watched that closely. I know I'm not."

"Neither am I," I admit. "But I'd rather be bad at it together than
good at hiding things separately. I've had considerable practice at the
second one. I don't think it's served either of us particularly well."

Isla laughs, once, low, surprised out of her the way my own laugh
surprised me the night of the kiss, and something in the room settles
— not resolved, not fixed, but shared, finally, in a way neither
secret managed to be on its own.

"For what it's worth," she says, "I don't think you're the only one
who's going to have to relearn how to do this. I've spent six years
believing that needing anyone was a debt I could never repay evenly. I
suspect unlearning that is going to take longer than one honest
conversation on a cold night."

"We have time," I say, and understand, even saying it, how much of a
hope that sentence currently is rather than a fact either of us can
properly guarantee — but I say it anyway, because some hopes are worth
stating plainly, even uncertain, especially to someone who has spent
her whole life refusing herself the comfort of stating them at all.

·

I go to see Cera Voss myself, the following day, curious what she might
tell me that she hasn't already told Isla, and find the healer
considerably more direct with me than I expected, given we've barely
exchanged a dozen words in the whole time I've lived at Highstone.

"You should know," Cera says, without preamble, examining my hand with
the specific brisk competence of a woman who has done this exact
examination more times than either of us would like to count, "that
what's happening to you isn't unique. I've seen it twice before, in
wardwrights' hearths, over the course of my own career. Neither
survived the season."

I absorb that plainly, without flinching, because I have decided,
these past weeks, that flinching from hard truths costs more,
eventually, than simply hearing them properly the first time.

"What happened to them," I ask. "The other two."

"One refused to say anything, the way you very nearly did, until it was
too late for the wardwright involved to do anything useful with the
knowledge. The other told his wardwright plainly, the way you've
finally started doing, but neither of them had anywhere further to
take that honesty. There was nothing left to try beyond what the
standard rite already offered, and the standard rite simply couldn't
save him in time."

"So being honest early actually matters," I say. "Even without knowing
yet what it buys us."

"It's the only thing that gives you any chance at all," Cera agrees,
"whatever that chance eventually turns out to be worth. I can't promise
you honesty solves anything on its own. I can tell you that neither of
the two men I lost ever got the chance to find out, because neither of
them said anything soon enough for it to matter."

"I understand," I say, and thank her, and walk back toward the cottage
turning her words over — the specific weight of being told, plainly,
that simply being honest early enough might be the only thing that
gives me any chance at all, whatever shape that chance eventually takes.

I do not tell Isla about this particular conversation immediately. I
tell myself it's because there's nothing new in it, strictly, beyond
what we already knew. I understand, walking home, that it is closer to
not wanting to hand her one more frightening fact before she's had time
to properly rest from the last one — a small, well-intentioned
management I catch myself doing and decide, before I reach the cottage
door, to correct before the evening's out, rather than let it become
another quiet debt.

·

I tell her about the visit that same evening, exactly as I resolved to,
and watch her absorb it with the particular stillness she reserves for
information she's already half-expecting.

"Two others," she says, quietly. "That you know of."

"That Cera knows of, in her own career. There may be more, further
back, that neither of us has any record of at all."

"I want to know their names," Isla says, "if Cera has them. Not for any
practical reason. I think they deserve to be remembered properly,
rather than simply referenced as a statistic in a healer's professional
accounting."

I find, hearing that request, that it does not surprise me in the
slightest — precisely the kind of instinct I have come to expect from
a woman who copied her own mentor's ledger into her own hand rather
than let his memory go unwritten.

"I'll ask her," I say. "I think you're right that they deserve it."

We sit together a while longer, the weight of two unnamed predecessors
settling into the cottage alongside everything else this winter has
handed us to carry, and I find myself, watching Isla's face in the
firelight, grateful, even now, even frightened, for a woman who insists
on properly naming every single cost this whole arrangement extracts,
rather than letting any of it pass unmarked and unmourned.

·

Cera provides the names within the week, delivered by Tomas, who
apparently offered to fetch them himself rather than let either of us
make the trip specifically for something this heavy.

*Corwin Vane and Petra Ash,* the note reads, in Cera's own brisk hand.
*Corwin's wardwright was his own sister, bound by circumstance rather
than the usual rite-office process — a small mercy the crown permitted,
in those days, before the current procedures tightened. Petra's
partner was named Iven Hallow, a stonemason by trade, who told her
everything, plainly, and still couldn't save him in time.*

I read the names aloud to Isla, that evening, watching her absorb each
one properly, the way she absorbs every fact she considers worth
remembering.

"Corwin Vane," she repeats, quietly. "Petra Ash. Iven Hallow." She
writes them into her own private notebook, alongside Ossian's name and
her mother's, a small, growing record of everyone this rite has cost,
properly named rather than left as statistics.

"Thank you," I say, "for insisting on this. I don't think I'd have
thought to ask, myself."

"Someone should remember them properly," Isla says. "Whatever else
happens with the mutual form, whatever it eventually costs either of
us, I want it understood that these names mattered, and that we
carried them forward, rather than simply building on their loss without
ever once looking back to see what it actually was."

·

I find myself thinking, that same night, about my own father's name,
and whether it belongs in Isla's small, growing record alongside
Corwin Vane and Petra Ash and Iven Hallow — not a wardwright's cost,
exactly, but a cost all the same, paid quietly, over years, by a man
who never once let either of his children see the toll his own
careful management was taking.

"I've been thinking about my father," I tell her, later, once the
names have been properly written down and the notebook set aside for
the evening.

"What about him?"

"Whether he belongs in your record," I say. "Not the same kind of
cost. But a cost, all the same. He spent himself managing our whole
childhood alone, the same way I've spent myself managing the exemption
and the rite and everything else. I don't think anyone ever properly
named what it cost him, doing that."

Isla considers this seriously, the way she considers most things I
bring to her that matter.

"I think he belongs somewhere," she says, "though perhaps not in the
same record as the others. Theirs is a record of the rite's own
particular cruelty. His is something else — a record of what a whole
culture of careful, silent management costs a family, generation over
generation, until someone finally decides to do it differently."

"That's a fair distinction," I say. "I think I'd like to write it down
somewhere, regardless. Properly. Not simply carry it in my own head the
way I've carried everything else."

"Then write it," Isla says. "I'll help, if you want company doing it."

I do write it, that same evening, a short account of my father's own
quiet cost, the specific weight of a man who loved his children
considerably more than he ever let himself show it, and I find,
finishing the account, a measure of the same lightness Isla described
feeling after copying Ossian's ledger into her own hand — not simply
a chore completed, something closer to a proper goodbye, offered late
but offered honestly, to a man who never got the chance to hear it
while he was still here to receive it.

"Thank you for helping me find the words," I say, once it's finished.

"Thank you for finally looking for them," Isla says, "instead of
letting the whole account go unwritten, the way I very nearly let
Ossian's go unwritten myself."

·

Sela reads the account, a few days later, at my own invitation, and
sits with it a long while in the grain-house's quiet before saying
anything at all.

"You wrote this properly," she says, finally. "Not the tidy version we
usually tell each other about him. The actual shape of it."

"I wanted it honest," I say. "I think we've spent our whole lives
telling each other a kinder, simpler version of Father than the one
either of us actually lived through. I didn't want to keep doing that,
writing this down."

"I'm glad you didn't," Sela says. "I've been carrying my own version
of him for years, and I don't think I've ever properly compared it to
anyone else's. This one's closer to what I remember, underneath the
stories we tell company."

"Do you want a copy," I ask, "for yourself?"

"I'd like that," Sela says, "very much," and I promise to make her one,
and we sit together a while longer in the grain-house's quiet, two
siblings finally examining a shared grief honestly, together, rather
than each carrying a separately managed version of the same man,
alone, the way both of us learned to do from watching him do exactly
that his whole life.

·

"Can I ask you something else," Sela says, once the account's been
properly set aside, "while we're being honest about him."

"Go on."

"Do you think he'd have approved," she asks, "of what you're doing
now. The mutual rite. Spending yourself openly, alongside a wardwright,
rather than managing everything quietly the way he taught us both to."

I consider the question properly, turning it over with more care than
my first instinct offers.

"I don't know," I admit. "Part of me thinks he'd have been frightened
for me, the way he was frightened of anything that looked like an
unnecessary risk. Another part of me thinks he might have recognized,
watching it, exactly what he never let himself have — someone to share
the weight with, rather than simply provide for from a careful
distance."

"I think the second part is more likely," Sela says, "though I
understand why the first part frightens you more."

"Why do you think that," I ask.

"Because if he'd have approved," Sela says, "it means he knew, all
along, that there was a better way to carry difficulty than the way he
chose. And I think that's a harder thing to sit with than simply
believing he did his best with what he had available to him."

I don't have a ready response to being understood quite that precisely
by my own sister, so I simply sit with her words a while longer, the
whole complicated shape of our father's legacy settling into something
I can finally examine honestly, rather than only mourn from a
respectful distance.

·

I tell Isla about the conversation that same evening, curious what
she'll make of Sela's own sharp observation.

"She's right, I think," Isla says, once I've finished. "Approval and
recognition aren't quite the same comfort. I understand why the second
one frightens you more."

"It shouldn't, logically," I say. "If he'd have approved, that's a
kinder reading of him, not a harsher one."

"Kinder readings of the dead are sometimes harder to sit with than
harsh ones," Isla says, "because they mean the person was capable of
more than they ever let themselves have. That's a grief of its own,
separate from simply missing someone."

"I hadn't thought of it that way," I admit, "but I think you're
right, again, about something I hadn't properly examined myself."

"I've had a great deal of practice this winter," Isla says, "examining
exactly this kind of grief," and we sit together a while longer,
turning over the specific complicated comfort of understanding a dead
man better than either of us understood him while he was still here to
be understood.

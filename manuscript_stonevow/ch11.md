## Chapter Eleven — Aftermath, Underlit (Aldric)

We shelter below the Long Reach cairn, in the lee of a shepherd's stone
hut that has stood on this stretch of the line longer than either of
our own families, until I can stand without the ground doing something
unreliable underneath me.

The whole world has gone strange and underlit around the edges, the
particular hollowed clarity that follows a cost this size, and I find
myself grateful, sitting in the hut's small shelter, simply for the
solid, unremarkable fact of stone at my back and Isla's coat still
warm across my shoulders.

Isla's coat is over my shoulders, which is, absurdly, the warmer of the
two, a fact neither of us comments on directly, though I feel her
notice it and file it away, the way she files everything she isn't
ready to spend yet.

"It's always like that," I say, finally, into the quiet, because I have
decided, somewhere in the walk down from the cairn, that she has earned
the whole truth rather than the managed version I've been handing her
in pieces. "Not always that bad. But always something. Every ward,
every inspection where you've had me near enough to feel it secondhand
— there's always a cost, and it's always been considerably more than
I've let on."

"Why didn't you tell me," Isla asks, not accusing, simply asking.

"Because knowing wouldn't have changed the numbers," I say. "You still
had forty miles of cairns to hold, with or without my honesty about
what holding them costs me specifically. I didn't see the use in
handing you a fact you couldn't do anything about, except worry."

"It changes what I'm willing to ask of you," Isla says, and there is
something in her voice, saying it, that surprises me more than it
surprises her — a specific weight, the sound of a woman discovering,
in real time, that a fact she'd filed under *unchangeable* has just
become, instead, something she has actual choices about.

"How do you mean," I ask.

"I mean I've been setting wards at whatever pace the line requires,
without once asking whether that pace is sustainable for the hearth
actually feeding them. I assumed — because it was easier to assume than
to ask — that the cost was fixed, regardless of frequency. I understand
now that it isn't. That every ward I set with you standing at the stone
is a specific, countable withdrawal, and that I have some say, however
limited by the line's own demands, in how quickly I make those
withdrawals."

I don't argue with that. I don't entirely agree with it either — the
line's demands are the line's demands, regardless of what either of us
would prefer, and I have never once, in a lifetime of watching my own
family absorb costs quietly, believed that acknowledging a cost changes
its actual size. But I don't say so, tonight, because I understand,
watching her work through the realization, that this is the first time
in six years anyone has let her believe her own choices might matter to
the arithmetic at all, and I am not willing to be the one who takes
that belief away from her on its very first evening.

"Thank you," I say instead, "for finishing the ward properly, rather
than stopping when it started costing more than either of us
expected."

"I nearly didn't," Isla admits. "I want you to know that. I very
nearly broke it mid-cast, watching what it was doing to you."

"I'm glad you didn't," I say. "An unfinished ward costs worse, in the
long run, than a finished one. I'd rather pay the whole price once than
half of it twice."

She looks at me for a long moment, in the specific underlit quiet of a
shepherd's hut with no fire and only the last grey light of an early
winter afternoon finding its way through the gaps in the stone, and I
understand, from the look, that she is doing the same arithmetic I've
been doing since the byre at Hollow Farm — recognizing, in me,
precisely the shape of her own habit, and finding, in the recognition,
something considerably more complicated than simple sympathy.

"You do that," she says, eventually. "Pay the whole price rather than
half of it twice. It's what you did with Sela's exemption. It's what
you did volunteering for a binding sight unseen. I don't think you've
ever once, in your whole life, let anyone else help you carry a cost
you'd already decided was yours alone to bear."

"No," I admit. "I don't think I have."

"I know the shape of that particular weight," Isla says, quietly. "I
carry it too. I have for six years."

Neither of us says the word that's sitting in the hut with us, small
and precise and considerably larger than either of us is prepared to
name outright tonight. It doesn't need saying yet to be true. I simply
lean, slightly, into the coat and the cold and the fact of her sitting
close enough to share both, and let the moment be exactly as large as
it actually is, without either of us trying to manage it into something
smaller.

## Tomas Watches

Tomas has been keeping his own private ledger of the two of us for the
better part of three weeks, and finally, over breakfast the morning
after the Long Reach inspection, decides to present his findings.

"You made him two mugs of tea before you made yourself one," he
announces, entirely too pleased with himself for a nineteen-year-old.
"I'm simply reporting the data."

"It's efficient," Isla says, with the specific dignity of a woman who
knows exactly how unconvincing that sounds even as she says it.

"Very efficient," Tomas agrees, in the tone of someone who has decided
not to argue with a losing position out loud, purely for the pleasure
of watching her hold it anyway.

I say nothing, mostly because I have learned, in three weeks of living
alongside the two of them, that the safest position in any exchange
between Isla and her apprentice is silence, offered from a safe
distance, while the two of them work out their own particular rhythm
without requiring my input.

"How's the hand," Tomas asks me, once Isla has gone to check the
morning's post, in a considerably more serious register than the one
he used a moment ago.

"Cold," I say, which is true, and considerably less than the whole of
what's true, in the same specific way that most of what I tell people
about my own condition tends to be.

Tomas looks at me for a long moment, the particular assessing look of a
young man who has learned, watching Isla, exactly how to read a person
who's decided not to volunteer the whole truth.

"You're doing the thing she does," he says, finally. "The not-quite-
lying thing. I've watched her do it for three years. I'd know it
anywhere."

I don't have a ready answer for being caught so precisely, so I simply
nod, once, an acknowledgment rather than a confession, and Tomas,
mercifully, lets the observation stand without pushing further, though
I understand, from the specific way he goes back to his reports, that
he has filed it, carefully, for whenever the moment finally arrives
that either of us runs out of room to keep managing it quietly.

·

I spend the rest of that morning helping Tomas catalogue the winter's
accumulated correspondence — a task neither of us particularly enjoys,
sorting the crown's routine bureaucratic notices from the handful of
letters that actually matter, and one that gives us both, I suspect,
considerably more time to talk than the actual work requires.

"Can I ask you something," Tomas says, partway through the second
stack, "about before. Before the binding, I mean. What Isla was like,
when it was just the two of you running the line."

I consider the question, and find myself, answering it, surprised by
how much has already shifted in the few short weeks since the rite.

"Quieter," I say. "Not that she isn't quiet now. But there was a
particular quality to it before — a kind of bracing, I think, against
having to explain herself to anyone. I don't see that as often now."

"I've noticed that too," Tomas says. "She laughs more. Actually laughs,
not just the polite version she used to manage for visitors." He
hesitates, then adds, careful, "I'm glad. I've watched her carry this
whole line practically alone since I was sixteen. I didn't think
anything could actually reach her, underneath all that carrying."

"I don't think I've reached the whole of it," I say, honestly. "I
think I've reached some of it. I suspect there's considerably more
underneath that neither of us has found yet."

"That's probably true of most people," Tomas says, with the specific
wisdom of a nineteen-year-old who has clearly given the matter more
thought than his age would suggest. "I don't think anyone reaches the
whole of another person. I think you just keep finding new rooms, as
long as the other person keeps letting you in."

I think about that observation for the rest of the afternoon, turning
it over the way I turn over most of what Tomas says that lands harder
than his age would predict, and find, walking home that evening, that
it has settled somewhere useful — a small, steadying thought to carry
alongside everything else I am slowly learning about the woman the rite
bound me to, one room at a time, as she continues, gradually, to let me
in.

·

I find myself, that same evening, doing something I have not done
since I was a boy — sitting down with a scrap of paper and actually
writing out, plainly, the things I am afraid of, rather than simply
letting them circle unexamined in my own head the way I've always
managed fear before.

I am afraid the rite will take more of me than either of us has
properly accounted for. I am afraid Isla will spend herself trying to
prevent that, the same silent self-spending she has already admitted to
once, at Hollow Farm, and has not fully stopped since. I am afraid,
underneath both of those fears, of a considerably smaller, more
selfish one: that whatever this has become between us will not survive
whatever the winter still has left to demand of either of us.

I do not show the list to anyone. I am not certain, writing it, that
showing it would help — some fears, I think, need simply to be named,
once, properly, in a person's own hand, before they can be managed at
all, regardless of who else eventually gets to see the naming.

I fold the paper carefully and tuck it into the same drawer where I
keep the small collection of things I've decided, this winter, are
worth keeping rather than discarding — a pressed leaf from the crooked
tree, a scrap of the rite-cord from the binding itself, saved without
quite deciding why at the time. I understand, adding the list of fears
to that particular collection, that I am building, slowly, a private
archive of my own — not the formal kind Isla keeps in the record-room,
something considerably smaller and more personal, a record of exactly
what this winter has actually cost and given me, in equal, complicated
measure.

·

Sela visits the cottage the following afternoon, ostensibly to discuss
a grafting question, actually, I suspect, simply curious enough about
how things are progressing to invent an excuse for the visit.

"You look well," she observes, studying me with the particular
scrutiny only an older sister can properly deploy. "Rested, even.
I don't think I've seen you properly rested since before Father died."

"I've been sleeping better," I admit. "I don't know that I'd have
predicted that, given everything else currently happening."

"Fear and rest aren't always opposites," Sela says. "Sometimes the
right kind of company makes even a frightening season easier to
actually sleep through, properly, rather than simply enduring it."

"Is that wisdom from experience, or simply a theory?"

"A bit of both," Sela says, with a small, private smile that tells me
there's more to the observation than she's currently willing to share.
"I'll tell you properly, someday, when it's relevant. For now, I'm
simply glad to see my brother sleeping properly again."

I don't push for the rest of whatever she's not saying, understanding,
from years of being her brother, that Sela shares things on her own
schedule and no one else's, and we spend the rest of the visit on the
actual grafting question, though I find myself, watching her go, newly
curious about whatever quiet happiness she's apparently been keeping to
herself.

·

I mention Sela's odd, private smile to Isla that same evening, curious
whether she's noticed anything similar in the weeks since they've
started properly getting to know each other.

"I've noticed something," Isla admits. "She mentioned, once, in
passing, wisdom gained from experience rather than theory. I didn't
press. I assumed it was hers to raise properly, when she was ready."

"That's rather generous of you, given how curious I know you actually
are."

"I've learned patience about other people's private doors," Isla says,
"mostly by watching how carefully you guard your own, and deciding I'd
rather earn the opening than force it."

"Fair," I say, considering this. "I think I'd like to ask her
properly, myself, at some point. Not tonight. But eventually."

"I think that's wise," Isla says. "I suspect she's been waiting a long
while for someone in this family to actually ask, rather than simply
notice and let the noticing go unspoken."

We sit together a while longer, turning over the small mystery of my
sister's guarded heart, and I find myself, thinking about it, grateful
for a season that has taught this whole family, slowly and imperfectly,
that the people who love you best are usually the ones most overdue
for being properly asked what they're actually carrying.

·

Tomas mentions Sela's small mystery himself, unprompted, a few days
later, having apparently noticed the same private smile I described to
Isla.

"Has anyone asked her about it," he says, careful, "or is everyone
simply waiting for her to volunteer it?"

"We're waiting," I admit. "I think it's hers to raise, in her own
time."

"That's probably wise," Tomas says, "though I confess I'm curious. I
don't think I've ever seen her look quite that private about anything
before."

"You've noticed her more closely than I expected," I observe.

"She's become rather important to me, this winter," Tomas admits,
somewhat shyly. "Not simply as your sister. As her own person. I think
I understand her considerably better than I did in the autumn, and I
find I rather like what I've learned."

"I think she'd be glad to hear that," I say, "whenever you find the
occasion to tell her directly."

"Maybe I will," Tomas says, considering it, and I find myself,
watching him turn the possibility over, grateful for the whole small
web of genuine affection that has grown, this winter, between people
who started the season as near-strangers to one another.

·

Isla mentions, that same evening, that Tomas has apparently developed
a genuine fondness for my sister, and I find the observation lands with
more warmth than surprise.

"I noticed something similar," I admit. "He speaks of her differently
than he speaks of anyone else at Highstone. More carefully, somehow.
More like someone he's decided matters."

"Does it worry you," Isla asks, "the age difference, or simply the
complexity of feelings tangled up in an already complicated family?"

"A little," I admit. "Mostly I find myself simply glad. Sela's spent
years guarding herself carefully. If Tomas's own quiet regard is any
part of what finally coaxes her guard down properly, I don't think I'm
inclined to worry about the particulars."

"That's a generous read," Isla says.

"I've had a good season for practicing generous reads," I say, and we
sit together a while longer, both of us quietly hopeful about a small,
tender thread growing between two people we've each come to love in
our own particular ways.

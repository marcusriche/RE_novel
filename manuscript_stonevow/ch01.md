## Chapter One — The Assembly (Isla)

I have worn the same coat to every binding since my mentor's, and I have
never once had it cleaned properly, on the theory — untested, unproven,
mine alone — that a coat that has already survived one funeral is luckier
than a coat that hasn't, and that luck of this particular kind is not
something a person ought to risk washing away.

It has not, so far, been especially lucky. It has simply been present,
which I have come to understand, over six years of sole practice on the
northern line, is most of what luck actually is.

I stand before the assembly at Highstone Cairn in that coat, in the
rite-hall's particular cold — a cold with weight to it, unlike the cold
outside, which merely arrives — and do not let my face do anything at
all. I have had a great deal of practice not letting my face do anything
at all. It is, along with the coat, one of the two pieces of equipment I
can be certain will still be working by the end of the evening.

Keeper Bram Ossory reads the rite in the old cadence, the one that has
not changed in longer than Highstone's own foundations, and I have heard
it enough times now — mine, my mentor's, the three before his that I
learned only as names in the record-room's ledger — that I could recite
it backward without once losing the rhythm.

A wardwright to shape the stone. A hearth to feed it. A name read aloud
at the binding, so the village knows, in advance, precisely whose
funeral to expect.

It is not a comfortable sentence to have memorized. I have made my peace
with that the way I have made my peace with most uncomfortable things:
by refusing to examine the peace too closely, in case it turns out, on
inspection, not to be peace at all.

The name Ossory reads is Aldric Marrow's.

I do not know him. I have seen him twice, both times at the Highstone
market, both times buying grafting wax in the specific quantity of a man
who intends to use every last measure of it himself rather than send a
boy to fetch it cheaper elsewhere. I know, from the ledger Sela Marrow
keeps for the crown's own tax rolls, that he is twenty-eight, that he
runs the last living orchard above the line, that he has a sister three
years his junior whose name does not, as of eighteen months ago, appear
on any exemption roll it should by rights still appear on.

I do the arithmetic on that absence in the half-second between hearing
his name and watching him step forward, the way I do arithmetic on
everything, out of a habit older than my apprenticeship and considerably
more reliable than hope.

He steps forward before Ossory has finished the sentence.

That is the detail I will turn over longest, afterward, lying awake in a
cottage that has not yet learned to hold two people's weight instead of
one. Not dragged. Not summoned by a name he had no say in. He simply
walks forward, unhurried, into the space the rite has left for whichever
hearth the crown's ledgers deemed suitable, and stands there with the
specific stillness of a man who decided this outcome for himself some
considerable time before the rite ever got around to confirming it.

I understand, watching his face — sun-marked despite the season, broad
through the shoulders in the particular way of men who have spent their
whole lives lifting things heavier than themselves and never once
complaining about the weight — that he volunteered. Not for the binding
generally. For me, specifically, sight unseen, on the strength of
whatever the crown's assessors told him about the northern line's
casualty rate and his own arithmetic about what his sister's name was
worth against it.

I have spent six years being the only wardwright standing between forty
miles of cairns and the Rime's own patient, directionless advance. I
have never once, in all that time, had anyone volunteer to stand beside
me in it. I do not know what to do with a man who has, except distrust
the timing of it entirely.

Ossory calls us both to the rite-stone at the hall's center — a slab of
the same grey, frost-veined granite as every standing cairn on the line,
smaller, worn smooth by six generations of hands exactly like the two
about to be placed on it — and binds our wrists together with the
rite-cord, an unremarkable length of grey wool that has, by the old
accounts, held through worse winters than this one intends to be.

The cord itself does nothing. I have been told this since I was
nineteen and believed it, on the evidence available, entirely.

What arrives instead, the moment the knot closes, is not touch and is
not quite anything I have a proper word for — a warmth, borrowed rather
than felt, arriving in my chest from a direction that has nothing to do
with the hall's own hearth, settling there with the specific unwelcome
permanence of a guest who has already decided he isn't leaving before
morning.

I do not let my face do anything about that either. It costs
considerably more than the usual stillness costs, and I pay it anyway,
because the alternative is letting an assembly of people I have spent
six years keeping at a careful distance watch me discover, in real time,
exactly how thoroughly the rite has just rearranged the furniture of my
own chest.

Aldric's hand, bound against mine, is warm in the specific way of a man
who has spent his whole life outdoors and never once resented the cold
for costing him that warmth back. Mine, against his, is cold enough that
I feel him notice it — a small, involuntary flinch, quickly stilled,
the reflex of someone raised to be careful with things he suspects might
be fragile.

I am not fragile. I have buried a mentor and kept forty-one cairns
standing with nothing but my own two hands and an apprentice too young
to be trusted with the worst of the work, and I have never once, in any
of that, been fragile.

I do not say any of this. I let Ossory finish the rite, and I let the
cord come away from both our wrists at the appointed line in the old
cadence, and I do the thing I am best at, which is standing perfectly
still while an entire hall of people who have just watched my life
change completely fail to notice that anything about my face has moved
at all.

·

Afterward, in the corridor outside the rite-hall, where the cold is
merely the ordinary kind and considerably easier to bear, Aldric finds
me before I have finished deciding whether to let myself be found.

"I know what I did," he says. Not an apology. A fact, offered plainly,
the way a man states the weight of a thing before handing it over so
the other person isn't caught off guard by the carrying of it. "I'd
rather you heard it from me than from the ledger."

"The ledger already told me," I say. "Sela Marrow's name. The exemption
roll it should still be on and isn't."

Something moves behind his face at that — not surprise, exactly.
Recognition, perhaps, that the woman he has just been bound to for the
rest of whatever this winter turns out to cost either of them reads
ledgers the way other people read weather, and draws the same plain,
unavoidable conclusions from both.

"She'd have died on the exemption roll instead of off it," he says.
"Two winters back, before the crown tightened the terms. I had a name
to spend and she didn't. It wasn't a large decision, in the end. It
just looked like one, from outside."

I do not tell him that I do not believe decisions of that particular
size are ever small from any angle, inside or out. I file the sentence
instead, the way I file everything I am not yet ready to spend, in the
part of my chest that has, until roughly four minutes ago, belonged
entirely to me.

"I run cold," I say, instead, because it is true, and because it is the
only warning I currently have the nerve to give a stranger the rite has
just made considerably less of a stranger than either of us planned
for. "Not as a manner of speaking. As an actual, professional fact.
I've spent six years learning not to need anyone to warm me, because
needing that from a hearth I'm also responsible for spending is a debt
I was never going to be able to pay back evenly."

"I know how to be patient with a debt," Aldric says. "It's the one
skill this whole business has actually taught me properly."

I do not have an answer to that which doesn't risk giving away
considerably more than an hour-old binding has any right to extract
from me, so I do not attempt one. I simply nod, once, and let him
understand, from the nod, that the conversation is over for tonight,
and walk back out into a cold that has, for the first time in six
years, an actual second weight standing somewhere inside it alongside
my own.

·

I do not sleep, that first night, though not for any of the reasons I
had braced myself to lie awake over.

I lie instead running the same cold arithmetic I have run every night
since my mentor walked into the cold and left me the only wardwright
standing on forty miles of failing line — the cairns, the record book,
the specific number of scenes I have left before the season's demands
outstrip whatever I currently have left to give it — except tonight
there is a new variable sitting in the sum that refuses, however many
times I run the numbers, to behave like the rest of the variables I am
used to managing.

He decided this. Before he knew my name, before he knew what the
binding would actually feel like arriving, he decided that whatever it
cost him was worth less to him than what it would buy his sister.

I have spent six years believing, professionally, that the rite's whole
architecture runs on cost taken from one hearth and paid, silently, into
stone. I have never once, until an hour ago, felt what that cost
actually resembles arriving in a body I can put a face to.

I do not sleep. I lie in the specific dark of a cottage that has, as of
this evening, quietly and irreversibly stopped being only mine, and I
let myself, for exactly as long as it takes the fire to burn down to
its last coals, wonder what it might cost me to let that be something
other than a debt.

·

I had not expected, walking back from the rite-hall alone, to be
stopped at Highstone's own gate by Reny Ashe, who has run the winter
market stall nearest the gatehouse for as long as I have been
wardwright and considerably longer than that besides, and who has
never once, in all my years passing that stall, addressed a single
word to me that wasn't strictly transactional.

"They bound you to the Marrow boy," she says, not quite a question,
wrapping a twist of dried herbs I have not asked for and setting it in
my hand regardless.

"They did."

"Good," Reny says, with the specific finality of a woman who has
already decided the matter and is not currently interested in
discussing the reasoning behind it. "He's spent six years driving that
cart of his down from the last orchard in every kind of weather,
without once complaining to a soul about the cold it costs him to do
it. I'd rather the rite bind you to a man who already knows how to
carry a burden without setting it down on someone else's account."

"You know him better than I expected."

"Everyone at this gate knows him," Reny says. "You're the one who's
kept yourself too occupied holding up forty miles of stone to notice
the people walking past it." She says this without unkindness, though
it lands, regardless, considerably harder than she likely intends it
to. "That's not a criticism, Wardwright. It's an observation. You've
earned the right to be occupied. I only mean to say — you might find,
now that the rite's gone and noticed for you, that there was rather
more standing around you than you'd accounted for."

I do not have a ready answer to that, and Reny, apparently satisfied
with having said her piece, turns back to her stall without waiting for
one, leaving me standing at the gate with a twist of herbs I did not
ask for and a sentence I turn over the whole rest of the walk home,
unable to decide whether it unsettles me more for what it says about
Aldric or for what it says about the six years I have spent, by Reny's
own accounting, failing to notice anyone at all.

The herbs, I discover later, are for sleep. I do not use them that
first night. I lie awake instead with the fire burning down and Reny's
observation keeping company with everything else I am not yet ready to
examine, and find, somewhere before the coals go entirely cold, that I
am considerably less alone in this village than six years of careful,
solitary practice had ever let me believe.

·

Highstone Cairn itself, in the days before the binding, is not a place
I have ever properly described to anyone, in the sense of setting down
whole what it actually looks like rather than simply moving through it
the way you move through your own hands — familiar past the point of
noticing.

It sits at the northern edge of the last properly warm valley before
the ground begins its long climb toward the line, a squat, grey
fortification of the same frost-veined granite as every standing cairn
on the whole forty miles, built, according to the earliest records I
have ever managed to find, by wardwrights considerably more numerous
than the single practitioner it currently houses. There were, once, a
dozen of us working this line together, the record shows, back before
the crown's own arithmetic decided a smaller staff cost less to fund,
regardless of what the smaller staff actually cost the line itself.

I think about that history often, walking the keep's own corridors —
stone worn smooth by considerably more feet than currently walk it,
rooms clearly built for staff the crown no longer sees fit to maintain,
a whole institution slowly, quietly, shrinking around the single
wardwright it insists is sufficient. I have never once, until this
particular week, properly considered what it might mean to be handed,
by the same crown that shrank this institution down to one, a second
hearth to share the weight of it.

The rite-hall itself, where the binding took place, was clearly built
for considerably larger assemblies than the modest gathering that
witnessed mine — a vaulted ceremonial space with room, easily, for
several hundred, currently hosting perhaps forty on any given binding,
the crown's own patronage having thinned alongside its funding. I stood
at that rite-stone, the night of my own binding, keenly aware of all
the empty space around the small crowd that had gathered, a physical
reminder of exactly how much this whole institution has shrunk in the
years since it was first built to hold considerably more than it
currently does.

I fall asleep, eventually, that first night, still turning over the
whole shape of Highstone's own diminished grandeur, and dream, briefly,
of a rite-hall properly full again, though I could not, waking, say
whether the dream was memory, hope, or simply the particular kind of
wishful thinking that arrives easiest in the last hour before dawn.

·

I wake at my usual hour regardless, the specific discipline of six
years refusing to yield even to a night this strange, and go through
the whole of my ordinary morning routine with the deliberate care of a
woman trying to prove to herself that nothing has actually changed —
checking the previous night's inspection log, laying out the day's
priorities, making tea I mostly forget to drink.

It is only standing at the washbasin, catching my own reflection in
the small mirror I've owned since apprenticeship, that the whole of it
properly arrives. I look, if I am honest, exactly as I did yesterday —
the same weathered face, the same hair bound back hard against the
cold, the same permanent half-pallor that six years of underfed winters
has left on me. And yet I find myself examining the reflection for
some visible sign of the change, as though a binding this significant
ought to have left a mark I could actually point to, rather than simply
sitting, invisible, somewhere behind my own ribs.

I think, briefly, of what my mother might have said, seeing this
particular morning — a wardwright bound, at last, to someone, standing
at a washbasin looking for evidence of a change no mirror could ever
properly show. I decide, drying my face, that she would likely have
told me to stop looking in mirrors for answers that only time is
actually going to provide, and go down to the record-room to begin the
day's work, carrying the whole unresolved shape of last night's binding
quietly alongside me, the way I carry most things I haven't yet learned
what to do with.

·

Tomas is already at the record-room table when I arrive, considerably
earlier than his usual hour, and I understand, from the specific
careful way he doesn't quite look at me directly, that he has spent at
least part of the night composing whatever question he's currently
working up the nerve to ask.

"You don't have to say anything," he says, finally, "if you'd rather
not. I only wanted you to know I'm glad it's him. Whoever the rite
could have named, I mean. I've delivered enough reports up to the
Marrow orchard, these past three years, to know he's decent in a way
that isn't performed for an audience."

"Thank you, Tomas," I say, surprised by how much the plain sentiment
actually settles something in my chest that I hadn't realized was
still unsettled.

"I also wanted to say," he continues, working up to something else
entirely, "that whatever this means for the apprenticeship — for me,
specifically — I'd rather you tell me plainly, whenever there's
anything to tell, than have me work it out secondhand from whatever
the village happens to be saying at market."

"There's nothing to tell yet," I say. "The rite bound us last night.
I don't know myself, this morning, what any of it actually means
beyond the binding's own bare requirements."

"That's fair," Tomas says, accepting this with more grace than his
nineteen years would suggest he owes anyone. "I only wanted it said
plainly, since you've spent three years teaching me that plain things
said early cost considerably less than the same things discovered
late."

I look at him properly, understanding, not for the first time, exactly
how much of my own careful discipline he has already absorbed simply
through years of proximity, and find myself, this particular morning,
grateful for an apprentice sharp enough to ask the honest question
rather than simply speculate quietly the way the rest of the village
undoubtedly already is.

"I'll tell you," I say, "as things become clear. I promise you that
much, at least."

"That's all I'm asking," Tomas says, and goes back to the morning's
reports, and I find myself, watching him work, considerably steadied
by the specific, ordinary comfort of a routine that has, at least this
morning, remained exactly as it was the day before.

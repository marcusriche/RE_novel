## Chapter Four — First Frost, First Crack (Isla)

A routine inspection, nothing more, the sort of errand I have run
perhaps four hundred times in the six years since I became the
northern line's sole practitioner: walk to the nearest standing cairn,
lay a hand against the stone, read the charge it's holding, note the
reading in the ledger, walk home, exactly as unremarkable as every one
of the four hundred inspections that came before it.

The morning holds the particular stillness of first frost properly
arriving, the whole valley gone quiet and white overnight, and I find
myself grateful, walking out to the waystone, for an errand ordinary
enough to require no thought at all beyond simply placing one foot
after the other.

Aldric comes with me because the rite pulls at him when I go too far
from the line without him, an ache he describes badly and does not
complain about, in the specific tone of a man reporting a fact about
the weather rather than a grievance about his own body.

"It's not pain, exactly," he says, on the walk out, working the
description the way he works most things that cost him — carefully,
without asking anyone to make it smaller than it is. "More like a
room you've left, and can't stop feeling the shape of, from a distance."

"That's a good description," I say, meaning it, and surprised, hearing
myself say it, by how rarely I offer that particular compliment to
anyone.

"I've had a fortnight to work on it," Aldric says. "You've had six
years to get used to reading stone. I've had a fortnight to get used
to being read by one, secondhand, through you. I thought the least I
could do was describe it properly, since you're the one who has to
make sense of whatever I hand you."

We reach the nearest standing cairn — not the Long Reach, a smaller
waystone closer to Highstone, unremarkable except for the fact that it
has stood, uninterrupted, for longer than either of our own lifetimes
combined — and I lay my palm flat against the frost-veined granite, the
way I have laid a hand against stone perhaps four hundred times, to
read what it's holding and how much longer it intends to hold it.

What arrives, this time, is not the stone's charge at all.

It arrives sideways, uninvited, the way the warmth arrived at the
binding — except larger now, specific in a way that terrifies me before
I've even fully identified what I'm feeling: a cold ache, precise,
located in the smallest two fingers of a hand that is not, currently,
touching any stone at all. Aldric's hand. His left. A cold that has
nothing to do with the winter air around either of us and everything to
do with whatever the rite has quietly begun doing to him, day by day,
simply for standing near enough to a wardwright doing her ordinary work.

I take my hand off the stone as though it has burned me, though nothing
about the granite has changed temperature at all.

"You felt that," Aldric says. Not a question. He has gone very still,
in the specific way of a man who has just watched someone else confirm
a fact he had hoped, however faintly, might not be a fact at all.

"I felt something," I say, which is the closest to honest I have
managed with anyone in longer than I can accurately place, and
considerably less than the whole truth, which is that I felt his cold
hand arrive in my own chest as plainly as if it had been my own hand
going numb.

"How much," he asks. "Of what I feel. How much of it actually reaches
you."

I do not have a precise answer. I have never once, in six years of
professional practice, encountered a description in any text —
Ossian's own careful hand included — of a bond doing what this one has
just done, unbidden, without either of us calling for it, without any
rite requiring it of either of us at all.

"I don't know yet," I say, honestly. "More than I expected. More,
I think, than either of us bargained for, standing at that binding a
fortnight ago believing the cord did nothing once the knot closed."

Aldric flexes the hand in question, experimentally, the way you test a
fact you're not yet ready to fully believe, and I watch him do it with
the specific attention of a woman cataloguing, for the first time,
exactly how much of another person's cost she is now going to be able
to feel arriving, whether or not either of them has decided, yet, what
to do with that particular unasked-for gift.

"Does it frighten you," he asks. "Feeling it."

"Yes," I say, because it is true, and because I have decided, somewhere
in the last fortnight, that lying to this particular man costs more
than it's worth. "I've spent six years believing, professionally, in
the price the rite extracts. I've never once, until just now, felt it
happen to someone whose face I can put a name to while it's happening."

"I'm sorry," Aldric says, "that knowing costs you something too. I
didn't account for that, volunteering. I only ever did the arithmetic
on what it would cost me."

"You couldn't have accounted for it," I say. "Nobody's bound a hearth
to a wardwright who feels this precisely in over a century, as far as
the record shows. We're both finding out what this actually is at the
same time."

We stand at the waystone a while longer, neither of us in any
particular hurry to walk back toward whatever the cottage has quietly
become in the fortnight since the binding, and I find myself doing the
thing I did on the road below Highstone — running the cold list,
silently, item by item — except this time the list has stopped being
useful in the way lists are usually useful to me. It keeps arriving,
every single time, at the same unhelpful conclusion: that whatever this
is, arrangement or accident or something the old rite never properly
accounted for, it has already, in a fortnight, stopped behaving like
paperwork.

"We should walk back," I say, eventually, because someone has to say
it, and because standing at a waystone doing arithmetic that refuses to
resolve is not, strictly, useful to either of us.

"We should," Aldric agrees, and does not move for another moment
regardless, and neither, for that same moment, do I.

Neither of us names what just happened, on the walk back, in either
direction. But I feel it settle into the shape of a fact between us —
solid, load-bearing, the kind of thing I suspect I will find out, much
later, I have been leaning my whole weight against since the exact
second his cold hand arrived in my own chest without either of us
asking it to.

The cottage, when we reach it, has two fires lit rather than one, which
neither of us discusses, and two chairs pulled to the hearth rather
than the single one that has stood there alone for six years, which
neither of us discusses either, and I go to bed that night having felt,
for the first time since my mentor died and left me the whole of the
northern line's arithmetic to carry by myself, something arrive in my
own chest that was never, strictly, mine to begin with, and finding
that I do not, on reflection, especially want to give it back.

·

I write to the crown's rite-office the following morning, a formal
inquiry regarding any prior record of a bond exhibiting involuntary
sensation transfer beyond the standard cost-accounting the rite
requires, phrased in the careful, bloodless language the office
prefers, and receive, within the week, a reply that tells me
considerably more by what it doesn't say than by what it does.

*No such record exists in the current archive,* the reply states,
in a hand I don't recognize, signed only with an official seal rather
than a name. *Should further instances arise, the office would welcome
notification for the purposes of the historical record.*

I read the reply three times, looking for the specific bureaucratic
evasion I suspect is buried somewhere in its careful phrasing, and
find, on the third read, the detail that unsettles me most: *the
current archive*. Not *no such record has ever existed*. Simply that
none exists in whatever portion of the archive the crown's office
currently maintains — a phrasing that leaves open, deliberately or
carelessly, the possibility that older records, kept elsewhere, by
different hands, might tell a considerably different story.

I do not mention the letter to Aldric immediately. I tell myself this
is because there is nothing conclusive to report, though I understand,
turning the reply over at my own kitchen table, that it is closer to
the same habit I am only beginning to recognize in myself: the
instinct to hold uncertain, potentially frightening information alone
until I have made proper sense of it, rather than sharing the
uncertainty itself and letting someone else help carry the weight of
not knowing.

Tomas finds the letter on the table that evening and reads it before
I can stop him, apprentice's privilege, three years deep.

"You're not going to tell him," Tomas says, not quite a question.

"Not yet," I say. "There's nothing conclusive in it."

"There's something in it," Tomas says. "I don't know exactly what.
But I know you well enough to see you turning it over, and I'd rather
you turned it over with someone than alone at this table at midnight,
same as always."

"I'm not turning it over alone," I say. "I'm turning it over with you,
apparently, given you've just read my private correspondence without
asking."

"That's not the same as him," Tomas says, gently, "and you know it
isn't."

I do know it isn't, and I do not have a ready defense against being
called out so precisely by a nineteen-year-old who has spent three
years learning to read exactly this kind of evasion in me, so I simply
fold the letter and set it aside, and resolve, without quite deciding
when, to mention it to Aldric properly, once I've found the words that
don't make the uncertainty sound larger than it currently is.

·

I use the delay, unproductively, to walk the northern stretch of the
line alone the following morning, checking three waystones that don't,
strictly, need checking yet, on the theory that physical activity might
settle whatever the letter has unsettled in me more efficiently than
simply sitting with it would.

It does not, particularly, settle anything. I find myself, instead,
doing the arithmetic I have avoided doing properly since the binding —
not the practical arithmetic of cairns and charges, the considerably
less comfortable arithmetic of exactly how much of my own guardedness
is genuine caution and how much is simply habit, worn so deep by six
years of solitary practice that I no longer properly distinguish
between the two.

I think about Ossian, walking the third waystone's perimeter, and the
particular way he never once, in seven years of apprenticeship, spoke
to me about anything beyond the rite's own mechanics. I understood
that, at nineteen, as simply how a wardwright's training properly
proceeded — focused, disciplined, uncluttered by anything personal. I
understand it now, considerably differently, as its own kind of
loneliness, handed down from teacher to student without either of them
ever examining whether it needed to be handed down at all.

I do not want to hand that particular inheritance to Tomas. I have
already, I realize, walking the last stretch back toward Highstone,
begun doing something rather different with him than Ossian ever did
with me — the plain questions about how he's actually doing, the
genuine interest in his own history rather than merely his technical
progress. I had not, until this exact moment, properly recognized that
shift as a deliberate choice rather than simply an accident of my own
different temperament.

I resolve, reaching the keep's own gate, to be considerably more
deliberate about it going forward — not simply training a wardwright,
the way Ossian trained me, but raising one, properly, with all the
attention and honesty that word actually implies.

·

I find Aldric at the cottage that evening considerably earlier than
usual, having apparently finished the day's orchard work faster than
planned specifically to have more time before dinner.

"You're early," I observe.

"I wanted to be," he says, simply, in the particular plain register
I've come to recognize as his most honest one. "I found myself, today,
looking forward to this part of the evening more than I've looked
forward to anything in longer than I can properly place, and I decided
there wasn't any good reason to make myself wait for it."

I don't have a ready response to being told, so plainly, that I've
become a thing someone looks forward to, so I busy myself instead with
the kettle, giving my hands something practical to do while I work out
what to say.

"I feel the same," I admit, finally, "though I don't think I'd have
found the nerve to say it first."

"I know," Aldric says, gently, no criticism in it at all. "I don't
think you need to find that nerve every time. I'm happy to go first,
as often as it takes, until saying things plainly starts feeling less
like jumping off something and more like simply talking."

"That's a generous offer."

"It's not entirely generosity," Aldric admits. "I find I rather enjoy
being the one who gets to say the true thing first, this time, after a
lifetime of being the one who never quite manages to ask for what he
actually wants."

We sit together over the kettle's slow boil, and I find myself,
watching him settle into the good chair with the specific ease of a man
who has decided, somewhere in these past days, that this cottage is
somewhere he belongs rather than merely somewhere the rite requires him
to be, considerably more certain than I was this morning that whatever
we are building together is worth every uncertain step it demands of
either of us.

·

I tell him about the rite-office letter that same evening, finally,
having decided over the kettle's slow boil that the delay has already
cost more than the telling ever would.

"I should have told you two days ago," I say, handing him the letter.
"I told myself there was nothing conclusive in it. I understand now
that wasn't the real reason I waited."

Aldric reads it carefully, the way he reads most things that matter,
and looks up once he's finished.

"What was the real reason," he asks, gently, no accusation in it at
all.

"I wanted to be the one who understood it first," I admit. "I've spent
six years being the person who understands things before anyone else
needs to worry about them. I think I wasn't ready to hand you an
uncertainty before I'd resolved it into something manageable."

"I'd rather have the uncertainty," Aldric says, echoing something he's
said to me before, though I notice he doesn't say it as a rebuke,
simply as a plain restatement of what we've already agreed to. "Shared
properly, even unresolved, rather than managed alone until it's tidy
enough to hand over."

"I know," I say. "I'm finding the agreement considerably easier to make
than to actually practice, apparently, on both our parts."

"Then we're evenly matched at failing it," Aldric says, with a small,
wry smile, "which I suppose is its own kind of progress."

"I'll try to tell you sooner, next time," I say. "The frightening
things. Before I've resolved them into something comfortable."

"I'll hold you to that," Aldric says, "the same way I hope you'll hold
me to it, whenever I inevitably do the same thing myself."

We sit together with the letter between us a while longer, and I find
myself, watching the kettle finally come to its proper boil, grateful
for a man who receives an admitted failure with patience rather than
disappointment, and considerably more willing, because of it, to keep
trying at the harder practice of sharing uncertainty properly, rather
than only the tidy conclusions it eventually resolves into.

## Chapter Fifteen — The Commissioner's Deadline (Isla)

Feld returns to Highstone with a second ledger, this one already half
written before he's even sat down, in a hand considerably less careful
than my own or Aldric's, the specific rushed economy of a man who has
already decided the outcome and is merely gathering the paperwork to
justify it.

I recognize the particular set of his shoulders before he's said a
single word, the same posture he wore delivering the first ultimatum,
and understand, watching him settle into the council room's hardest
chair, that today's visit is going to cost considerably more than the
last one did.

"One binding isn't holding forty miles, Wardwright," he says, without
preamble. "The crown wants a plan for the apprentice, in case the
current arrangement doesn't outlast the season."

Aldric is present for this meeting, for the first time, having come
down from the orchard on the pretext of grafting supplies neither of us
has bothered to examine too closely, and I feel, through the bond, the
specific cold that moves through him at the word *doesn't* — not fear,
precisely, something closer to a man hearing his own mortality
discussed as a line item by someone who has never once had to feel what
that line item actually costs a body to carry.

"The binding will outlast the season," I say, which is not, strictly, a
fact yet. It is a refusal, offered plainly, to hand Feld the
alternative he has come here angling for, the same refusal I've given
him before and intend to keep giving him for as long as I have any say
in the matter at all.

"I hope, for everyone's sake, that it does," Feld says, in the tone of
a man who does not, in fact, especially hope so, having already
budgeted for the alternative in whatever ledger he answers to further
up the crown's own chain. He gathers his papers, pauses at the door.
"You should know, Wardwright — the crown is not sentimental about
lines it's decided cost more than they return. I'd have you prepared
for that, regardless of how this particular winter resolves."

He leaves without further comment, and I stand in the council room a
long moment afterward, doing arithmetic I don't especially want to be
doing, on exactly how much time the crown's patience is likely to
extend before Feld's contingency plan for Tomas becomes, simply, the
plan.

"He's not wrong to be worried," Aldric says, once Feld's footsteps have
faded down the corridor. "Even if I don't care for how he's worried
about it."

I stand a moment longer at the council room's window, watching Feld's
retreating figure grow small against the winter light, and find myself
wondering, not for the first time, exactly what it costs a man to
spend a career treating people as figures on a ledger rather than
lives worth knowing properly.

"I know," I say. "That's rather what makes it difficult to hate him
properly. He's doing his job. His job simply happens to require
treating people like line items, and he's decided, somewhere in his own
career, that doing the job well means not examining that requirement
too closely."

"Are you frightened," Aldric asks, "of what happens if the binding
doesn't hold the way you're telling him it will?"

"Yes," I say, honestly, because I have decided that half-truths cost
more between us now than whole ones do. "I'm frightened for Tomas,
mostly. He's nineteen. He shouldn't be doing arithmetic about his own
mortality this early in a career he's barely begun."

"And for yourself?"

I consider the question properly, the way I've learned to consider most
of what he asks me plainly.

"Less than I was, before the binding," I admit. "I used to be
frightened, mostly, of running out before the line did — of the
arithmetic simply failing, one winter, with nobody left to notice until
it was too late to matter. I'm still frightened of that. But I'm
frightened now, also, of something considerably more specific, which is
losing you to an arithmetic neither of us built and neither of us fully
controls."

Aldric doesn't have an immediate answer to that, and I don't need one.
I simply let the fear sit between us, named rather than managed, the
way I am slowly, unevenly, learning to let most things sit rather than
filing them away where nobody has to look at them directly.

·

Ossory finds me alone in the record-room the next morning, before
Tomas has arrived for the day's reports, and I understand, from the
particular formality of his entrance, that this is not a casual visit.

"Feld came to see me as well," he says, without preamble. "After his
meeting with you. He wanted my assessment of whether the current
binding is likely to hold."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I don't make predictions about a rite I've watched hold and fail
in equal measure over four decades of practice," Ossory says. "Which is
true, and which also bought you rather more time than a direct answer
would have."

I look at him properly, surprised by the specific generosity of the
evasion. Ossory has never, in my six years of dealing with him, gone out
of his way to protect my standing with the crown's own office.

"Why," I ask, plainly.

He considers the question for a long moment, the particular gravity of
a man deciding how much of his own reasoning to share.

"Because I watched your mentor die completing a rite I have never
fully trusted," he says, finally. "I signed off on it, at the time,
because the record demanded a signature and there was no alternative
form on offer. I have wondered, every year since, whether there might
have been a better way, and never found the nerve to go looking for
one myself."

"You think there might be an older form," I say, carefully, testing
whether he's guessed at something I haven't yet told anyone outside
this cottage.

"I think," Ossory says, "that a rite this old, practiced this long,
rarely arrives at its current shape by accident. Corruption implies an
original. I don't know what that original looked like. I would be
considerably less surprised than Feld to learn that someone had found
evidence of it."

I do not tell him, that morning, about the record from Calder's Reach —
I have not yet made the journey, do not yet have the proof in hand —
but I file his words away carefully, understanding, walking back to the
cottage that evening, that I may have, in Keeper Bram Ossory, an ally
considerably more willing to hear an unorthodox truth than his careful,
formal manner has ever let on.

·

I tell Aldric about the conversation that evening, curious what he'll
make of Ossory's unexpected openness.

"That's not the Ossory I've encountered at village councils," Aldric
says. "The one I've met is considerably more concerned with proper
procedure than with entertaining unorthodox theories about corrupted
rites."

"I think there are two versions of him," I say, considering it
properly. "The one who performs the formal role — careful, procedural,
unwilling to be seen taking risks in front of the crown's own
representatives. And another one, underneath that, who's spent four
decades watching this line fail people and has never quite made his
peace with it."

"Which version do you think we'll get, if it ever comes to actually
proposing something unorthodox to him formally?"

"I don't know," I admit. "I hope the second one. I suspect it depends
considerably on how much proof I can ever put in front of him, if
proof of anything is even out there to be found."

"And if it's the first version instead? The procedural one?"

"Then we'll need another approach," I say, "though I'm not yet certain
what that would look like, or even what, precisely, I'd be proposing.
I don't have anything yet beyond a suspicion and a dead man's private
doubt."

Aldric is quiet for a moment, turning the whole conversation over.

"For what it's worth," he says, "I think you've already started
building the kind of trust with him that might tip the scales toward
the second version, whenever the moment actually arrives. Today's
conversation wasn't nothing. I think he's already telling you,
carefully, that he wants to be convinced of something, even if neither
of you yet knows precisely what."

I think about that for the rest of the evening, turning Aldric's
observation over alongside Ossory's own careful words, and find myself,
for the first time since Feld's last visit, cautiously hopeful that
whatever this suspicion eventually turns out to mean, I may have more
allies within the crown's own formal process than I initially believed.

·

I sleep poorly that night regardless, my mind circling the whole
conversation with Ossory, turning over every careful word for
additional meaning, and wake before dawn to find Aldric already up,
sitting by the fire with two cups of tea he's clearly made some time
ago, one now gone properly cold.

"You couldn't sleep either," I observe.

"I woke and found you gone from the bed," he says, "and came to check.
Found you'd been asleep at the kitchen table the whole time, over the
crown's own correspondence, of all things."

"I don't remember falling asleep there."

"You looked exhausted enough that I didn't have the heart to wake
you properly," Aldric says. "I simply put a blanket over your
shoulders and let you rest, until the cold woke you on its own."

I look at him properly, understanding the specific, unglamorous
kindness of a man who chooses to let someone sleep rather than manage
their comfort more efficiently by waking them.

"Thank you," I say, "for that particular restraint."

"I'm developing quite a collection of small restraints, these days," he
says, with a dry humor that reminds me, pleasantly, of exactly how much
lighter he's become, in small ways, since the binding first brought us
together. "I intend to keep collecting them, if you'll have me."

"I'll have you," I say, and mean it entirely, and we drink our tea
together in the cold kitchen, the day's business waiting patiently for
whenever either of us feels ready to properly begin it.

·

Tomas arrives earlier than usual that same morning, catching us still
at the kitchen table, and hesitates at the door, visibly uncertain
whether he's interrupting something.

"You can come in," I say. "We're only drinking cold tea and putting
off Feld's correspondence."

"I heard he came by again," Tomas says, settling at the table with the
particular careful posture of someone bracing for bad news. "Sela
mentioned it, in passing, at the market yesterday. I didn't like to ask
her directly what he wanted."

"He wants a contingency plan," I say, honestly, because I have decided
Tomas deserves the plain account rather than a softened one, whatever
his age. "In case the current binding doesn't hold the season."

Tomas absorbs this with more steadiness than I expect, though I catch
the specific stillness underneath it, the particular effort of a young
man determined not to let his own fear show more than he can help.

"Is it going to come to that," he asks. "Me, I mean."

"I don't know," I say. "I'm doing everything I can to make certain it
doesn't. I want you to know that, plainly, rather than simply trusting
it without being told."

"I believe you," Tomas says. "I think I've believed it for a while now,
actually. I just wanted to hear it said directly, rather than gathered
secondhand from whatever Feld happens to let slip to people who aren't
even supposed to be listening."

"You'll always hear it directly from me," I say, "whatever it turns
out to be."

Tomas nods, absorbing this, and helps himself to what's left of the
now properly cold tea, and the three of us sit together a while longer
in the kitchen's early morning quiet, the day's harder business
waiting, patiently, for whenever any of us feel ready to face it.

·

Aldric walks Tomas partway to the record-room that same morning,
ostensibly to fetch a tool he's left there, actually, I suspect, to
have a private word out of my own hearing, and returns some while
later considerably more settled than he left.

"What did you say to him," I ask, once he's back.

"I told him I wasn't going to let Feld's contingency touch him," Aldric
says. "Not while I have breath left to prevent it. I know that's not
strictly my promise to make, given I'm not the wardwright. I wanted him
to hear it from me too, regardless."

"That's not nothing," I say, "hearing it from both of us."

"I don't think a nineteen-year-old should have to carry this kind of
fear on the strength of one adult's promise alone," Aldric says. "I
wanted him to have two people telling him the same true thing, in case
either one of us ever falls short of keeping it."

I take his hand, considering this, grateful for a man who has learned,
this whole difficult season, to extend to Tomas the same fierce
protectiveness he's always shown Sela, folding our young apprentice
properly into the family's own inner circle of people worth fighting
for.

"Thank you," I say, "for making sure he heard it twice."

"I'd make sure he heard it a hundred times, if that's what it took," Aldric
says, and we sit together a while longer in the kitchen's morning
quiet, both of us considerably more determined, after the conversation,
to make certain neither promise ever has cause to be tested.

·

Sela sends word that same afternoon, asking whether she might join us
for the evening meal, an unusual enough request that I understand,
reading the note, she has something specific on her mind beyond simple
company.

She arrives with a basket of the orchard's own stores and a directness
that doesn't wait for pleasantries.

"I've heard about Feld's contingency plan," she says, once we're
settled. "The village grapevine again. I wanted to hear the actual
shape of it from you both, rather than whatever version's currently
circulating at market."

I give her the plain account, and watch her absorb it with the same
gravity she's shown toward everything touching this family's safety.

"I want to help," she says, once I've finished. "Whatever this
requires. I have standing with the crown's grain office that neither
of you has cause to have built. I'd like to use it, properly, on
Tomas's behalf, if it comes to needing leverage neither of you
currently possesses."

"That's generous," I say, "and considerably more than I expected you
to offer, given you barely know him."

"I know enough," Sela says. "I know he's been folded into this family's
own accounting the same way I was, uninvited, by a rite neither of us
chose. I don't intend to let the crown treat him as a disposable
contingency while I have any standing left to prevent it."

We finish the meal together, considerably more fortified by Sela's own
fierce offer of help, and I find myself grateful, watching her, for a
family that keeps finding new ways to extend its protection outward,
rather than guarding it jealously within its own original boundaries.

·

Aldric walks Sela partway back to the orchard once the meal's finished,
and I use the quiet hour alone to properly write out, for the first
time, a formal account of Feld's contingency threat — dates, specific
language, the whole pattern of pressure he's applied since the binding
began.

"Building the record again," Aldric observes, returning.

"I've decided it's worth doing properly this time," I say, "rather
than simply reacting to each visit as it comes. If Sela's going to use
her own standing on Tomas's behalf, I want her working from an
accurate accounting, not simply secondhand impressions."

"That's good thinking," Aldric says, settling beside me to help.

We finish the account together, methodically, and I find myself,
closing the finished pages, considerably more confident facing
whatever Feld's office has waiting for us next, armed now with
something more substantial than simply hope and careful memory.

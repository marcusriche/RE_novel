## Chapter Three — Ground Rules (Isla)

Aldric comes to the cottage that evening, in the fading winter light, because the rite requires it —
proximity, some minimum measure of it, though the old texts are
maddeningly vague about exactly how much — and because neither of us
has anywhere else the binding will hold properly, given that it was
performed, deliberately, at Highstone rather than at either of our own
doors.

I lay out terms the way I lay out a ledger, because it is the only
register I currently trust myself to speak in without saying something
I haven't fully decided I'm willing to have said.

"Separate rooms," I say. "The cottage has two; I've had the second
aired since this morning. Separate meals, unless the rite calls us
together for some specific purpose the record requires documented. And
no pretending, between the two of us, at anything the rite hasn't
actually made true. I'd rather we both know exactly where the line sits
than have either of us guess wrong about it later and pay for the
guess."

Aldric listens to the whole of it without once interrupting, which is
itself a kind of information — a man giving a woman the room to finish
saying an uncomfortable thing rather than filling the silence early to
manage his own discomfort with hearing it.

"I'm not asking you to want this," he says, once I've finished. "Any of
it. I'm asking you to let me be useful in it, since I'm going to be
here either way, whether either of us wanted a stranger in the second
room or not."

"Useful how," I say, because it is, genuinely, the practical question,
and because I have found, over six years, that vague offers of
usefulness tend to cost considerably more than specific ones once
they're actually called upon.

"However you'll let me be. I can chop wood. I can carry water further
than you should be carrying it yourself after a full day at the cairns.
I know how to graft a wound closed on a tree that's taken damage, which
I'm told is not entirely unlike some of what a wardwright does to
stone, structurally, though I'll grant the materials are different."
He pauses, considering. "I'd also like to know the actual shape of your
work, if you're willing to teach any of it. Not to take it over. To
understand what I've agreed to be bound to."

I had not expected that particular request, and I turn it over for
longer than the pause between us comfortably allows, before answering.

"Most people bound to a wardwright want to understand as little as
possible," I say. "It's easier to be spent by something you haven't
looked at closely."

"I've never once found not-looking to make a cost easier to bear," Aldric
says. "Only easier to ignore until it's too large to manage. I'd rather
look at it properly, from the start, and know exactly what I'm carrying."

I write the terms down, in the end, on a single sheet from the record
book — separate rooms, separate meals except by the rite's own
requirement, no performance of anything not actually true between us —
and I notice, writing them, that I have drawn up rules for a stranger
with considerably more care than I have drawn up anything for my own
comfort in the six years since my mentor died and left me the sole
practitioner of an arithmetic that has never once come out even.

"Sign it if you agree," I say, sliding the sheet across the table.

He reads it twice, signs it without argument, and adds a single line of
his own beneath my signature, in a hand considerably less economical
than Ossian Thale's ever was, though no less careful for the
difference: *I agree to all of the above, and to being taught, if she's
willing to teach me.*

"You added a term," I say.

"I did," Aldric agrees. "I thought it only fair, since you'd written
all of yours first."

I do not have a ready response to a stranger correcting the exact
imbalance I hadn't yet noticed I'd built into my own document, so I
simply fold the sheet and file it, and go to see about the second
room's fire, which needs building regardless of whatever else either of
us has just quietly agreed to.

## Someone Else's Warmth

My glove has been split at the seam for a fortnight. I have not
mentioned it to anyone, because mentioning small cold things has never
once, in my adult life, been a habit I let myself keep — a torn seam is
a torn seam, survivable, and survivable things do not, in my
experience, warrant the specific vulnerability of asking someone else
to fix them.

I find it mended on the cottage step before dawn, stitched along the
whole seam in a neat, close hand that has clearly done considerably more
delicate work than splitting firewood, still warm from whatever fire it
was mended beside, with no note attached and no comment left waiting to
be thanked for.

Aldric is already at the woodpile when I come out, not looking at me,
splitting logs with the specific steady rhythm of a man giving the
moment nowhere convenient to become a scene about itself.

"You didn't have to," I say, because it is the sentence available to
me, and because I have not yet worked out a better one for a situation
I did not know, until this exact moment, that I needed a sentence
prepared for.

"I know," Aldric says, and keeps splitting wood, and does not turn
around, and it is — I realize, standing on the step with a mended glove
in both hands — the first sentence anyone has said to me in longer than
I can accurately place that asks for absolutely nothing back in
exchange for having been said.

I go back inside, eventually, once the cold has done what it always
does and made the choice for me, and sit at the table with the glove
still in both hands for considerably longer than the glove itself
requires. I turn it over, looking at the stitches, small and even and
plainly practiced, and think about a man who mends a stranger's glove
before dawn and says nothing about it afterward, on the theory —
untested by me until this exact morning, and apparently entirely proven
by him — that some kindnesses are only real if nobody's kept a ledger
of them.

I have kept a ledger of nearly everything in my life since I was
nineteen years old. It occurs to me, sitting at my own table with a
stranger's careful stitching warm against my palms, that I have just
been handed the first entry I have absolutely no intention of ever
writing down.

Tomas arrives an hour later to collect the morning's reports and finds
me still at the table, glove on, flexing the mended fingers experimentally
the way you test a fact you're not yet sure you believe.

"New glove?" he asks, entirely too innocently for a boy who has clearly
already worked out that it is not, in fact, a new glove at all.

"Mended," I say.

"By whom?"

"You know by whom, Tomas."

"I do," he agrees, with the specific satisfaction of a nineteen-year-old
who has just confirmed something he already suspected, and goes to put
the kettle on without further comment, which is, from him, its own kind
of mercy.

·

The terms sheet sits on the record-room table for three full days
before I finally file it properly, mostly because I keep finding
reasons to reread it — checking the wording, I tell myself, though I
know every clause of it by heart within the first hour of writing it
down.

Aldric comes to the cottage each of those three evenings with the same
unhurried regularity he brought to the road below Highstone, chopping
wood without being asked, carrying water further than I'd have carried
it myself, precisely as promised, and asking, each evening, a single
question about the actual shape of my work that I find myself,
somewhat to my own surprise, genuinely wanting to answer.

"How do you know which cairns to inspect first," he asks, the second
evening, watching me sort the day's reports by urgency the way I have
sorted them every evening for six years.

"Age, mostly," I say. "The older stones hold their charge longer, but
they also crack more easily once they finally start to go, so I check
them more often even though they need it less frequently, on the
theory that catching a crack early costs less than repairing one
that's already spread."

"That's not so different from grafting," Aldric says. "An old tree
holds a graft better, mostly, but it also splits worse if you cut it
wrong. You learn to read which trees can bear which kind of cut."

"I hadn't thought of it that way," I say, "but I suppose it's the same
principle, dressed in different materials."

"Most principles are," Aldric says, "once you look past what they're
made of."

I find myself, listening to him, doing the thing I rarely do with
anyone — actually enjoying an evening's ordinary conversation, rather
than simply enduring it until I can retreat to my own thoughts. It
unsettles me slightly, the enjoying, in the specific way that anything
unfamiliar tends to unsettle me, though I notice, by the third evening,
that the unsettlement itself has started to feel less like a warning
and more like the ordinary discomfort of learning something new.

"You're different than I expected," I tell him, on the third evening,
because I have decided that the observation deserves saying rather than
simply filing away unspoken.

"Different how?"

"I expected someone who volunteered for a binding sight unseen to be
either reckless or performing nobility for an audience. You're neither.
You're simply — steady. About all of it. The binding, the cost, the
stranger you've been bound to. I don't know what to do with steady. I'm
not certain I've encountered much of it, in my adult life, from anyone
who wasn't being paid to provide it."

"I'm not steady because it's easy," Aldric says. "I'm steady because
the alternative costs more, in the long run, than the steadiness does.
I learned that young, watching my father manage a bad harvest without
once panicking in front of Sela and me, even when I know, now, looking
back, that he must have been considerably more frightened than he ever
let either of us see."

"That's not the same as not being frightened."

"No," Aldric agrees. "It's a different skill entirely. I'm not sure
it's always the healthier one. I'm only sure it's the one I have."

I think about that sentence for a long while after he's gone home for
the evening, turning it over the way I turn over most of what he says
that lands harder than its plainness would suggest, and find myself,
filing the terms sheet away at last, considerably less certain than I
was three days ago that steadiness and honesty are, in fact, the same
thing, or that either of us has yet worked out how to properly tell the
two apart.

·

I dream, that night, for the first time in longer than I can properly
place, of my mother — not Ossian, my actual mother, dead since I was
seven, a woman I remember mostly in fragments: the particular blue of
a shawl she wore constantly, the smell of the bread she baked every
third day without fail, a voice I can no longer properly recall the
pitch of, however hard I try.

I have never told anyone at Highstone that I had a mother at all,
beyond the bare fact recorded in whatever administrative document
brought me into Ossian's care at nineteen. It has always felt, telling
that particular story, like handing someone a piece of myself I am not
certain I can afford to have handled carelessly.

I tell Aldric the following morning, over the kettle's own slow boil,
mostly because the dream has left me unsettled enough that keeping it
entirely private feels, for the first time, like more effort than it's
worth.

"I dreamed about my mother last night," I say. "I don't talk about her,
generally. I'm not certain why I'm telling you now."

"You don't have to have a reason," Aldric says, simply. "I'd like to
hear about her, if you're willing to tell me. Whatever you remember."

I tell him the fragments — the shawl, the bread, the voice I can't
quite place — and watch him listen properly, without once trying to
fill in the gaps with reassurance or comment, simply receiving what I
have to offer exactly as it is.

"Thank you," he says, when I've finished, "for trusting me with that."

"I don't know why it felt important to say," I admit. "It's such a
small thing, in the scheme of everything else currently happening."

"I don't think it's small," Aldric says. "I think it's the opposite of
small. I think you've just handed me the first piece of yourself that
existed entirely before any of this — before the rite, before Ossian,
before the whole architecture of duty you've built your adult life
around. That's not nothing. That's rather everything, I'd say."

I don't have a ready response to being understood quite that
precisely, so I simply let the kettle finish its boil, and pour us both
tea, and carry the specific warmth of having been properly heard
through the rest of that morning's ordinary business.

·

Tomas, arriving partway through the morning to collect the day's
reports, finds the two of us still sitting over the tea, considerably
more relaxed than our usual professional posture, and clocks it
immediately, the way he clocks most things he isn't technically meant
to notice.

"Am I interrupting something," he asks, with the specific innocence of
a young man who already knows the answer and is asking purely for the
pleasure of watching us both scramble slightly to compose ourselves.

"You're not interrupting anything, Tomas."

"I only ask because you're both sitting considerably closer together
than the tea service strictly requires."

"The tea service requires exactly this much proximity," I say, with as
much dignity as I can currently muster. "It's a small table."

"Of course it is," Tomas says, entirely unconvinced, and sets the
morning's reports down with exaggerated care, as though handling
something considerably more fragile than paperwork. "I'll just leave
these here, then, and let the two of you get back to your very
proximity-requiring tea."

He goes, whistling something tuneless and entirely too pleased with
himself, and Aldric watches him leave with a small, amused shake of his
head.

"He's going to be insufferable about this for weeks," Aldric observes.

"He's already insufferable about it," I say. "I don't think there's a
version of this arrangement that doesn't involve considerable teasing
from a nineteen-year-old who's appointed himself its official chronicler."

"I find I don't mind it as much as I probably should," Aldric admits,
and I find, turning the observation over, that I don't either — a
small, unexpected comfort, having someone else's genuine delight
folded into whatever this is becoming, rather than simply the two of
us managing it alone, in careful privacy.

·

The terms sheet resurfaces a week later, when Tomas, sorting through
the record-room's own filing on some unrelated errand, comes across it
by accident and reads it before either of us thinks to stop him.

"Separate rooms," he reads aloud, entirely too delighted with himself.
"Separate meals unless the rite requires otherwise. No pretending at
anything the rite hasn't made true." He looks up at me, eyebrows
raised. "This reads like a legal document. Did you actually make him
sign this?"

"It was a reasonable precaution," I say, "for two strangers suddenly
required to share a household."

"It's the least romantic document I've ever read," Tomas says,
"and I include the crown's actual grain-exemption contracts in that
assessment."

"It wasn't meant to be romantic. It was meant to be clear."

"Well, it's certainly clear," Tomas says, examining Aldric's added
line at the bottom with obvious relish. "He added a term. That's rather
sweet, actually, now that I look at it properly. Practically poetry,
by the standard the rest of the document sets."

"Give it back, Tomas."

"I'm simply appreciating the historical record," he says, though he
does hand it over, still grinning. "I'd like it noted, for that same
record, that I predicted this arrangement would outgrow its own terms
sheet within the month, and I fully intend to remind both of you of
that prediction once it happens."

"Nothing has outgrown anything," I say, with as much composure as I
can currently manage, filing the sheet properly away at last, out of
his reach.

"Of course not," Tomas says, entirely unconvinced, and goes back to
his own work, whistling, and I find myself, once he's gone, rereading
the sheet one final time before setting it in the drawer for good,
newly aware of exactly how much has already shifted since the evening
I wrote it, without either of us formally amending a single clause.

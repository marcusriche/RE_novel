## Chapter Thirty-Four — What She Won't Let Him Pay Alone (Isla)

Tomas wakes me with the note in hand, his face already telling me the
whole of what I'm about to read before I've properly opened it. He has
run the whole distance from the record-room, I understand later, the
moment he found the cottage door standing open and Aldric nowhere in
it, and his own breathing hasn't properly settled even now, standing
at my bedside in the grey pre-dawn light, his knuckles white around
the folded page.

*I renounced the grant,* it says, in Aldric's considerably less
economical hand. *I've gone to Ossory to finish the old rite, on my own
terms, before the crack in the cairn finishes it some other way. I
don't want you spending yourself proving an untested form for a hearth
that's already this far gone. I'm sorry. I love you. Please don't
follow me.*

I read it twice, standing barefoot on the cold floor, my mind doing the
same fast, cold arithmetic it's done every crisis this whole winter —
how long the old rite typically takes, how far the rite-hall sits from
the cottage, how much of that distance I can cover if I stop wasting
time reading a note a third time instead of moving.

I am dressed and running before I've finished reading the last line.

The road to Highstone has never once, in six years of walking it,
seemed this long, and I find myself, running it now, doing the same
arithmetic I did the morning after the byre thawed at Hollow Farm — not
whether the cost is worth paying, simply whether I can possibly move
fast enough to make sure I'm the one who gets to help pay it.

I reach the rite-hall to find Ossory mid-ceremony, the old cadence
already well underway, Aldric standing at the rite-stone with the
specific stillness of a man who has decided, entirely, to let this
particular ending happen to him rather than fight it, and I feel,
through the bond, the whole of what the old rite is currently taking —
not a single scene's cost, the entire final measure, arriving all at
once, and I understand, feeling it, that we have considerably less time
than the note suggested to stop this before it finishes what it's
started.

"Stop," I say, loud enough that it carries over Ossory's own recitation,
loud enough that the whole hall turns to look at a wardwright who has
never once, in six years, raised her voice in this particular room.

Ossory stops. Aldric does not turn, does not look at me, the specific
deliberate refusal of a man who knows, if he looks, he will not be able
to finish what he's started.

The whole hall holds its breath around us, witnesses to something none
of them properly understand yet, and I find I do not care, in this
exact moment, who's watching or what it costs my own professional
standing to be seen shouting down a rite mid-ceremony. Some things
matter considerably more than composure.

"You don't get to decide this alone," I say, walking toward the
rite-stone, saying the thing I have spent this whole arrangement
learning to say instead of silently absorbing whatever cost the world
hands me. "Not the cost. Not the ending. Not what I'm allowed to be
spent on, or protected from, or asked to simply accept without a say in
the matter."

"I renounced the grant," Aldric says, hollow, still not looking at me,
meaning to make it simple, meaning, I understand, to make it
irreversible before I could argue him out of it. "You don't have to—"

"I don't have to," I agree. "I'm choosing to. That's the whole of what
the mutual rite actually means, Aldric — not a debt either of us is
compelled to pay, a choice each of us makes, openly, in front of
witnesses, precisely because it costs something and we've decided,
together, that the cost is worth sharing rather than avoiding. You
don't get to protect me out of choosing it. That's not protection.
That's simply making the choice for both of us, alone, the exact thing
you promised me you'd stop doing."

He turns, finally, to look at me, and I see, in his face, the specific
devastation of a man realizing, in real time, that the thing he
believed was the single bravest act of his life has just been named,
plainly, as its opposite, the whole hall's silence pressing in around
the two of us as though even the old rite-stones themselves were
waiting to hear what he'd say next.

"I'm sorry," he says. "I thought—"

"I know what you thought," I say. "You thought giving before being
asked was love. I understand where that comes from. I understand it
better than almost anyone could, given how long I've practiced the
exact same habit myself. I need you to understand, right now, in front
of Ossory and whoever else is in this hall, that it isn't. It's fear,
wearing love's coat, the same coat I've worn myself for six years."

He does not argue the point further, and I understand, watching the
fight visibly go out of him, that some part of him has known it all
along, underneath the careful reasoning — that the note, the silence,
the whole quiet architecture of this morning's decision was never
protection at all, simply the oldest habit he owns, dressed up one
final time in its most convincing coat.

Ossory, watching two wardwrights argue in his rite-hall over which one
gets to be spent, does something neither of us expects: he calls the
old rite's progression to a halt, formally, and sends for the mutual
form's translation himself.

"I'll hear the mutual form argued properly," he says, "tonight, rather
than let either of you decide anything further alone, in a hall built
for exactly the kind of decision neither of you should be making without
proper witness."

It is not, yet, a rescue. The old rite has already taken considerably
more than either of us would have chosen to spend this morning, and I
do not know, watching Aldric sway slightly where he stands, whether we
have caught this in time. But it is, for the first time since the
crack in the Long Reach cairn, a fight we are both actually in
together, rather than one either of us is attempting to survive alone.

I go to him, finally, now that Ossory's halted the rite properly, and
take his good hand in both of mine, and he lets me, entirely, without
the reflexive flinch of a man still trying to manage how much of his
own weakness I'm allowed to see. It is, I think, the first time all
morning that he has let himself simply be held rather than continuing,
somewhere underneath the surrender, to argue for his own right to be
spent alone.

·

Sela arrives at the rite-hall within the hour, summoned by Tomas the
moment the halt was called, and I watch her cross the whole distance
from orchard to hall faster than I have ever seen her move, arriving
breathless, her face white with a fear she does not bother hiding from
anyone in the room, still in her working clothes, hands still marked
with the morning's grafting wax, having clearly dropped everything the
instant word reached her.

"How bad," she says, to me, direct, the same question she asked the
night the cairn cracked.

"Bad," I say, honestly. "He's stable, for now. Ossory's halted the old
rite before it finished taking everything. But he's spent, considerably,
and we don't yet know if the mutual form can be readied fast enough to
matter."

Sela's composure cracks, briefly, entirely, and I watch her allow
herself the full weight of the fear for exactly as long as it takes to
properly feel it before she gathers herself back into the fierce,
practical woman this whole family has come to depend on.

Sela goes to Aldric's side, kneeling beside where he sits, propped
against the rite-stone's own base, and takes his good hand in both of
hers.

"You absolute fool," she says, with a specific, fierce tenderness that
tells me exactly how much love is folded into the insult. "You could
have told me. Any of it. I'd have come to Highstone myself and sat on
you bodily before I let you walk in here alone."

"I know," Aldric says, weakly, something almost like a smile crossing
his face despite everything. "I think that's rather why I didn't tell
you."

"That's not funny."

"No," Aldric agrees. "It isn't. I'm sorry, Sela. For all of it. For
letting you find out this way, again, the same as every other time
I've decided a cost was mine alone to carry."

Sela doesn't answer immediately, simply holding his hand, her own
composure visibly costing her considerable effort to maintain. When she
finally speaks, her voice is steadier, though I can see what the
steadiness costs her.

"We're doing this properly," she says, "the mutual rite, whatever it
requires of any of us. I don't care what Ossory's usual timeline
prefers. I want my brother alive considerably more than I want this
handled according to anyone's comfortable schedule."

"Agreed," I say, and mean it entirely, and the three of us — Sela,
Aldric, and I — wait together at the rite-stone's base for Ossory's
final word on how quickly the mutual form might actually be attempted,
a small, frightened, determined company, refusing, all of us, to let
this particular cost be paid alone by anyone.

·

Tomas keeps watch at the rite-hall door while Sela goes to find Ossory,
and Aldric and I are left, briefly, with something almost like privacy,
his head resting against the rite-stone's cold base, my hand in his
good one, neither of us saying anything for a long moment because there
does not seem to be anything left worth managing between us.

"I need you to promise me something," I say, finally, "and I need you
to actually mean it, this time, rather than simply telling me what
you think will let me stop worrying long enough to leave you alone
again."

"Anything," he says, and I hear, underneath the single word, exactly
how much he means it, chastened in a way no lecture of mine could have
produced as thoroughly as this morning's near-catastrophe already has.

"If we survive the mutual rite today," I say, "you don't get to decide,
ever again, that a cost is yours alone to manage quietly. Not the small
ones. Not the large ones. Not even the ones you're certain I'd tell you
not to worry about. I would rather be woken at any hour, for any reason,
than find another note in Tomas's hand telling me you've already decided
something on my behalf."

"I promise," Aldric says. "Properly, this time. Not the version of
myself that believes his own reasoning right up until the exact moment
it very nearly kills him." He turns his head, slightly, to look at me
properly, the effort of it visibly costing him something. "I understand
now what you meant, that first week, about the difference between being
protected and being managed. I thought I understood it. I didn't, not
really, until I was the one lying against a rite-stone realizing I'd
done to you exactly what I promised I'd never do."

"I forgive you," I say, "conditionally, on the promise actually holding
past today's fear."

"That's fair," he says. "I'd forgive me conditionally too."

I laugh, despite everything, a short, startled sound that surprises me
more than it seems to surprise him, and he manages something like a
smile in return, weak but genuine, and for a moment, in the cold
rite-hall with the whole outcome of the day still entirely unresolved,
it feels almost bearable, sitting here together with nothing left
between us to manage.

"Whatever happens at the cairn," I say, "I want you to know I'd rather
have had these six weeks with you, exactly as difficult and exactly as
frightening as they've actually been, than the whole quiet, careful,
unbound life I had before you arrived on that road."

"I feel exactly the same," Aldric says, "though I'd have preferred
arriving at saying so without quite this much blood loss involved."

"Next time," I say, "we'll manage the confession part without the
near-fatal old rite attached to it."

"I'll hold you to that," he says, and closes his eyes, not sleeping,
simply resting, and I sit with him, holding his hand, until Sela's
returning footsteps announce that Ossory is on his way back with
whatever answer he's spent the last hour deciding.

·

Tomas slips back inside briefly, checking on us before returning to his
post at the door, and I catch him looking at Aldric with an expression
that isn't quite anger anymore, though it isn't quite forgiveness
either.

"I'm not ready to say I've forgiven you," he tells Aldric, plainly,
"for the note. I want that understood before whatever happens next at
the cairn."

"That's fair," Aldric says, weakly. "I don't think I've forgiven myself
yet either."

"I'm frightened," Tomas admits, "of what happens if the mutual form
doesn't work, after everything. I wanted to say that properly, rather
than manage it silently the way apparently everyone in this hall has
spent their whole lives doing."

"You're allowed to be frightened," I say. "We all are. I think that's
rather the only honest way to walk into what's coming."

"Is there anything I can actually do," Tomas asks, before turning back
to the door, "besides simply keeping watch?"

"Keep believing this is going to work," I say. "I know that's not
practical, in the way you'd probably prefer. It's what I actually need
from you right now."

"I can do that," Tomas says, with more conviction than his frightened
face quite manages, and I understand, watching him steady himself
against his own fear for my benefit, exactly how much this whole
crisis has already asked of someone barely out of boyhood.

Tomas nods, absorbing this, and returns to his watch at the door, and
I find myself, in the brief quiet that follows, grateful for a young
man young enough to say his fear plainly, rather than manage it the
way the rest of us have spent a lifetime learning not to.

·

Ossory returns within the hour, considerably more decisive than his
own usual careful pace generally allows, and I understand, watching
his face, that whatever internal deliberation he's just concluded, it
has cost him something to move this quickly.

"I have spent every spare hour since you first brought me this record
verifying what I could of its provenance," he says, "and I find myself
unable, in good conscience, to let procedural caution cost a man his
life when the alternative has already very nearly killed him once this
morning. I will sanction the attempt. Today. At the Long Reach cairn,
where the crack already stands as its own kind of evidence that the
standard rite has run out of road."

"Thank you," I say, and the words feel entirely insufficient for the
weight of what he's just decided to risk on our behalf.

"Don't thank me yet," Ossory says, echoing, almost word for word, the
exact sentiment I have heard offered, in one form or another, by
everyone who has ever handed me something significant this winter
without full certainty of the outcome. "I am sanctioning an attempt,
not guaranteeing a result. I
have no proof this form is survivable, only a dead woman's careful
account and my own considerable hope that hope is not, in this
instance, foolish."

"That's more than we had an hour ago," Aldric says, from where Sela is
helping him to his feet, considerably steadier than he was, though
still visibly diminished by the morning's near-catastrophe.

"Then let's not waste the daylight we have left to attempt it properly,"
Ossory says, and the whole small company — Ossory, Tomas, Sela, Aldric,
and I — set out together for the Long Reach cairn, carrying between us
considerably more hope than any of us arrived at the rite-hall with
this morning.

## Chapter Twenty-Nine — Feld's Deadline (Isla)

Feld's letter arrives three days after the meeting with Ossory, shorter
than his usual ledgers and considerably more dangerous for the
brevity: one more failed cairn, it states, and the crown will authorize
the apprentice's binding regardless of any wardwright's personal
objection to the timing.

I read it twice at the record-room table, the specific bureaucratic
economy of its phrasing somehow more chilling than any of Feld's
lengthier ultimatums have managed to be.

"You've had a season to prove the current binding sufficient,
Wardwright," Feld says, in person this time, having ridden down from
whatever crown office issues these particular ultimatums specifically
to deliver this one with his own hand. "The Rime doesn't extend
courtesy for good intentions, and neither, I'm afraid, does the crown's
patience."

I want, badly, to tell him about the mutual rite — to offer him
something other than my own bare word that the current arrangement is
worth preserving a little longer. I cannot, not without the proper
translation Ossory has requested, not without risking Feld deciding the
whole discovery is exactly the kind of destabilizing nonsense a
desperate wardwright, out of options, would simply invent to buy time.

"The binding is holding," I say instead, which is true, this week,
though the truth has never once felt more provisional than it does
saying it to Feld's carefully unreadable face.

"For how much longer," Feld asks, "is the actual question the crown
needs answered, Wardwright, and I notice you haven't given me a number,
in any of our conversations, that I could actually take back to them."

"I don't have a number I'm prepared to guarantee," I say, honestly,
because I have decided, somewhere in these past weeks, that lying to
Feld costs considerably more, eventually, than simply refusing to hand
him false comfort.

Feld's letter, once read fully, contains the part that frightens me
considerably more than the ultimatum itself: a specific date, one
month hence, by which the crown expects either proof of the current
binding's sufficiency or Tomas's own binding properly arranged,
regardless of my objection.

Tomas, told the contents of the letter afterward, goes quiet in a way
that isn't fear exactly — closer to a young man doing the specific
arithmetic of duty that nobody his age should have to perform this
early in a career he's barely begun.

"I won't let it be you," I tell him, and hear, saying it, exactly how
much that promise is going to cost me to keep, given a month is
considerably less time than either the translation or Ossory's own
careful consideration is likely to require.

"You can't promise that," Tomas says, gently, "not if the choice ends
up not being yours to make."

"I know," I say. "I'm promising it anyway, because I intend to make
certain the choice stays mine to make, one way or another, before that
date arrives."

Aldric finds me afterward, still holding Feld's letter, and reads it
over my shoulder without asking.

"A month," he says.

"A month," I agree.

"That's not much time to properly test an unproven rite."

"No," I say. "It isn't. But it's the time we have, and I'd rather
spend all of it trying to make the mutual form work than spend any of
it managing Feld's patience instead of the actual problem."

Aldric doesn't argue with that, though I feel him weighing something,
privately, in the particular stillness he gets when he's about to say
something he suspects will cost him to say.

"If it comes down to a choice," he says, finally, "between Tomas and
me—"

"It won't," I say, before he can finish the sentence, sharper than I
intend, because I understand, hearing the shape of where he's headed,
exactly what he's about to offer, and I am not, tonight, prepared to
hear it out loud.

"I need you to hear the rest of the sentence," Aldric says, gently, "even
if you don't like where it's going."

"Not tonight," I say, and he lets it go, this once, though I feel the
unfinished sentence sitting between us for the rest of the evening,
patient, waiting for whichever night either of us finally runs out of
room to keep postponing it.

·

Tomas brings the finished translation to Ossory himself, the following
morning, insisting, with a stubbornness I recognize as entirely my own
teaching bearing unexpected fruit, that he be the one to present the
portion he cross-checked, rather than simply letting me carry the whole
of it forward on his behalf.

"You did good work," I tell him, watching him gather the pages with
visibly shaking hands, nervous in a way I haven't seen from him since
his very first solo cairn inspection.

"I want him to see that I understand it too," Tomas says. "Not just
that I helped. That I actually understand what we found, properly, the
way a wardwright should."

"He'll see that," I say, and I am right — Ossory listens to Tomas's
own portion of the presentation with the particular careful attention
he generally reserves for considerably more senior voices, asking two
genuine questions about the grammar Tomas cross-checked, both of which
Tomas answers precisely, without hesitation.

"You've trained him well," Ossory says to me, afterward, once Tomas has
gone, visibly relieved, to report the meeting's success to Aldric.

"He's trained himself, mostly," I say. "I've simply tried not to get in
the way of it."

"That's rarer than you'd think, among practitioners," Ossory says.
"Most wardwrights I've known guard their own expertise rather more
jealously than you seem inclined to. I take it as a good sign, for the
line's future, that you've chosen otherwise."

I think about that observation for the rest of the day, turning it over
alongside everything else this whole strange season has taught me
about the difference between carrying a burden alone because you
believe it's noble, and handing pieces of it forward, deliberately, to
whoever's ready to carry their own share of it properly — a lesson, I
understand, watching Tomas practically glow with the specific pride of
having been taken seriously, that applies to considerably more than
simply the mutual rite's translation.

·

Feld's letter, once the shock of its deadline properly settles, prompts
a considerably more practical response from me than my first reading
allowed — I begin, that same evening, drafting a formal reply of my
own, one that does not yet reveal the mutual form's existence, but that
lays out, plainly, precisely how much the current binding has already
achieved against the line's failing arithmetic.

"You're building a case," Aldric observes, watching me work through
several drafts.

"I'm building a record," I say. "If Feld intends to force Tomas's own
binding regardless of what Ossory eventually decides, I want the
crown's own office to have, in writing, an honest account of exactly
how much this current arrangement has already held, in case anyone
further up the chain ever needs reminding that the current binding is
worth defending on its own merits, independent of whatever else Ossory
eventually sanctions."

"Do you think it'll matter? A written record, against Feld's own
timeline?"

"I don't know," I admit. "I know that Ossian never once, in his whole
career, kept this kind of formal defense of his own work. I think, if
he had, the crown's office might have thought rather harder before
funding this line down to a single practitioner in the first place. I'd
rather leave a proper record than simply hope the crown's better
judgment eventually prevails on its own."

Aldric reads the finished draft that evening, and I watch him take in
the whole of it — six years of cairns held, of the line's slow,
patient defense against a cold that never once extends courtesy for
good intentions — with something that looks, on his face, considerably
closer to awe than I expected from a man who has watched me do this
exact work, day in and day out, for the whole of our acquaintance.

"I don't think I properly understood, until reading this, quite how
much you've actually done," he says, quietly. "I've watched you work.
I don't think I've ever seen the whole of it laid out, plainly, in one
place."

"Neither had I," I admit, "until I sat down to write it."

·

Feld's own response to my formal letter, when it finally arrives,
surprises me with its brevity — a single line, considerably less
bureaucratic than his usual correspondence, acknowledging receipt and
noting only that "the record will be considered alongside all other
relevant factors in the crown's ongoing assessment."

"That's not much of a response," Aldric observes, reading it over my
shoulder.

"No," I agree, "but it's not a dismissal either, which is rather more
than I expected from him. I think — and I could be wrong — that he's
actually reading it properly, rather than filing it unread the way I
half-feared he would."

"Do you think he's capable of being convinced? Genuinely, I mean, not
simply outmaneuvered."

I consider the question properly, thinking back over every interaction
I've had with Feld across six years of dealing with his particular
brand of careful, bloodless bureaucracy.

"I think he's a man who's decided that following the crown's own
arithmetic exactly is the safest way to conduct himself professionally,"
I say. "I don't think he's cruel, precisely. I think he's simply never
been given a reason to imagine an alternative worth risking his own
career over. I'd like to think this letter might be the beginning of
that reason, though I'm not holding out excessive hope."

"That's a considerably more generous read of him than I'd have
managed," Aldric says.

"I've had six years to develop it," I say, "and I find, this particular
season, that I have rather more patience for complicated people than I
used to. I suppose that's simply one more thing this whole arrangement
has taught me."

·

Aldric raises, that same evening, the unfinished sentence from a few
nights before — the one I refused to let him complete — choosing his
moment carefully, once the day's harder business has settled and
neither of us has anything further urgent demanding attention.

"I'd like to finish what I was saying," he says, "about Tomas. If
you're willing to hear it now."

I consider refusing again, the old reflex rising immediately, and then
make myself set it aside, because I have decided, this whole season,
that avoiding a fear rarely makes it smaller.

"Go on," I say.

"If it comes down to a choice between his binding and mine," Aldric
says, carefully, "I want it to be mine. Not because I think my life is
worth more than his. Because I chose this, at the rite field, with my
eyes open, and he hasn't had the chance to choose anything yet. I don't
think it's fair to let a nineteen-year-old inherit a crisis neither of
us managed to solve in time."

"I understand why you'd want that," I say, "and I need you to
understand that I'm not going to let it come to a choice at all. I'm
not choosing between the two of you. I'm going to find a way to save
you both, or I'm going to fail trying, but I refuse to sit here and
plan which one of you I'm prepared to lose."

"That's not always a choice either of us gets to make," Aldric says,
gently. "Sometimes the arithmetic simply runs out before anyone's
ready for it to."

"I know," I say. "I've spent six years watching the arithmetic run out
on people I couldn't save in time. I'm not accepting it in advance,
this time, simply because it might eventually prove necessary. I'd
rather exhaust every possibility first."

Aldric doesn't argue further, though I can see the offer still sitting
there, unwithdrawn, a small, terrible kindness he's prepared to make if
the month's deadline ever forces the question. I choose, for tonight,
simply to hold his hand rather than argue the point any further, both
of us understanding that whatever the coming weeks demand, neither of
us is fully in control of how this particular arithmetic will
eventually resolve.

·

Tomas arrives the following morning with news of his own, considerably
more cheerful than the previous evening's difficult conversation, and I
find myself grateful for the ordinary interruption.

"I've finished the second draft of the translation," he announces,
setting a neat stack of pages on the table. "Properly this time, cross-
checked against every fragment in the record box twice over. I don't
think there's a single ambiguous phrase left unexamined."

"That's excellent work," I say, examining the pages, genuinely
impressed by the thoroughness. "This is considerably more polished than
what we brought Ossory initially."

"I wanted it to be unimpeachable," Tomas says, "given how much rides on
Ossory trusting the translation completely. I didn't want a single
loose thread anyone could use to dismiss the whole argument."

"You've more than managed that," I say. "I think this might be the
single most careful piece of work I've seen from you in three years."

Tomas's pride at the compliment is entirely unguarded, the particular
pleasure of a young man who has poured real effort into something and
finally been told, plainly, that the effort was worth it.

"I wanted to actually contribute something concrete," he admits, "rather
than simply watch the two of you navigate the harder decisions. This
felt like the one part I could genuinely help with, properly."

"You've helped considerably more than you know," I say, and mean it,
and the morning's harder business waits a little longer while the three
of us properly celebrate a piece of careful, patient work finally,
thoroughly finished.

·

Aldric takes the finished translation from Tomas that afternoon,
reading it carefully in a quiet corner of the record-room, and I find
him some while later, considerably more moved than the document's dry,
careful language would seem to warrant.

"What is it," I ask.

"This line," he says, pointing to a passage near the middle. "About
naming, before the stone, exactly what each person is choosing to risk.
I keep thinking about how differently my own life might have gone, if
someone had ever asked me to name what I was risking, properly, before
I volunteered for anything — Sela's exemption, the binding itself. I
don't think I've ever once been asked to name the risk before taking
it on."

"You're asking now," I say, "in your own way, simply by noticing what
you missed."

"I suppose I am," Aldric says. "I find I want that for Tomas,
specifically, whenever he eventually faces his own significant choices.
I don't want him inheriting the habit of volunteering blindly, the way
I always have."

"I don't think he will," I say. "I think he's already learned,
watching all of us this winter, that naming the risk properly is part
of choosing it honestly."

"I hope you're right," Aldric says, and we sit together with the
translation between us a while longer, both of us grateful for a
document that has, in its careful, ancient language, taught us
something considerably more personal than its authors likely intended.

·

Tomas returns that evening, curious what we've made of the finished
translation, and I share Aldric's own observation about naming the
risk before taking it on.

"I hadn't thought of it that way," Tomas admits, "though I think I
understand it properly now, hearing it named. I suppose that's rather
what I've been doing, drafting this translation so carefully — naming,
to myself, exactly what I was risking, getting a single detail wrong
in something this significant."

"That's a good instinct," I say, "carrying it into your own work,
even before either of us pointed it out directly."

"I think I learned it from watching both of you, this whole season,"
Tomas says, "long before either of you said it plainly enough for me
to properly notice I'd already absorbed the lesson."

We sit together a while longer, the three of us, considerably grateful
for a document that has, in its own quiet way, taught this whole
household the same steadying truth about naming a risk honestly before
choosing to carry it.

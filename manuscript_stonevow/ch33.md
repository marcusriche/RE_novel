## Chapter Thirty-Three — Vow Renounced (Aldric)

I go to Keeper Ossory alone, before dawn, while Isla is still asleep,
and I want to be honest about the reasoning, because I have promised
myself, walking to Highstone in the dark, that whatever else I do this
morning, I will not lie to myself about my own motives the way I have
spent a lifetime managing everyone else's understanding of them, even
though every step of the walk down feels like an argument I am
carefully avoiding having with myself.

The frost has properly set in overnight, thick enough that my own
breath fogs visibly in the pre-dawn dark, and I find myself grateful,
walking, for the cold's specific clarity — a fact demanding attention
plainly enough that it leaves no room for the reasoning I am not yet
ready to properly examine.

I am not asking him for the mutual rite. I am asking him for the old
one, finished, on my own terms, before the crack in the Long Reach
cairn finishes the job some other way, at some other cost, that neither
of us gets to choose the shape of.

I left the note on the kitchen table, weighted under the good mug, in
the specific spot I knew she'd find it first thing, and told myself,
writing it, that this was mercy — a clean explanation rather than a
frightened absence with nothing attached to it. I understand now,
walking the last stretch to Highstone in the pre-dawn cold, that I
chose the spot as carefully as I chose the words, and that carefulness
itself should have told me something about what I was actually doing,
if I'd been willing to look at it plainly before the choosing was
already finished.

"The rite was mine to volunteer for," I tell him, in the rite-hall's
particular pre-dawn cold, considerably emptier than it was the morning
of the binding. "Let it be mine to finish. Don't let her spend herself
proving an untested form to save a hearth that's already this far
gone."

Ossory looks at me for a long moment, weighing considerably more than
my own words.

"You understand what you're asking," he says. "The old rite, completed
properly, takes the whole of what's left. There's no partial version of
it, once begun."

He does not ask, immediately, why I've come alone, though I see the
question sitting in his face, and I understand, watching him hold it
back, that he is extending me a courtesy I have not properly earned —
the space to make this decision without being made to justify, aloud,
exactly why I've chosen to make it without the one person who might
have talked me out of it.

"I understand," I say, and I do, entirely, and I have decided, walking
here in the dark, that understanding it fully and choosing it anyway is
the one genuinely brave thing available to me, rather than the
cowardice I suspect, somewhere underneath my own careful reasoning, it
might actually be.

I understand something else too, standing in that hall, that I have
not fully let myself examine until this exact moment: that this is the
exact corrupted shape of the rite Isla and I swore, together, to
rewrite. One hearth, alone, spent quietly, called noble instead of
named for what it actually is, which is a man too frightened of being
asked to simply wait to be asked.

I understand it, and I proceed anyway, which is perhaps the truest
measure of how deep this particular habit runs in me — that even full,
clear-eyed recognition of the pattern isn't, this morning, quite
enough to stop me from repeating it one final time.

I do it anyway. I ask Ossory to begin the old form, and I write a note
for Isla — because I am not, whatever else I am doing this morning,
willing to let her wake to an absence with no explanation at all,
though I understand now, considerably too late, that a note is not
the same as a presence, and that I chose the smaller, safer offering
precisely because it asked nothing further of my own nerve — and
I hand it to Tomas to deliver once the sun is fully up, and I go with
Ossory to the rite-stone, and I formally, through him, withdraw the
grant I gave Isla the night we asked each other properly at the cairn
field.

It does not feel like betrayal, doing it. It feels, in the moment,
like the single most loving thing I have ever done for anyone.

I understand, considerably later, once it is nearly too late to
understand anything at all, exactly how wrong I was about that.

Ossory's own hand does not waver, beginning the recitation, though I
catch something in his face that might, on another man, be called
reluctance — the specific weariness of a Keeper who has performed this
exact rite too many times to properly believe, any longer, in its own
claimed nobility.

The rite begins to take. I feel it pull at whatever's left of me — not
the specific, located cost of an ordinary ward, something considerably
larger, the whole final measure the old form was always designed to
extract from a single hearth, and I let it, because I have decided,
walking here in the dark, that giving before being asked is the same
thing as love, a belief I have held my entire life and am only now,
with the rite's own considerable weight bearing down on me, beginning
to properly suspect might be the exact opposite of what it's always
felt like from the inside.

I think, somewhere in the taking, of Isla's face the night she told me,
plainly, that she'd rather have the true promise than the clean one. I
think that I have just handed her, instead, the cleanest, most
carefully managed lie I have ever told anyone — a note instead of a
conversation, a decision made unilaterally instead of asked for
properly, the exact thing she made me promise, at the cairn field,
never to do to her.

I understand, as the old rite takes hold in earnest, that I have broken
that promise as thoroughly as it is possible to break one, and that I
am not, any longer, entirely certain I will live long enough to
properly apologize for it.

·

I think, in the strange, distant clarity the old rite's taking seems to
grant a person even as it empties them, about the whole of the reasoning
that brought me here — reasoning that felt, walking to Highstone in the
dark, entirely sound, and that feels, now, considerably less sound with
every measure the stone draws out of me.

I think about Sela, and the specific relief I felt, putting my own name
forward at the rite two winters ago, at finally having a cost I was
permitted to pay openly, rather than the whole accumulated weight of
providing for a household that never once asked me to carry quite this
much of it alone.

I think about my father, managing our whole childhood without ever once
asking either of us what we needed, and understand, with the particular
lucidity of a man running out of time to understand anything further,
that I have just done exactly what he did — decided, unilaterally, that
my own suffering was a private matter, to be managed rather than shared,
regardless of who else's life it was also going to reshape.

I understand, with the same terrible clarity, that he likely felt this
exact same conviction, dying — that he was sparing us something, rather
than simply denying us the chance to properly help carry it. I wonder,
distantly, whether he ever once, in his own final hour, glimpsed the
shape of what I am only now beginning to see.

I think, last of all, about Isla asking me, plainly, at the cairn field,
whether I actually wanted this — the two of us, whatever we've become —
and giving her, in return, the truest yes I have ever given anyone. I
understand, feeling the old rite pull at whatever's left, that renouncing
the grant this morning was not, in fact, an act of love at all. It was
the oldest fear I have, dressed up carefully enough to fool even myself:
the fear of being asked for more than I believed I was worth giving, and
choosing, instead, to give everything at once, alone, so that no one
would ever have the chance to find the asking insufficient.

I do not get to finish the thought properly. The rite's taking has its
own momentum now, considerably larger than my own capacity to reason
against it, and I understand, distantly, that whatever happens next is
no longer entirely mine to decide.

I think, in the strange suspended time the taking allows, about Tomas,
too — about the specific relief on his face when I first showed him
how to properly grip a graft knife, months before any of this began,
long before either of us knew the rite would eventually bind me to the
woman training him. I think about the small carved bird I never got
the chance to finish giving him, the lesson in carving I'd half-promised
and never properly delivered. I think, distantly, that there are a
great many small unfinished things a person leaves behind, dying this
way, that no formal record of the rite ever properly accounts for —
not simply a hearth's cost to a wardwright, but a brother's cost to a
sister, a teacher's cost to a student he never got to finish teaching.

I think, last, about the crooked tree above the orchard's northern
wall, and understand, with a clarity that frightens me more than the
rite's own taking does, that I have spent my whole life keeping things
alive that stopped bearing fruit, on the theory that the keeping itself
was worth something regardless of the fruit — and that I am, this
morning, in this rite-hall, doing precisely the opposite to myself,
choosing to be cut down rather than kept, on the mistaken belief that
my own barrenness would somehow spare everyone else the trouble of
tending me.

I understand, with the last clear thought the rite's taking properly
affords me, that I have gotten the whole lesson of that tree
backward from the very beginning — that keeping a thing alive was
never truly about the fruit at all, and that I am no exception to a
rule I have spent two winters believing applied to everything except
myself.

·

I think, somewhere further into the taking, about the note I left for
Isla, and understand, with a clarity that arrives too late to be of
any use, exactly how carefully I chose every word in it to make the
leaving sound like generosity rather than what it actually was — a
man too frightened to be asked, choosing instead to decide everything
alone and dress the deciding up as a gift.

I think about the specific phrase I wrote — *please don't follow me* —
and understand, only now, feeling the rite's own considerable pull,
that it was never a request at all. It was an instruction, the same
kind my father used to give, gently, lovingly, and completely without
room for refusal, dressed in language soft enough that neither Sela
nor I ever quite noticed, growing up, that we'd never actually been
asked anything at all.

I understand, with the same late clarity, that Isla would have caught
the falseness of it immediately, reading it, the exact hollow center
underneath the careful, generous-sounding words — and that whatever
frightened, desperate hope I had, writing it, that she might simply
accept the note and let me go, was never a hope grounded in who she
actually is. It was a hope grounded in who I needed her to be, this
one morning, so that my own decision could go unchallenged.

She was never going to simply accept it. I understand that now, with
the rite's own weight bearing down, in a way I should have understood
writing the note in the first place, if I had been honest with myself
about what six weeks with her have actually taught me: that she does
not let costs go unnamed, does not let unilateral decisions stand
unquestioned, does not, under any circumstance I have yet discovered,
simply accept a version of events handed to her instead of asked of
her.

I think, with something that might be the beginning of proper regret
rather than the earlier's manufactured nobility, that I owe her
considerably more than an apology, if either of us survives what's
currently taking hold of me. I owe her the plain admission that I knew,
somewhere underneath my own careful reasoning, exactly how she would
respond to that note — and wrote it anyway, hoping, against everything
six weeks should have taught me, that this once, she might simply let
me have my own quiet ending without a fight.

I think, too, about Tomas, and the promise I half-made him without
quite meaning to — that he would inherit a line properly held, rather
than one still shaped by the same corrupted arithmetic that killed
Ossian Thale. I understand, feeling the old rite's considerable pull,
that dying here, quietly, alone, having renounced the very mutual form
that might have spared him the same eventual choice, would teach him
precisely the wrong lesson at precisely the moment he's most vulnerable
to learning it. I would be handing him, as my last act, the exact
inheritance I always meant to spare him — a wardwright's line held by
solitary sacrifice, dressed up as nobility, precisely because nobody
before either of us found the nerve to try it any other way.

I understand, with the rite's own weight bearing down harder now, that
this is not, in fact, only my ending to choose. It never was. Not
Isla's alone to grieve, not Tomas's alone to inherit lessons from, not
Sela's alone to explain to whatever remains of our shrinking family. I
have spent this whole morning believing I was making a private decision
about my own private cost, and I understand now, far too late to
properly act on the understanding, that there has never been a private
version of this available to me at all.

·

There is a strange mercy, I discover, in the particular clarity the old
rite grants even as it empties a person — a mercy I would not have
believed possible, reading about it secondhand in whatever accounts of
the standard rite I've encountered over the years. I find myself, in
the taking's own peculiar suspended moment, remembering things I had
not thought about in years: my mother's specific laugh, a sound I'd
believed entirely lost to memory; the particular satisfaction of a
graft finally taking root after a difficult season; Isla's face, the
first time she properly smiled at something I'd said, unguarded, before
either of us had admitted what was growing between us.

I think, distantly, that if this is how it ends — not tragically, not
with the specific violence I'd always imagined the old rite's taking
must involve, simply this quiet accounting of a life properly lived,
however briefly, however incompletely — then perhaps I have not,
after all, wasted the whole of what I was given.

I do not believe this thought fully. Some part of me, even now, even
emptying, understands that ending here, quietly, alone, having broken
the one promise that mattered most to the person who taught me what
honesty actually costs, would not be a good ending at all. It would
simply be the same old failure, dressed up in a peaceful enough
feeling that I very nearly let myself mistake the dressing for the
substance underneath.

I hear, distantly, a voice raised over Ossory's own recitation, and
understand, even as the old rite's taking briefly falters, that Isla
has arrived, exactly as I feared and precisely as I should have known
she would, refusing, as she has refused everything else this winter,
to let this particular cost be paid without her.

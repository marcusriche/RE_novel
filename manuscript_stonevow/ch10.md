## Chapter Ten — What the Stone Takes (Isla)

Every ward I have ever set, I have set alone. The rite that bound me to
Aldric requires, for the first time in six years of practice, that I
set one with an actual hearth standing at the stone beside me, and I
find, the morning of it, that I have spent considerably more of the
night rehearsing how to manage my own nerves about the fact than I have
spent, in years, rehearsing anything at all.

I have performed this exact rite structure sixty times, by my own
count, and never once, in all those sixty performances, have I had to
consider anything beyond my own steadiness at the stone. This morning,
for the first time, I find myself considering someone else's entirely.

"Nothing's going to go wrong," I tell Tomas, in the tone I use for
things I have not yet finished believing myself, when he asks, careful,
whether he should come along in case something does.

"I didn't say I thought something would," Tomas says. "I said I'd feel
better coming, in case."

"Stay and mind the reports," I say. "I need someone I trust minding the
ledger while I'm at the Long Reach cairn, in case Feld sends anything
requiring an immediate answer."

He accepts this, though not entirely, and I find Aldric at the cottage
door already dressed for the cold, having assumed, correctly, without
being told, that today was the day.

"You didn't need to be ready this early," I say.

"I've been ready since you first mentioned it, three days ago," he
says. "I wanted to give you the fewest possible reasons to postpone."

I do not have an answer to being so precisely understood, so I simply
lead the way to the Long Reach cairn, through a cold that has, this
morning, the specific bright, brittle quality of the deepest part of
Coldwake finally arriving in earnest.

·

The Long Reach cairn stands at the northern edge of the line proper, a
slab of frost-veined granite worn smooth by generations of hands, and I
have set wards at this exact stone perhaps sixty times in six years,
alone, without incident, without once needing to account for anyone's
comfort but my own.

Today there is a hearth actually standing at the stone, and I feel the
difference in the air before I've even laid my palms against the
granite — a specific charge, expectant, the way a room feels different
once a second person has properly entered it. Even the cairn itself
seems to know something has changed, the frost along its base catching
the morning light differently than I remember it catching on any of my
sixty previous visits.

"Tell me what to expect," Aldric says, "as plainly as you can. I'd
rather know than be surprised."

"I don't fully know myself," I admit. "No wardwright's set a ward with
an actual hearth present at the stone in longer than the record shows.
I know what the rite requires, structurally. I don't know exactly what
it will feel like, to either of us, doing it properly for what may be
the first time in generations."

"Then we'll find out together," he says, simply, and steps to the
stone beside me.

I lay both hands flat against the Long Reach cairn's frost-veined
surface and call the ward the way I have called perhaps sixty others at
this exact stone — the old words, the old shape of intention, the
specific focused attention that has become, over six years, as natural
to me as breathing.

What arrives, this time, is not the stone's charge alone.

The cost leaves Aldric in a way I have only ever read about in older
wardwrights' journals, in the careful, clinical language of a rite
performed properly, with an actual hearth to draw from — heat drawn out
through his palms into the standing stone, a specific, visible
draining, and the bond, unbidden, hands me the whole of what that costs
him, not as knowledge, the way it has arrived once or twice before, but
as sensation, entire, arriving in my own chest as though the cost were
happening to me as well.

I have spent six years believing, professionally, in the price the
rite extracts. I have never once, until this exact moment, felt it
happen to a person whose face I can watch, in real time, going white to
the lips beside me.

He does not make a sound. I understand, feeling what he is feeling,
exactly how much that costs him — the specific discipline of a man who
has decided, somewhere long before he ever met me, that showing pain
is a debt he isn't willing to ask anyone else to help him pay.

I very nearly break the ward mid-cast to stop it. I do not, because I
understand, in the same instant, that stopping mid-cast would cost him
considerably worse — an incomplete rite, the stone left half-fed,
demanding to be finished properly some other day, at some other cost,
rather than concluded cleanly now.

I finish it. The ward settles into the stone the way a ward is meant to
settle, solid, load-bearing, good for another season's worth of holding
back the Rime's patient advance.

Aldric sits down hard in the snow the moment it's done, not quite a
controlled descent, and I am on my knees beside him before I've fully
decided to move.

"Now you know," he says, through it, and it is not quite a question and
not quite simply a fact either. It is closer to a confession — the
specific relief of a man who has been carrying a secret weight and has
just, for the first time, had someone else feel the actual size of it
rather than merely being told about it secondhand.

"Now I know," I agree, and do not have anything further to say for a
long moment, because I have spent six years of professional practice
believing in a cost I have just, for the first time, actually felt
arrive in a body I can put a face to, and the knowing of it has
rearranged something in my chest that I do not think is going to
rearrange itself back.

·

Tomas is waiting at the record-room door when we finally make it back
to Highstone, having clearly been watching the road for the better
part of an hour, and I understand, from his face, that he's read
something in our slow, careful walk home that tells him the inspection
did not go entirely as planned.

"What happened," he says, direct, none of his usual sideways
carefulness in the asking.

"The ward went as it should," I say. "The cost was considerably more
visible than I expected."

"How visible."

I consider how much to tell him, weighing his own safety — the
specific danger of a nineteen-year-old learning too much, too soon,
about exactly what awaits him if the crown ever does force his own
binding — against his right to understand the actual stakes of the
work he is apprenticing toward.

"Visible enough that I understand, now, precisely why the record shows
so few wardwrights setting wards with their bound hearth actually
present," I say. "It isn't simply tradition keeping the two separate.
It's mercy. For both parties."

Tomas absorbs this with the particular gravity he's shown, all
evening, watching the two of us come up the track.

"Is he going to be alright?" he asks.

"Tonight, yes," I say. "I don't know about the season."

"And you? Are you alright?"

The question catches me slightly off guard, coming from him rather than
from Aldric, and I find myself, considering it properly, uncertain how
to answer.

"I don't know that either," I admit, finally. "I've spent six years
believing I understood the whole shape of this rite's cost. I
understood, apparently, only my own half of it. Learning the other
half, this directly, has left me considerably less certain of a great
many things I thought were settled."

Tomas nods slowly, filing that away with the particular gravity of a
young man who has just been handed a piece of honesty he wasn't
entirely prepared for and has decided, regardless, to hold onto it
properly.

"Thank you for telling me the truth," he says, "instead of the managed
version."

"You're going to inherit this line eventually," I say. "I'd rather you
inherit it with a clear understanding of what it actually costs, than
with the tidy version I was handed at nineteen and spent seven years
slowly discovering was considerably incomplete."

He goes to his own quarters not long after, and I sit a while longer in
the record-room, alone, turning over the whole of what today has
taught me, before finally making my own way home to a cottage where
Aldric is already, mercifully, asleep, the specific deep, exhausted
sleep of a man whose body has spent everything it currently has to
spend and is, for tonight at least, simply grateful for the rest.

·

I do not sleep myself, that night, despite the exhaustion of the day —
the particular wired alertness that arrives after a genuine shock,
keeping the mind circling long after the body has signaled it should
properly rest.

I get up, eventually, and sit by the low fire with Ossian's own private
journal, the one I inherited alongside his official ledgers and have
never, in seven years, properly finished reading cover to cover. I find,
this particular night, a passage I do not remember reading before,
tucked between two considerably drier inspection notes, dated some
years before his death.

*I wonder, some nights,* he has written, *whether the whole discipline
of this practice — the solitary cost, the careful accounting, the
absolute refusal to let the hearth's own experience of the rite factor
into anything beyond the arithmetic — is not simply tradition, but a
specific, deliberate choice made by someone, at some point, who found it
easier to manage a line's failing cairns than to properly reckon with
what the managing costs the people actually doing it. I do not know
who made that choice. I suspect, some nights, that I have simply
inherited it without ever properly questioning whether it needed
inheriting at all.*

I read the passage three times, in the firelight, understanding, with
each read, exactly how close Ossian came, in his own private writing,
to the very discovery I am now chasing formally at Calder's Reach and
its predecessor's record. He suspected. He never had the proof, or the
nerve, or perhaps simply the time, to go looking for it properly.

I fold the journal closed, eventually, and sit a while longer with the
particular grief of understanding a dead man slightly better than I did
that morning — not simply as my teacher, but as a person who came
almost, almost close enough to the same answer I am now pursuing, and
ran out of chances to find it before the old rite finally claimed him.

·

I do not mention the journal entry to Aldric the following morning,
though I turn the decision over carefully before settling on it. It
feels, this time, different from the small managements I am trying to
unlearn — not a withholding meant to spare him something, simply a
piece of Ossian's own private grief that I am not yet certain is mine
to share with anyone, even someone I trust as completely as I have come
to trust him.

I tell Tomas instead, carefully, in general terms, over the morning's
reports.

"He knew," Tomas says, absorbing it. "Ossian. He suspected the rite
wasn't right, and he never did anything about it."

"He suspected," I say. "I don't think suspecting and knowing carry the
same weight of obligation. I think he was frightened, the way I'd
likely have been frightened, of pursuing a suspicion that could have
cost him his whole career if it came to nothing, or worse, if it came
to something the crown's own office refused to hear."

"Do you think you'll do differently?"

"I hope so," I say, honestly. "I think the difference, if there is
one, is that I'm not alone the way he was. I have you. I have Aldric.
I suspect that changes what a person's actually willing to risk,
having people alongside them rather than simply carrying a suspicion
in private, for decades, the way he apparently did."

Tomas nods, slowly, and goes back to the reports, and I sit with
Ossian's own quiet, unfinished doubt a while longer, understanding that
whatever I eventually find, wherever this suspicion leads, I owe it to
his memory to actually follow it through, properly, rather than let it
join his own journal in a drawer nobody opens again for another seven
years.

·

Aldric finds me still sitting with the journal that same evening,
considerably later than either of us usually stays at the record-room,
and settles into the chair across from me without asking permission,
which I have come, these past weeks, to recognize as his own particular
way of offering company without demanding conversation in return.

"You found something," he observes, watching my face.

"A passage," I say. "Ossian's own private doubt about the rite,
written years before either of us had any reason to think its shape
might not be the only one that ever existed. He suspected something
was wrong with it. He never followed the suspicion anywhere."

"Do you think less of him, for that?"

I consider the question properly, turning it over with more honesty
than my first instinct offers.

"No," I say, finally. "I think I understand him considerably better
for it. I think pursuing a suspicion like this, alone, with nobody to
share the risk or the eventual cost, is considerably harder than
either of us give it credit for, sitting here with the whole of Ossian's
private doubt laid out in front of us like it should have been obvious
all along."

"You're not alone, though," Aldric says. "Pursuing it."

"No," I agree. "I'm not. I think that's precisely the difference. I
don't think it's bravery on my part, chasing this further than he did.
I think it's simply that I've been handed something he never had —
people willing to chase it alongside me, rather than a solitary
suspicion I'd have to justify entirely on my own authority."

Aldric takes my hand across the table, turning it over the way he's
started doing lately, tracing the calluses six years of ward-work have
left there.

"Then we'll chase it properly," he says, "together, the way he never
got the chance to."

We sit together in the record-room's low lamplight a while longer,
Ossian's own careful hand still open on the table between us, and I
find myself, closing the journal finally for the night, considerably
more determined than I was this morning to make certain his quiet,
unfinished doubt does not go unanswered a second time.

·

Tomas asks about Ossian's journal the following morning, having
noticed it still sitting on the record-room table rather than properly
shelved.

"Did you find something in it," he asks.

"A passage," I say. "About his own private doubts regarding the rite.
I've decided to tell you about it, since you're going to inherit this
line's whole history eventually, doubts included."

I read him the relevant passage, and watch him absorb it with the same
gravity he's shown toward everything touching the line's deeper
mysteries this season.

"He suspected, and never acted," Tomas says, finally. "That's rather
sad, isn't it. Knowing something might be wrong and never having the
proper support to pursue it."

"I think it is," I say. "I think that's rather why I wanted you to
hear it directly, alongside Aldric, rather than let his doubt stay
buried in a journal nobody else properly reads."

"Thank you for trusting me with it," Tomas says, and I watch him file
the whole account away with the same careful attention he gives
everything he considers part of his proper inheritance, and I find
myself, watching him, grateful for an apprentice determined to carry
forward not simply the mechanics of this line, but its whole honest
history, doubts and all.

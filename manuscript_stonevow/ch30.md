## Chapter Thirty — The Cairn at Long Reach, Again (Isla)

We set another ward at the Long Reach cairn, ten days into Feld's
month, because the line does not pause for translations or sanctioned
deliberation, and forty miles of stone do not care whether the crown
has properly authorized whichever form of the rite currently holds
them up.

I feed the ward everything I have held back before, trying to spare
Aldric even a fraction of what the stone wants, and it is not enough,
and I know, before I've even finished the old words, that it will not
be enough, because the Long Reach cairn has been reading low for
months now and a single ward, however generously fed, was never going
to reverse an entire season's accumulated debt.

I feel the specific, sinking recognition of an arithmetic I have run
before, at other failing cairns, in other winters — the moment a
wardwright understands that no amount of personal sacrifice, however
total, is actually going to be sufficient, and that the only honest
next step is admitting the current method has simply run out of road.

Aldric's hand — the numb one, useless now for anything requiring fine
control — still presses to the stone regardless, without complaint,
without hesitation, because the rite does
not care which hand a hearth has left to offer it, only that a hand is
offered at all.

Afterward, he cannot stand without my arm under his, and neither of us
says the word FAILING, though it is the only honest word currently
available to describe what just happened.

"This can't hold through the season," I say, flat, to myself as much as
to him, doing the arithmetic I have been avoiding doing plainly since
Calder's Reach.

"Then we don't hold it the old way," Aldric says, and it is the first
time either of us has said the mutual rite's name out loud as an
actual plan rather than merely a discovery still awaiting Ossory's
consideration.

"Ossory hasn't sanctioned it yet."

"Ossory doesn't have a month," Aldric says. "We have considerably less
than that, if this cairn is any indication of how quickly the others
are going to follow it. I don't think we can afford to wait for
sanction before we're certain we're going to need it regardless."

I sit with him on the cold ground beside the cracked, exhausted stone,
and feel, for the first time since Calder's Reach, a fear that has
nothing at all to do with the crown's timeline and everything to do
with the plain, unmanageable arithmetic of a rite that may simply run
out of time to be properly tested before the old form finishes what
it's already started.

"I'm frightened," I say, "that we won't have the choice of doing this
properly. That the old rite will simply take you, one cairn at a time,
before Ossory's finished weighing whether the mutual form is safe
enough to attempt."

"Then we don't wait for him to finish weighing it," Aldric says,
quietly. "We go back to him tomorrow, and we tell him the timeline has
changed, and we ask him to decide faster than he's comfortable
deciding, because comfortable isn't a luxury either of us currently
has."

I help him up, eventually, once he can stand on his own again, and we
walk back from the Long Reach cairn slower than either of us would
like, his weight leaning more heavily on my shoulder than either of us
is willing to name aloud, and I understand, walking that slow, quiet
mile, that whatever careful deliberation any of us had hoped to afford
this decision has just, this evening, run considerably shorter than any
of us accounted for.

·

Sela is waiting at the cottage when we finally arrive, having heard,
somehow — the village's grapevine again, faster than any official
report could travel — that something had gone wrong at the Long Reach
cairn, and I watch her take in the whole of Aldric's condition with the
specific controlled fear of a sister who has spent her entire life
being the one who copes, regardless of how frightened she actually is
underneath the coping.

"How bad," she asks, direct, no preamble.

"Bad enough that we can't wait for Ossory's usual pace any longer," I
say. "I'm going back to him tomorrow, to press for a faster decision.
I wanted you to know, before I go, exactly how urgent it's actually
become."

Sela sits with Aldric a long while that evening, the two of them
talking quietly in a register I don't fully intrude on, giving them the
space I suspect a lifetime of shared history has earned, and it is only
once Aldric has finally gone to sleep, exhausted past the point of
staying properly conscious, that Sela finds me alone by the fire.

"I want to help," she says. "Whatever you need tomorrow, at Ossory's
hall — testimony, evidence, simply a witness who can speak to what
this has already cost our family. I'm not willing to sit at the orchard
waiting for news any longer than I have to."

"I'd welcome that," I say, honestly. "I think Ossory takes the whole
matter more seriously with the family's own voice in the room,
alongside the wardwright's."

"Then I'll be there," Sela says, with the specific steel I've come to
recognize as entirely her own, distinct from Aldric's quieter version
of the same stubbornness. "I've spent this whole winter watching from
the outside while the two of you carried the actual weight of it. I'm
not doing that tomorrow. Whatever this costs, I want to be properly
inside it, standing with my own family, rather than simply hearing
about the outcome secondhand."

I don't have a ready answer for the specific fierceness of her
declaration, so I simply nod, grateful, and the two of us sit together
by the dying fire a while longer, two women who have each, in their own
way, learned this winter exactly how much easier a burden becomes once
it's properly shared rather than carried in careful, well-meaning
isolation.

·

Tomas joins us before the fire burns down entirely, having heard,
through whatever channel now reliably keeps him informed of every
significant development at Highstone, that the Long Reach cairn had
cracked.

"I want to come with you tomorrow," he says, to me, direct, no
hesitation in the asking. "To Ossory's hall. Not simply to wait here for
news."

"It's not your burden to carry, Tomas."

"It is, though," he says, with a firmness I haven't heard from him
before. "You've told me I'm the next wardwright of this line. That
means it's my line too, eventually, and I'd rather understand properly
how this crisis gets resolved than simply inherit whatever's left of it
once the two of you have already decided everything without me."

I look at Sela, who nods, and understand, between the two of them,
that I am considerably outnumbered in my instinct to protect Tomas from
the whole of tomorrow's uncertainty.

"Alright," I say. "You come. But you listen, properly, and you let
Aldric and me speak for ourselves in the actual hearing, whatever
Ossory decides to hold."

"Agreed," Tomas says, satisfied, and the three of us — soon to be four,
once Aldric wakes properly in the morning — sit together by the fire a
while longer, a whole small household drawn close around the same
shared fear, refusing, together, to let any single member of it face
tomorrow alone.

·

Tomas lingers after Sela has gone up to check on Aldric, turning
something over in his hands that I recognize, after a moment, as the
small carved bird Aldric started for him months ago and never quite
finished.

"He gave me this half-done, back in the autumn," Tomas says, "and told
me he'd finish it once the season slowed down enough to properly sit
with the knife again. I don't think either of us believed, saying it,
that the season was ever actually going to slow down."

"It hasn't, has it," I say. "Not once, this whole winter."

"No," Tomas agrees. "I keep it anyway. Half-finished. I think I like it
better this way, honestly, though I wouldn't have said so a few months
ago. It reminds me that some things are worth keeping even unfinished,
rather than only the tidy, completed version of them."

"That's a wardwright's instinct," I say, "whether you've fully claimed
the title yet or not. Most of what we hold on this line is exactly
that — unfinished, imperfect, kept anyway because the keeping matters
more than the completing."

Tomas turns the little bird over once more before setting it carefully
on the mantel, where I notice, for the first time, he has apparently
been keeping it in plain view rather than tucked away in some private
drawer.

"I want him to finish it properly," Tomas says, quieter now, the
bravado of the earlier conversation with Sela fully set aside, "once
all of this is actually behind us. I'm not ready for it to stay
half-done permanently. I just needed to say, out loud, that I could
bear it if it did."

"I understand," I say, and mean it more than the simple words properly
carry, understanding exactly the particular courage it takes to name a
fear plainly rather than let it manage itself quietly in the dark.

"Do you think he'll survive whatever tomorrow decides," Tomas asks,
finally, the question he has clearly been circling since he first
arrived at the cottage door tonight.

I do not have a comforting answer ready, and I have decided, this
whole difficult season, that honesty costs less in the long run than
whatever comfortable lie might be available in the moment.

"I don't know," I say. "I think our chances are better than they were
a week ago, and considerably worse than either of us would like. I
think the honest answer is that I don't know, and that not knowing is
something we're all going to have to hold together, rather than
pretend certainty either of us doesn't actually have."

Tomas nods slowly, accepting the answer's honesty even though it
plainly isn't the reassurance he'd hoped for, and I find myself,
watching him, grateful for a young man who has learned, somewhere in
these past months, to prefer a difficult truth over an easy comfort.

"Thank you," he says, "for not pretending."

"Thank you for not needing me to," I say, and the two of us sit a
while longer in the banked firelight, saying nothing further, simply
keeping each other company against a fear that neither of us can
properly resolve alone tonight.

·

I do not sleep, that night, despite the exhaustion of the previous
day's crisis, my mind circling the whole shape of what tomorrow might
demand of all of us. I find myself, near dawn, going through Ossian's
old journal one final time, searching for anything I might have missed
— some fragment of the mutual form's mechanics that his own private
doubt might have preserved, even incompletely.

I find nothing new. But I find, rereading his careful, economical
hand one more time, a measure of comfort I hadn't expected — the
specific companionship of a predecessor who wondered the same
questions I am now asking, even if he never found the nerve, or the
proof, to act on them himself.

"I'm going to finish what you started," I say, quietly, to the
journal, to the empty record-room, to whatever remains of a man who
taught me everything I know and never once let me see how much he
himself was carrying. "Whatever it costs. I promise you that."

I fold the journal closed, finally, and go to check on Aldric, finding
him asleep, properly, for the first time since the cairn cracked,
Sela keeping her own quiet vigil in the chair beside him. I settle in
alongside her, and the two of us wait out the last hours before dawn
together, two women bound by the same fierce love for the same man,
neither of us willing to face the coming day anywhere but exactly
where we currently are.

·

"Can I ask you something," Sela says, quietly, somewhere in the
deepest, stillest part of the pre-dawn hours, "that I've been meaning
to ask since the Long Reach cairn cracked."

"Go on."

"Do you regret it," she asks. "Any of it. The binding, the mutual
form, the whole arrangement the rite handed you without asking whether
you wanted it. I know what you'd say if I asked you plainly, in
daylight, with everyone listening. I'm asking now, in the dark, because
I want the answer you'd give if nobody else were listening at all."

I consider the question properly, in the particular honest quiet the
hour seems to grant.

"No," I say, finally. "I've been frightened, considerably, more than
I've let show most days. I've done arithmetic on outcomes I don't like
to examine directly. But I don't regret it. I don't think I've ever,
in six years of careful, solitary practice, felt as thoroughly alive as
I have these past weeks, frightened and exhausted and uncertain as
they've been."

"That's not a small thing to say," Sela says, "about a rite that very
nearly killed him tonight."

"I know," I say. "I think that's rather the whole complicated shape of
it. The fear and the aliveness aren't opposites. I've learned that,
this winter, more than almost anything else."

Sela is quiet a long moment, considering this.

"I think I understand that better than I expected to," she says,
eventually. "I've spent this whole crisis frightened out of my mind,
and also, somehow, more properly connected to my own family than I've
felt in years. I don't think I'd have predicted that combination, going
into this winter."

"Neither would I," I agree, and we sit together the rest of the night,
two women holding the same complicated truth, waiting out the dark
until whatever the coming day demands finally arrives.

·

Tomas finds us there, near dawn, having risen early himself, unable to
properly sleep, and settles quietly on the floor beside our two chairs
rather than disturb the room's particular stillness.

"I couldn't sleep either," he admits, when Sela glances at him.

"None of us managed much of that, tonight," Sela says.

"I keep thinking about what I'd say to Ossory," Tomas says, "if he asks
me anything directly. I want to make sure I've got the translation's
provenance properly memorized, in case he questions any specific
detail."

"You'll be fine," I say. "You've prepared more thoroughly than anyone
could reasonably ask of you."

"I know," Tomas says. "I think I simply need to keep rehearsing it
anyway, the same way you're clearly not sleeping either, doing whatever
arithmetic keeps circling in your own head."

"That's fair," I admit, and the three of us sit together in the
cottage's pre-dawn quiet, each holding our own private version of the
same nervous anticipation, none of us alone in the particular vigil of
waiting for a morning none of us can properly predict the shape of.

·

Aldric wakes properly not long before dawn, finding the three of us
still gathered, and takes in the scene with visible tenderness.

"You've all been keeping vigil," he observes.

"None of us slept much," I admit.

"Neither did I," he says, "though I think I managed rather more rest
than the rest of you, given the circumstances." He settles in among
us, taking my hand. "Whatever today brings, I want it said properly,
before we walk into that hall: I'm grateful, more than I know how to
say, for every single person currently sitting in this room."

"We feel the same," Sela says, and Tomas nods his own fierce agreement,
and the four of us sit together in the last of the night's darkness,
considerably more ready to face whatever the coming day demands, having
spent its final hours properly together rather than each alone with
private fear.

## Chapter Two — Cairns and Debts (Isla)

Down into the cold, quiet record-room, early, well before the sun's even properly up, where the ledger of every cairn on the
northern line hangs on the wall in a dead man's handwriting I have never
once had copied over into my own, on the theory that copying it would
mean admitting, formally, finally, that his hand is not going to write in it
again.

Forty-one cairns standing. Forty miles of line. Ossian Thale's
handwriting listing every stone by name — the Long Reach cairn, the
Hollow Wall cairn, the seven small waystones between Highstone and the
first proper marker — in the specific cramped, economical hand of a man
who never once wasted ink on a word that didn't need spending.

I have kept that arithmetic since I was twenty-six years old, the year
he walked into the cold and left me the only wardwright the crown was
willing to fund for a line that size, and it has never once, in five
years of keeping it, come out even.

Tomas Renn has the kettle on already, and the day's reports sorted by
urgency in three neat stacks on the workbench, the way he always does,
whether or not anyone has actually asked him to. He is nineteen, all
elbows and unfinished height, with the specific gravity of a young man
who has decided, somewhere in his own private arithmetic, that being
useful before he's asked is the safest available version of himself to
be.

"They bound you to the orchard-keeper," he says, careful, watching my
face for whatever shape my feelings about that have decided to take.
He has learned, over three years of apprenticeship, to read my face for
information it isn't otherwise offering, which is either the best or
the worst thing I have taught him, and I have not yet decided which.

"They bound me to a name and a hearth," I say. "The rest is paperwork."

I do not believe this even as I say it. I have never once, in six years
of running this exact defense against every uncomfortable fact that
arrives in my life, managed to make it sound convincing to Tomas
specifically, who has watched me deploy it against considerably smaller
facts than a rite-binding and clock every single failure.

"You don't have to pretend it's nothing, with me," he says, pouring the
tea without being asked, milk first, the way I've always preferred it
and never once explained why. "I do the ledgers. I know what a hearth
costs, structurally. I'd just like to know how you're actually doing
about it, separately from the structure."

"I'm doing the arithmetic," I say, which is, for me, the closest
available approximation of an honest answer.

Tomas doesn't push further. He has learned that too, over three years —
which questions I will actually answer if given room, and which ones
simply need to be asked once, gently, and then left alone to do their
work in private. It is, I think, watching him settle into his own chair
with his own tea and his own stack of reports, the single most valuable
thing I have ever taught anyone, and I did not teach it to him on
purpose. He simply watched me long enough to learn the shape of what I
actually needed, the way apprentices learn most of what actually
matters — sideways, from observation, rather than from anything I ever
said outright.

"The Long Reach cairn's reading low again," he says, changing the
subject to the one that will always, reliably, get my full attention.
"Third time this month. I checked the charge myself yesterday, before
you ask — it's not a fault in the reading. The stone's actually
weakening."

I take the report from him and read it twice, the numbers doing the
thing numbers do when they've already told you something you didn't
want confirmed a second time: confirming it anyway, patiently, without
apology.

"How much of the line is reading low," I ask.

"Six cairns, properly low. Another nine trending that way, if the
pattern holds through Coldwake the way it held through the last two
winters." He hesitates, the specific hesitation of a young man about to
say something he suspects will land badly and has decided to say
anyway, because saying it is his job. "You've been feeding some of them
yourself, haven't you. Outside the rite proper."

I do not answer that directly. I have not answered it directly to
anyone in two years, and I am not going to start with a nineteen-year-old
who has enough of his own arithmetic to do without adding mine to the
pile.

"The rite provides what the rite provides," I say, which is not a lie,
technically, in the specific way that most of the things I say to Tomas
about my own condition are not lies, technically, while being
considerably less than the whole truth.

He lets it go. He always lets it go, eventually, once he's registered
that I've heard the question and chosen, deliberately, not to answer
it — which is, I understand, its own kind of answer, the loudest one
available to a man who never once, in three years, has managed to get a
straight admission of weakness out of the woman training him to run
this line after her.

"What was he like," Tomas asks instead, meaning Aldric, in the careful
sideways way he asks most of the questions he actually wants answered.
"The name they read."

"Warm," I say, and am surprised, saying it, by how much the single word
costs me to admit out loud, in a record-room built entirely out of a
dead man's careful economy with ink.

"That's not an answer about what he's like. That's an answer about a
fact."

"It's the only fact I currently have," I say, which is, this once,
entirely true, and Tomas, wisely, chooses not to push any further into
territory neither of us has the proper equipment to survey yet.

·

The morning's business runs its ordinary course after that — three
reports from the outer waystones, a request from the crown's grain
office that I sign without reading closely, because I have learned,
over the years, exactly which of the crown's requests are worth the ink
it would cost me to actually argue with them — and it is nearly midday
before I let myself think about the man bound to my wrist for a second
consecutive hour.

I go, eventually, to the workshop window that looks out over the road
below Highstone, on the pretext of checking the weather, which is not,
strictly, a pretext, since the weather genuinely does need checking
before any cairn inspection this afternoon. I tell myself this several
times, in several different orders, while I stand at the glass longer
than any weather report has ever required of me.

He is not on the road. Of course he is not on the road; he has his own
orchard to run, his own sister's grain-count to keep straight, his own
entire life that existed in perfectly good order before the rite
decided it needed a second occupant.

I go back to my reports. I do not, for the rest of the morning, check
the window again, on the grounds that checking it a second time would
require admitting to myself that the first check was never actually
about the weather at all.

## A Stranger's Weight

Aldric finds me on the road below Highstone in the early afternoon,
which means either he has come looking or the errand that brought him
this direction was, itself, some manner of pretext I am not currently in
a position to accuse anyone else of running, given the morning I have
just had at my own window.

He has grafting wax in a satchel over one shoulder and the specific
unhurried gait of a man who does not need to arrive anywhere faster than
his own legs are willing to carry him, which I notice, watching him
approach, is already unlike most of the men I have dealt with
professionally, all of whom arrive at everything as though being late
were a personal failing rather than an occasional accident of weather.

"I didn't expect you on this road," I say, by way of greeting, which is
not quite an accusation and not quite not one either.

"I didn't expect to be on it," he says, entirely unbothered by the
half-accusation. "Sela needed wax fetched from the Highstone market.
I offered, mostly because I wanted to see whether you'd still be
standing here, doing whatever it is wardwrights do to a perfectly
ordinary road at midday."

"Reading weather," I say.

"Is that what you were doing at the workshop window an hour ago too?"

I do not have a good answer to that, mostly because I do not have any
answer to it that doesn't involve admitting he clocked me watching for
him before I had fully admitted it to myself, so I change the subject
with the specific bluntness I reserve for facts I intend to establish
early and permanently.

"I run the list on everyone the rite could plausibly have bound me to,"
I say. "I ran it before the assembly, the way I run every list before
a decision gets made without my input. You weren't on it. I didn't know
your name until Ossory read it."

"I know," Aldric says. "I made sure of that, actually. I asked the
assessors not to tell either of us who the other one was, ahead of
time. I didn't want you meeting a name in a ledger before you met the
person the name belonged to."

I consider this for longer than the sentence, on its face, seems to
warrant. It is, I realize, turning it over, the first genuinely
considerate thing anyone connected to the crown's rite-office has done
in the whole five years I've been dealing with them, and it arrived
from the one person in the entire arrangement who had no professional
obligation to consider my comfort at all.

"That's not how the assessors usually operate," I say.

"No," Aldric agrees. "I asked for a favour. I don't ask for many. It
made this one easier to grant, apparently."

We walk together for a while without any particular destination, the
way two people walk when neither has decided yet whether the
conversation is over, past the last standing waystone before the road
properly climbs toward the orchard line, and I find myself, against
every professional instinct I have cultivated for six years, doing the
thing I do to every person I am trying to understand: running the cold
list, item by item, out loud this time rather than silently.

"You're twenty-eight," I say. "You run the last orchard above the line.
Your sister keeps the grain record and should, by the exemption rolls
as they stood two winters ago, have had her own name protected without
your binding needing to cover it at all."

"The crown tightened the terms," Aldric says, exactly as he said it the
night before, in exactly the same flat register, which tells me it is
not a story he tells differently depending on the audience — a small,
useful fact, the kind I collect about people the way other wardwrights
collect cairn measurements. "Sela would have died on the roll instead
of off it. I had a name available to spend instead. It wasn't
complicated, from where I was standing."

"Most decisions that size are complicated from somewhere," I say.

"Maybe," he allows. "I've found it's usually simpler once you've
decided which direction you're willing to be uncomplicated in." He
glances at me, sideways, in the specific way of a man testing whether a
sentence has landed the way he intended it to. "You run cold on
purpose, you told me last night. I've been turning that over since.
I don't think I fully believe it's only a professional habit."

"It's a professional habit," I say, which is true, and also, I
suspect, listening to my own voice say it, considerably less than the
whole of what's actually true.

"I didn't say it wasn't a habit," Aldric says. "I said I don't think
it's only that." He does not push further. He simply lets the sentence
sit on the road between us, the way he let his own admission about
Sela sit last night, offered plainly and then handed over entirely,
without any further attempt to manage how I receive it.

I do not have an answer for that which doesn't risk telling him
considerably more than a single day's acquaintance has earned him, so I
do not attempt one. I run the rest of my cold list instead, silently
this time — what he can be trusted with, what he is likely to cost me,
whether I can afford, on any of the ledgers I actually keep, to let a
stranger become a person rather than merely an office the rite has
assigned me — and reach, by the time the road forks toward Highstone's
gate, no answer that holds up under its own weight.

"I should get back," I say. "The Long Reach cairn wants checking before
the light goes."

"I'll walk you as far as the fork," Aldric says, which is not quite an
offer to accompany me the rest of the way and not quite not one either,
and I let him, mostly because I have not yet worked out a version of
myself that knows how to say no to small, ordinary kindnesses without
it costing more than simply accepting them would.

We part at the fork with nothing resolved and nothing, strictly,
needing to be resolved yet. I walk the rest of the way to the cairn
alone, in the particular quiet that arrives once a conversation has
ended and the mind keeps right on having it anyway, turning each
sentence over for a seam I haven't found yet, and do not find one,
the whole cold walk back.

·

The Long Reach cairn reads within tolerance, which is its own small
mercy after a day spent doing arithmetic on considerably less
manageable things, and I take longer over the inspection than the
reading strictly requires, mostly because the cairn field at dusk has
always been the one place I can properly think without anyone's
expectations pressing in on the thinking.

I have stood at this exact stone the evening after every significant
change in my adult life — the evening after my apprenticeship began,
the evening after Ossian's funeral, the evening I finally accepted that
no wardwright before me had ever managed to hold this line without
losing at least one cairn a season to the Rime's patient advance. It
seems fitting, standing here now, that the evening of my binding should
join that particular list.

I think about Reny Ashe's herbs, still folded in my coat pocket. I
think about the specific weight of Aldric's hand catching mine as I
nearly went down on a patch of ice three days before any of this began,
an ordinary catch that meant nothing at the time and has, somehow, in
retrospect, taken on the shape of the first evidence of a pattern I
did not yet know I was watching for.

Tomas finds me there, eventually, having correctly guessed that I would
linger longer than the inspection required.

"You've been standing here an hour," he says, not quite a question.

"The cairn needed watching," I say, which is not, strictly, true, and
Tomas, wisely, does not challenge the lie directly.

"How are you finding him," Tomas asks instead, careful, in the specific
sideways way he asks most of his real questions. "Properly. Not the
professional answer."

I consider the question for longer than it probably warrants, watching
the last of the light go out of the sky over the standing stones.

"Considerate," I say, finally. "In a way I didn't expect a stranger to
be, this early. He mended my glove without being asked and hasn't once
mentioned it since, as though mentioning it would somehow diminish the
mending."

"That sounds like a good sign," Tomas says.

"It sounds like a great many things I haven't finished sorting through
yet," I say, which is, I suspect, considerably closer to honest than
the tidy version I'd have given him a fortnight ago.

We walk back to Highstone together in the last of the light, Tomas
peppering me with the ordinary questions of an apprentice eager for
the day's remaining lessons, and I find myself grateful, walking beside
him, for the particular steadiness of a routine that has not, despite
everything shifting underneath it, changed in any way that matters.

·

Tomas's own history, I am reminded that evening, watching him settle
into his usual chair with his usual careful economy, is considerably
more complicated than the steady young man currently sorting reports
would suggest to anyone meeting him fresh.

He came to Highstone at sixteen, an orphan of the outer waystones —
his own parents lost to a cold-sickness outbreak the winter before,
one of the smaller tragedies the line exacts regularly enough that the
crown's own records barely note it beyond a single administrative line.
Ossian took him in as an apprentice not out of any particular need for
a second hand, at the time, but because the alternative, Ossian told
me once, was watching a capable boy go to whatever thin charity the
crown's outer settlements could otherwise offer him.

"Do you remember much of them," I ask him, this particular evening,
apropos of nothing beyond the general softness the day's events have
left in the room. "Your parents."

"Some," Tomas says, without the particular flinch such questions
sometimes provoke in people who'd rather not examine an old grief in
company. "My mother kept bees, of all things, on the last stretch of
land before the cold made keeping them impossible. My father worked
the waystones same as everyone out there, minor maintenance mostly,
nothing as significant as what you do."

"That's not a small thing, maintaining a waystone."

"No," Tomas agrees, "I don't suppose it is. I didn't understand that,
at sixteen. I understand it rather better now, doing the work myself."

"Do you miss them?"

"Every day," Tomas says, plainly, "though it's gotten easier to carry,
these past three years. Ossian was kind to me, in his own quiet way. You
have been too, though I don't think either of you would call it
kindness outright — you'd both call it simply doing the job properly."

"Perhaps that's what kindness actually is," I say, "most of the time.
Simply doing the job properly, with someone else's actual wellbeing
folded quietly into the doing of it."

Tomas considers that for a long moment, and then nods, slowly,
accepting the observation the way he accepts most of what I offer him
that isn't strictly instructional — carefully, and with considerably
more gravity than his age would suggest he ought to be capable of.

·

We stop, on the way back into Highstone proper, at the small kitchen
that serves the keep's own remaining staff, where the cook — a
broad, weathered woman named Ida who has fed three generations of
wardwrights without once, as far as I know, missing a single meal's
proper timing — greets Tomas with the specific affection she reserves
for anyone she considers, functionally, one of her own children.

"You've heard, I take it," Ida says to me, setting down two bowls of
the evening's stew without being asked. "The whole keep's been talking
of nothing else."

"I'd assumed as much," I say.

"He's a good one," Ida says, plainly, meaning Aldric, with the same
directness Reny Ashe showed at the gate. "I've fed him at market twice
a year for a decade. Always thanks the stall properly. Always pays
even when the trader tries to wave the debt off out of respect for the
orchard's hard luck. That's not nothing, Wardwright, in a man."

"I'm gathering that," I say, "from rather a lot of people, rather
quickly."

"Good," Ida says, entirely unbothered by my dry tone, and goes back to
her kitchen, leaving Tomas and me to our stew.

"Everyone in this village seems to have an opinion about my binding,"
I say, once she's gone.

"Everyone in this village has been watching you run yourself thin for
six years," Tomas says, with a bluntness that surprises me slightly,
coming from him. "I think they're simply relieved someone's finally
going to make you slow down, whether or not you intend to let them."

I don't have a ready response to that, so I simply eat my stew, and let
the specific, considerable warmth of an entire village's quiet approval
settle somewhere I haven't previously made room for it.

·

Ida returns partway through the meal with a second helping neither of
us asked for, setting it down in front of Tomas with the particular
brisk affection of a woman who has decided a growing apprentice's
appetite is not a matter open to negotiation.

"Eat properly," she says, "you've been thin all winter, and I won't
have it said I let Ossian's boy go hungry on my watch." She turns to
me before I can protest on his behalf. "And you, Wardwright — I want it
understood that my kitchen's always open, whatever this binding turns
out to require of you. A body that spends itself the way yours does
needs feeding properly, regardless of how little time you generally
allow yourself to sit down and do it."

"I manage," I say, which is, I understand even saying it, precisely
the kind of answer Ida has clearly heard from me a hundred times
before and stopped believing considerably before tonight.

"You manage," Ida agrees, with the specific dry patience of a woman old
enough to have outlasted several wardwrights' particular brand of
stubbornness, "the same way a roof manages, right up until the one
winter it doesn't. I'm not asking you to stop managing. I'm simply
telling you there's a kitchen here that'll feed you properly, whenever
you're finally ready to let it."

I don't have a ready argument against that kind of plain, practical
kindness, so I simply thank her, and she goes back to her stove
satisfied, and Tomas, watching the whole exchange with poorly disguised
amusement, waits until she's properly out of earshot before speaking.

"She's right, you know," he says. "About the managing."

"I'm aware," I say. "I don't need it confirmed by my own apprentice on
top of the cook."

"I'm simply reporting the data," Tomas says, echoing, though he
doesn't yet know it, a phrase he'll use on me again in a few weeks'
time about considerably more embarrassing evidence, and we finish the
meal together in the kitchen's warm, ordinary bustle, and I find
myself, walking back out into the evening cold afterward, considerably
more fed — in every sense the word can properly carry — than I've
allowed myself to be in longer than I care to examine.

## Chapter Twenty-Six — Morning, Unhidden (Aldric)

Morning finds us unhurried, for once — the specific rare luxury of
neither of us having anywhere urgent to be, the fire built back up
early, tea brewing, the whole cottage quiet in a way that feels,
finally, like something closer to peace than to a truce between two
people managing an arrangement neither chose, the frost outside the
window catching the very first proper light of day.

I watch Isla move through the small kitchen with an ease I've come to
treasure these past weeks — humming, faintly, something tuneless she
doesn't seem to notice she's doing, the particular unguarded quiet of
a woman who has, somewhere in this arrangement, stopped performing
competence and started simply living inside her own ordinary morning.
I think, watching her, that whatever this winter still has waiting for
either of us, I would not trade this exact quiet hour for any version
of my life that didn't include it.

I reach for the cup, and my whole left hand simply does not close.

Not the partial failure I've been managing for weeks now, two fingers
gone, a third going — the entire hand, all at once, refusing to answer
at all, and the cup falls, breaks against the hearthstone a second
time, and I look at my own hand the way you look at a stranger's,
uncomprehending, before the specific cold horror of what's just
happened properly arrives.

Isla is already moving before either of us has decided anything.

"How long has it been the whole hand," she says, and there is no room
in her voice, this time, for me to manage the answer smaller than it
actually is.

"A week," I admit, finally, all of it, the whole truth I have been
managing in careful pieces for considerably longer than either of us
has properly acknowledged. "Maybe longer. I stopped counting precisely
because counting wasn't going to change the number, and I didn't want
to watch the number get worse in real time."

"You promised me there wouldn't be a second time," Isla says, and I
understand, hearing the specific quality of hurt underneath the anger,
that I have broken something considerably more fragile than a simple
agreement about honesty.

The hurt lands harder than any anger could have, precisely because I
recognize it — the same wounded disbelief I felt, once, discovering my
own father had hidden the true state of his health from Sela and me
until there was no longer any hiding it left to do.

"I know," I say. "I'm sorry. I kept telling myself I'd say something
once I was certain how bad it actually was, and the certainty kept
arriving in worse increments than I was prepared to hand you."

Isla sits with me on the cold floor, among the second broken cup, and I
watch her do the arithmetic I've been avoiding doing plainly, in front
of her, for a full week — the actual, undeniable shape of how much time
we might genuinely have left, if the numbness has taken the whole hand
this quickly, following whatever pattern it's been following since the
Long Reach cairn cracked something in me that hasn't properly healed
since.

I watch her face do the specific thing it does when she's converted a
fear into a task — the fastest way, I've learned, that she survives
anything genuinely frightening, turning terror into an arithmetic
problem she can properly work rather than simply feel.

"I need the truth now," Isla says. "The whole of it. Not managed. Not
softened for my sake. What does this actually mean, for how much time
we have to make the mutual rite work?"

"I don't know precisely," I say, honestly, because I have decided,
watching her face, that further management costs more now than the
truth does, however frightening the truth turns out to be. "I know
it's considerably less time than either of us was budgeting for a
fortnight ago. I know the numbness is spreading faster than the record
from Calder's Reach gives us any comfortable margin to account for."

Isla doesn't cry, exactly, though something in her face does something
close to it — the specific controlled devastation of a woman who has
spent her whole life managing grief before it fully arrives, watching
this particular grief refuse, entirely, to wait for her permission.

"Whatever time we thought we had," she says, finally, "it was never as
much as I was budgeting for either."

I do not have a comforting answer to that, because there isn't one
available, honestly, and I have decided, this morning, sitting on a
cold floor with a hand that no longer properly answers to me, that
honesty is the only currency either of us can afford to keep spending,
regardless of how much it costs either one of us to hand over.

"We go to Ossory," I say. "Today. We stop managing this quietly and we
put it in front of someone who can actually help us test whether the
mutual rite holds, before either of us runs out of the time we'd need
to find out properly."

"Today," Isla agrees, and helps me up off the cold floor, and I feel,
standing beside her, the specific, undeniable understanding that
whatever time we thought we had to manage this carefully has just, this
morning, run out entirely.

I do not, standing there with the broken cup's pieces still scattered
around both our feet, feel afraid in the way I expected fear to feel,
imagining this exact moment in whatever quiet corner of my mind has
been dreading it since the numbness first began. I feel, instead,
something closer to relief — the specific, unburdening relief of a
man who has finally run out of road for managing a thing alone, and
finds, arriving at the end of that road, that the company waiting
there is worth more than the managing ever was.

·

We stop at the orchard on the way to Ossory's hall, because I am not
willing to let Sela hear about the whole hand secondhand, from
whichever version of the story reaches her through the village's own
particular grapevine, and because I find, standing at her door, that I
want my sister to hear this from me directly, the way I should have let
her hear everything else about this whole arrangement from the start.

She takes one look at my face and knows, before I've said a word,
that whatever's happened is considerably worse than anything I've told
her so far.

"Show me," she says, and I do, and she goes very still, the specific
stillness of a woman absorbing a fact she has clearly been dreading
since the first time I mentioned the numbness at all.

"How long," she asks.

"Weeks, the healer thinks. Possibly fewer."

Sela sits down hard on the grain-house step, and for a moment I think
she might simply refuse to hear any more of it, the way our father used
to refuse to hear bad news until he'd had time to properly compose
himself first. She doesn't. She looks up at me, and at Isla standing
beside me, and asks the plain question, in the specific direct register
she's clearly decided, somewhere in the last several breaths, to adopt
permanently.

"What do you need from me. Right now. Not eventually. Today."

"I need you to trust that Isla and I are handling this properly," I
say, "and I need you to let us handle it, rather than trying to solve
it yourself the way you tried to solve the grain shortfall alone, two
winters back."

Sela's mouth tightens, recognizing the echo, and I watch her decide,
visibly, to accept the correction rather than argue against it.

"Alright," she says. "I'll trust it. But I want to be told everything,
from now on, as it happens, not gathered secondhand or handed to me
only once you've already decided how much I'm allowed to worry about
it."

"Agreed," I say, and mean it, understanding that I owe her this much,
at minimum, after a lifetime of deciding, unilaterally, exactly how
much of my own difficulty she was permitted to carry alongside me.

I think, watching her absorb the news with more steadiness than I have
any right to expect from her, about every other time I've handed her a
crisis already halfway managed, already softened, already decided —
and understand, properly, for perhaps the first time, exactly how much
that particular habit has cost her, over the years, in ways I never
once thought to ask about.

She hugs me, fiercely, before we leave for Ossory's hall, the kind of
embrace we haven't properly exchanged since before our mother died, and
I walk the rest of the way to Highstone considerably steadied by it,
understanding that whatever the day ahead demands of either Isla or me,
I am, for the first time in longer than I can properly place, not
facing it with my whole family kept carefully, quietly, at arm's
length.

I think, walking, about all the other mornings I might have let her
this close, over the years, and didn't — every hard season managed
quietly, every difficulty absorbed alone rather than properly shared —
and understand, with a clarity that costs something to hold, exactly
how much unnecessary distance I built into this family simply by
believing distance was the same thing as strength.

·

Sela insists on sending word to Tomas herself, rather than leaving that
particular task to us, and catches up with us halfway down the track,
having apparently run the whole distance to say one further thing
before we reach Highstone properly.

"I want you both to know something," she says, slightly breathless,
"before whatever happens in that hall today. Whatever Ossory decides,
however this turns out — I don't regret a single day of the two winters
your exemption cost this family. I want that said plainly, in case
either of you has spent any part of today quietly believing this
crisis is somehow a debt you owe for choices made back then."

"I hadn't thought of it that way," I admit, though I understand,
hearing her say it, that some quiet part of me had, in fact, been
carrying exactly that unspoken arithmetic.

"I know you hadn't consciously," Sela says. "I know you well enough to
suspect it was there anyway, underneath. I wanted to say it before it
had the chance to take root any further."

"Thank you," I say, and mean it, understanding, not for the first
time this winter, exactly how much my sister sees in me that I fail to
see in myself.

She turns back toward the orchard, and Isla and I continue on toward
Highstone, considerably lighter, both of us, for Sela's plain,
unmanaged reassurance.

·

We walk the rest of the way in a silence that isn't quite comfortable,
both of us turning over, privately, the specific weight of what's
waiting at Highstone, and I find myself watching Isla's face for
whatever sign might tell me how frightened she actually is underneath
the composed, purposeful stride she's kept up since the cup broke this
morning.

"You've gone quiet," I say, eventually.

"I'm rehearsing," she admits. "What I'm going to say to Ossory. I've
said the actual words to you, once, in the dark, and to no one else in
six years. I find the prospect of saying them to someone with the
standing to act on them considerably more frightening than I expected
it to be."

"Would it help to say them to me again first? A rehearsal before the
rehearsal."

She considers this, walking, and I watch her decide, visibly, that it
might.

"The line's oldest form wasn't broken," she says, quietly, testing the
words the way you test ice before trusting your full weight to it.
"It was corrupted, deliberately, by rite-keepers who found it easier
to spend one hearth than share the cost between two wardwrights loudly.
I have a record. I have proof it worked, once, before it was buried.
I want to attempt it, properly, before the numbness in your hand
finishes what the corrupted form has been quietly doing to this whole
line for generations."

"That's clearer than anything I could have said myself," I tell her,
"and considerably braver." I mean it entirely, watching her walk
beside me with the words finally settled properly in her own mouth,
considerably steadier for having said them once already, out loud, to
someone who already believes them.

"It's easier to say to you," Isla admits. "You already believe it.
Ossory hasn't decided anything yet, and I don't know which way he's
going to fall once I've said it in that hall, in front of witnesses,
where the saying actually matters."

"Whatever way he falls," I say, "you'll have said it plainly, which is
already more than most people manage with something this frightening.
I'll be standing right beside you for the whole of it."

"I know," Isla says, and something in her shoulders eases, slightly,
hearing it stated so plainly. "I think that's the only reason I'm
still walking toward that hall rather than turning back toward the
cottage to rehearse it another dozen times first."

We walk the last stretch to Highstone together, her rehearsed words
settling somewhere steadier in her chest than they were a few minutes
ago, and I understand, watching her, that whatever Ossory ultimately
decides, she has already done the harder part — deciding, finally, to
say the true thing out loud, to someone who isn't already convinced of
it.

·

We pass the small market stall near Highstone's gate on the way in,
and Reny Ashe catches sight of us both, setting down whatever she's
currently wrapping to properly look at Isla's face.

"Something's different today," Reny observes, not quite a question.

"We found something," Isla says, careful not to say more than that,
"at Calder's Reach. I'm taking it to Ossory now."

"Good," Reny says, with her usual finality. "I've watched this
wardwright hold this line practically alone for six years. I'd like to
think whatever you've found finally changes that particular
arithmetic."

"So would I," Isla says, and Reny presses a fresh twist of herbs into
her hand, unbidden, the same small ritual she's performed since the
night of the binding.

"For steadying the nerves," Reny says, "whatever you're about to walk
in and say. I don't need to know what it is to recognize the specific
look of someone about to say a frightening true thing to someone who
might not want to hear it."

"Thank you," Isla says, and we continue on toward the gate, the herbs
tucked away alongside everything else this whole strange season has
handed her to carry, and I find myself, watching her steady herself
against the market stall's small kindness, considerably more hopeful
about the day ahead than I was a few minutes before.

·

Isla does not take me to Ossory's hall directly, in the end, once we
leave the orchard — she stops short at the record-room instead, and I
understand, watching her hesitate at the door, exactly what's giving
her pause.

"I need a moment before I say this to him," she says. "What I've been
circling since Calder's Reach. I haven't said it plainly to anyone but
you, not even Ossory, and I find I need to be certain of the words
before I hand them to someone with the standing to act on them."

"Take the moment," I say. "I'll wait."

She stands at the door a long while, gathering whatever nerve the
saying requires, and I watch her, understanding that whatever she's
about to walk in and tell Ossory, she has been holding it, carefully,
privately, exactly the way she holds everything she isn't yet certain
enough to spend.

"Whatever happens in there," I say, "I'm grateful you found it at all.
Regardless of what Ossory decides."

"So am I," Isla says, and finally opens the door, and I follow her in,
understanding that whatever comes next is no longer a private theory
between the two of us, but about to become, for the first time, a
question put to someone else entirely.

## Chapter Forty — Cost Shared Openly (Isla)

The line has crept another mile since first frost, because the Rime
has never once, in eighty-eight days, given any of us reason to believe
it would stop advancing simply because we found a better way to bear
its cost. Forty-one cairns still stand anyway — the same number as the
morning of the binding, though I understand now, considerably more
plainly than I did that morning, exactly how much has changed
underneath a number that has, on its surface, stayed exactly the same
for the whole of this long, difficult winter.

Eighty-eight days. I have counted them, this time, properly, the way
I once counted only cairns and charges — not out of fear, the way I
counted in the beginning, but out of a specific, deliberate gratitude,
each day another one neither of us was fully certain, back at the
Long Reach cairn's cracked stone, that we would actually get to have.

Aldric and I set the season's last ward together at the Long Reach
cairn, the crack in the stone still visible under a fresh dusting of
frost, the cost between us ordinary now, in the way a shared thing
becomes ordinary once it has finally stopped being a secret either of
us feels the need to manage alone.

I trace the crack with one bare hand before we begin, the way I've
taken to doing at every visit since the day it very nearly took him
entirely, a small private ritual that has nothing to do with the
rite's own requirements and everything to do with properly remembering
what this particular stone has already cost, and spared, in equal
measure.

Tomas and Ossory both come out to witness it — not because the rite
requires witnesses for an ordinary seasonal ward, but because Ossory
has decided, since the mutual form's sanctioning, that every ward set
under it should be properly recorded, for whichever wardwright inherits
this line after both of us are done holding it.

Sela comes too, though nothing about a seasonal ward strictly requires
her presence, standing a little apart with the particular quiet pride
of a woman who has watched her brother survive a season she once
feared would simply take him the way it took their father, and I catch
her eye across the small gathered company, and she smiles, and I
understand, watching her, exactly how far this whole found family has
traveled since the morning of the binding.

"For everyone who's been someone else's warmth," Aldric says, at the
stone, an old blessing he alters as he speaks it, something I recognize
from a fragment of the Calder's Reach record, reworked into his own
plainer cadence, "and wanted, once, to be kept."

"Kept," I agree, and lay my hand over his marked one — the numb
fingers, the cold that never fully thawed, a permanent record of
exactly what this winter cost him — and mean the whole of what the word
costs and offers both, the way I have learned, this whole strange
season, that the truest vows are the ones taken apart, word by word,
and found to hold anyway.

The stone takes its ordinary measure from each of us, small and
unremarkable against everything the same granite has already
witnessed this winter, and I find myself grateful for precisely that
unremarkableness — a ward like any other, on a season like any other,
the specific, hard-won gift of an arrangement finally allowed to be
simply ordinary.

There is no proposal in this moment, no crown's formal blessing, no
ending any ledger I've ever kept would call properly complete. I find
I do not mind the absence of any of it, standing here at a stone that
has already given us considerably more than any formal ceremony could
properly account for. Ossory records the ward in his own careful hand,
for the historical account.
Tomas asks, immediately afterward, whether he can begin his own formal
training in the mutual form next week, and I tell him yes, and watch
him practically run back down the track toward Highstone with the
news, considerably lighter than the young man who first told me,
months ago, that he was simply making sure he was ready.

"Do you think it'll hold," Aldric asks, meaning the line, meaning the
whole arrangement we've built to hold it, meaning, I suspect,
considerably more than either of those things specifically.

"I don't know," I say, honestly, because I have decided, this whole
winter, that honest uncertainty is worth considerably more between us
than false comfort ever managed to be. "I know the Rime doesn't stop
advancing simply because we've found a better way to bear what it
costs. I know we're going to lose cairns, eventually, the way every
wardwright before me has lost cairns, regardless of how carefully
either of us manages the cost."

"That's not much comfort," Aldric says, though he's smiling, slightly,
hearing it.

"It isn't meant to be comfort," I say. "It's meant to be true. I've
found, this whole winter, that the true things hold up considerably
better than the comfortable ones, over time."

We walk back from the Long Reach cairn together, in no particular
hurry, past the village that has quietly rearranged itself around two
hearths instead of one, past Sela's orchard and Tomas's eager, ongoing
apprenticeship and Ossory's careful new record of a rite finally
practiced the way it was always meant to be practiced, and I find
myself thinking, for the first time in longer than I can accurately
place, that I have stopped running the cold arithmetic that has
governed my entire adult life, and started, instead, simply living
inside the answer it eventually arrived at.

Tomas catches up with us on the walk back from the Long Reach cairn,
having finished his own first solo inspection of the smaller waystones
closer to Highstone, and falls into step beside us with the particular
easy confidence of a young man who has, somewhere in these past weeks,
properly grown into the wardwright he is going to become.

I watch him walk that last stretch toward us, unhurried, entirely
himself, and think of the anxious sixteen-year-old who first arrived
at Highstone's gate, uncertain of his own place in a household still
shaping itself around a single grieving wardwright, and marvel, not
for the first time, at how completely that uncertain boy has grown
into someone this line can properly trust.

"The waystones held," he reports. "All seven, within tolerance. I
didn't need to feed any of them beyond the standard measure."

"Good work," I say, and mean it, watching him absorb the plain praise
with rather less flustered pleasure than he once showed, simply
accepting it now as an accurate accounting of a job properly done.

"I've been thinking," Tomas says, as we walk, "about what happens after
I've finished my own training. Whether I'll hold this line alone, the
way you did, or whether the crown might eventually sanction a proper
second practitioner, permanently, rather than treating the arrangement
as a one-time exception."

"I don't know," I say, honestly. "I know Ossory's argued for
precedent, not simply for us specifically. I'd like to think that
means the door's properly open for whoever comes after, to hold this
line however suits them best, rather than being handed the same
solitary arithmetic I inherited."

"I'd like that," Tomas says. "Not just for myself. For whoever comes
after me, too."

We walk the rest of the way to Highstone together, the three of us, in
the particular easy quiet of people who have survived a difficult
season and found, on the other side of it, something worth calling
family rather than merely circumstance, and I think, watching Tomas
peel off toward his own quarters and Sela's lamp glowing warm in the
distance above the orchard, that this is precisely the shape Ossian
never let himself have — a whole small company, properly sharing the
line's weight, rather than one wardwright, alone, holding it until it
finally held nothing back.

I think, too, about Corwin Vane, and Petra Ash, and Iven Hallow, and
every other name the old rite claimed before anyone thought to question
its shape properly. I do not know if word of what happened here will
ever properly reach whatever families they left behind, wherever the
crown's own scattered records might still hold some trace of them. I
resolve, walking the last stretch toward Highstone's gate, to write to
whatever offices might know, properly, so that their names, too, join
the small, growing record I keep of everyone this rite has cost —
not simply statistics, but people, properly mourned, properly
remembered, by the two hearths whose survival their own losses helped,
however unknowingly, to eventually purchase.

We stop at the record-room before heading home, because I have decided,
this evening, that Ossian's ledger and my own newer one belong beside
each other permanently rather than merely borrowed side by side for a
single night's copying, and Aldric waits while I set the two books
properly on the shelf, spines aligned, the old hand and the new one
keeping each other company for however many years the line requires
either of them to be consulted.

"Room for a third, eventually," Aldric observes, nodding at the space
beside them where Tomas's own ledger will someday sit.

"Room for a great many more than a third," I say. "That's rather the
whole point of finally writing it all down properly, instead of
letting each wardwright start over from nothing, the way I very nearly
did."

Ossory finds us there, on his way out for the evening, and pauses at
the doorway a moment before speaking.

"I wanted to tell you both something," he says, "now that the formal
business is finished and I'm no longer required to speak to you as a
crown official rather than simply as an old man who's watched this
line for considerably longer than either of you have been alive." He
gestures at the shelf. "I knew Ossian Thale. Not well. Well enough to
recognize, watching the two of you this winter, that he'd have been
considerably prouder of what you've built than he ever let himself
believe was possible, in his own time."

"You knew him," I say, surprised, something in my chest going
unexpectedly tight at the plain fact of it.

"Only a little," Ossory says. "Enough to know he carried more than he
ever let show. I think he'd have liked knowing his own student found
the nerve to finish what he couldn't." He nods once, formally, the old
gesture returning even as everything else about him, this evening,
feels considerably less formal than usual. "Goodnight, Wardwrights.
Both of you."

He leaves us there, in the record-room's quiet lamplight, and I find
myself, watching the door close behind him, holding Aldric's hand a
little tighter than the moment strictly requires.

"He called us both Wardwrights," I say. "Plural."

"I noticed," Aldric says. "I think I could get used to that, given
enough practice."

"So could I," I say, and we bank the record-room's lamp together
before heading home, out into the last of the evening's frost, the
whole line stretching north behind us in the dark, held now by
considerably more than one pair of hands.

·

Aldric takes my hand as we near the gate, marked fingers and unmarked
ones twined together the way they have been most evenings since the
rite was rewritten, and I feel, walking the last stretch home beside
him, the whole weight of everything this winter has cost and given,
settling, finally, into something I can properly carry rather than
merely survive.

No proposal. No crown's blessing. No ending that would satisfy anyone
keeping score on a ledger built for tidier stories than this one. Just
two hearths, sharing one winter, openly, on purpose, for as long as the
line needs holding — which, I understand now, watching Aldric's marked
hand rest easily in my own, is very likely to be considerably longer
than either one of us, alone, could ever have managed.

We pass Reny Ashe's stall on the way, shuttered for the night, and I
find myself smiling at the small, closed shopfront regardless, thinking
of every twist of herbs she's pressed into my hand, unbidden, since the
night of the binding.

"What is it," Aldric asks, catching the smile.

"I was thinking about how many people in this village decided, quietly,
to look out for me before I ever properly noticed they were doing it,"
I say. "Reny. Ida. Ossory himself, in his own careful way. I spent six
years believing I was managing this line entirely alone. I understand
now that I was never quite as alone as I believed."

"None of us are, I think," Aldric says, "once we finally let ourselves
notice."

"I'm glad I finally noticed," I say, and we continue on toward the
cottage together, considerably warmed by the whole quiet village's
accumulated, unspoken care.

·

We reach the cottage as full dark properly settles, the fire banked
low from the morning but easily coaxed back to life, and Aldric builds
it up while I set water for tea, the whole small domestic ritual
considerably more precious, tonight, for how ordinary it's finally
allowed to become.

"I keep thinking about the boundary marker at Calder's Reach," I say,
watching the fire catch. "The three rings. Your grandmother's lintel.
I wonder how many other small, unexamined fragments of this line's
older history are sitting in plain sight, waiting for someone to
finally ask what they mean."

"We could spend the rest of our lives chasing that question," Aldric
says, "and I suspect we still wouldn't run out of fragments to find."

"I'd like that, actually," I say. "Spending the rest of our lives
chasing it. I've spent six years believing the whole of my work was
simply holding the line steady against an ending. I like the idea of
spending the years after this one actually discovering something,
instead."

"Then that's what we'll do," Aldric says, settling beside me with the
tea properly steeped, "alongside everything else — the wards, the
ordinary evenings, whatever Tomas eventually needs from either of us.
Room enough, I think, for discovery too."

We drink the tea slowly, in no particular hurry, the cottage settling
around us in the specific comfortable quiet of two people who have,
somewhere in this long, difficult winter, stopped merely surviving
inside their own lives and started, instead, properly building one,
together, on purpose, for as long as either of them is given to keep
choosing it.

"Goodnight, Wardwright," Aldric says, echoing Ossory's earlier
farewell, with a small smile that tells me exactly how much he's
enjoying the new title's particular weight.

"Goodnight, Wardwright," I say back, and we bank the fire low for the
night, the whole line stretching north into the dark beyond our
windows, held now, and for as long as it needs holding, by
considerably more than one pair of hands.

·

I wake once, deep in the night, to find Aldric already awake beside me,
watching the last of the banked fire's glow with the particular quiet
stillness of a man turning something over privately.

"You're not sleeping," I observe.

"I'm simply grateful," he says. "I keep waking, some nights, and
needing to properly confirm that all of this actually happened — that
I'm still here, that you're still here, that the whole terrible
arithmetic of this winter somehow resolved into something worth waking
up for."

"I do the same," I admit. "I don't think either of us is going to
stop, entirely, checking for a while yet."

"I don't mind the checking," Aldric says. "I think I'd rather spend the
rest of my life occasionally waking to confirm this is real than take
any part of it for granted."

I settle back against him, and we lie together in the banked fire's
low glow a while longer, neither of us in any hurry to properly sleep,
both of us simply grateful, in the particular quiet of the deepest part
of the night, for a future neither of us quite believed, six weeks ago,
either one of us would get to keep.

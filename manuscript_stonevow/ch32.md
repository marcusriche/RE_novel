## Chapter Thirty-Two — The Cairn Cracks (Aldric)

I go out to the Long Reach cairn alone, at dusk, without waking her, telling myself the whole walk there that this once, surely, is different.

The evening's gone properly cold by the time I reach the track's edge,
the specific bright, brittle cold that means the frost is setting hard
overnight, and I find myself moving faster than the errand strictly
requires, some part of me already sensing, before the reading confirms
it, exactly how much worse the stone has gotten since morning.

It is not, I want to be clear, a decision I make lightly, or one I make
without understanding exactly what it will cost her to wake and find me
gone. I make it because the cairn's reading, when I checked it that
afternoon while Isla was still in the record-room with Tomas, had
dropped considerably further than either of us accounted for, and I
understood, checking it, that it would not hold through the night
without someone feeding it, and I was not willing to wake her and ask
her to spend herself on it again, not after what the last feeding cost
her, not with the mutual rite still unsanctioned and the whole of our
remaining time uncertain.

The cairn cracks at dusk, a sound like the whole line taking a breath it
cannot let back out, granite splitting along a fault line that has
clearly been building for considerably longer than the visible reading
suggested, and I throw everything I currently have left into holding it
rather than let it fail outright, because a cairn that fails outright
takes the whole surrounding stretch of line down with it, and I am not
willing to let that happen on a night when the alternative is simply my
own cost, spent alone, the way I have spent every cost in my whole
adult life.

I feel Isla arrive through the bond before I hear her arrive on the
frozen track — a cold that isn't weather, arriving all at once in my
own chest, her fear translated, somehow, into a sensation I recognize
immediately as hers rather than mine, the bond running, for the first
time, in the opposite direction from how it usually runs.

She reaches the cairn at a dead run, having covered three miles in
considerably less time than three miles should properly take, and finds
me upright, barely, one hand — the numb one, useless for anything
requiring feeling, entirely reliable for anything requiring simple,
stubborn contact — fused white to the cracked stone, the cairn holding
by a margin so thin it is not, in any honest accounting, really holding
at all.

"I wasn't going to let it be the one that finally breaks," I say,
through it, which is not, I understand even saying it, the same as
saying I expected to survive making sure it didn't.

"You went without me," Isla says, and I hear, underneath the fury, a
terror considerably larger than the anger itself.

"I didn't want you spending yourself on this one too," I say. "Not
after the last feeding. Not with the rite still unsanctioned."

"That wasn't your choice to make alone," she says, and gets me off the
stone, physically, her own arms considerably stronger than I would have
guessed, given how slight she is against my own weight, and I go where
she pulls me, because I no longer have the strength left to argue the
point, or even, entirely, to stand without her holding me upright.

The cairn holds, for now, cracked and ugly and standing, a scar in the
granite that will never fully close, and both of us understand, in the
long, slow walk back toward Highstone, that we have run out of time to
do this the careful, properly sanctioned way any of us would have
preferred.

"I'm sorry," I say, once we're far enough from the cairn that speaking
feels survivable again. "For going without you. I thought I was
protecting you."

"You weren't," Isla says. "You were doing the exact thing you promised
me you'd stop doing. Paying alone, in the dark, and calling it
protection instead of what it actually was, which was fear, dressed up
carefully enough that even you couldn't recognize it."

I don't have an argument against that, because I understand, hearing
it, that she is entirely correct, and that whatever ground we'd gained
these past weeks — the honesty, the shared counting, the mutual watching
we'd promised each other — I have just, in a single evening's decision,
undone considerably more of it than I intended to.

"We're out of time," I say, instead of arguing. "For the careful
version. For waiting on Ossory's sanction. The cairn's proof enough of
that."

"I know," Isla says, and something in her voice, saying it, frightens
me considerably more than the crack in the stone did, because I
understand, hearing it, that she has already begun doing the
arithmetic on what happens next, and that whatever answer she's
arriving at is not one she is currently prepared to say out loud to me.

·

Tomas is waiting at the cottage door when we finally reach it, having
apparently sat up the whole evening watching for our return, and his
face, seeing the state of us, does something I have not seen it do
since the night Feld's first ultimatum arrived — a specific, total
collapse of composure that he does not bother trying to manage back
into his usual careful calm.

"What happened," he says, already moving to help me get Aldric properly
seated by the fire.

"The Long Reach cairn cracked," Isla says, flatly, exhausted past the
point of softening the report. "Aldric held it alone rather than wake
me."

Tomas looks at me with an expression considerably harder than I've
ever seen from him, closer to genuine anger than the mild disapproval
he generally reserves for my own worse habits.

"That was a terrible decision," he says, plainly, with none of his
usual deference.

"I know," I say, too exhausted to muster any defense of it.

"I don't think you do, actually," Tomas says, "or you wouldn't have
made it. Do you understand what it would have done to her, to all of
us, if that cairn had taken the whole of you instead of merely nearly
taking it? We are not simply managing your condition as an abstract
problem, Aldric. We're managing it because we'd all considerably rather
you stayed alive."

I don't have an argument against a nineteen-year-old's fury, mostly
because I understand, hearing it, that he's entirely right, and that
the whole shape of tonight's decision looks, from outside my own head,
considerably more reckless than it felt making it alone in the dark.

"I hear you," I say. "I'm sorry."

Tomas's anger softens, slightly, seeing the honesty in the apology, and
he busies himself instead with building the fire back up properly,
fetching blankets, doing the ordinary, useful work of caring for
someone in crisis that requires no further argument to accomplish.

"We go to Ossory first thing tomorrow," Isla says, to both of us, once
Tomas has settled and the worst of the immediate cold has been pushed
back from the room. "No more waiting for his usual pace. Tonight
proved we don't have the luxury of it."

Neither Tomas nor I argue with that, and the three of us sit together
by the fire a while longer, the whole small household drawn close
around the specific shared fear of a night that very nearly took
considerably more from all of us than any of us were prepared to lose.

·

Isla does not sleep, once Tomas has finally gone to his own bed and
Aldric has drifted into the particular heavy, exhausted sleep of a man
who has spent everything he currently has to spend. She sits beside
him instead, in the low firelight, doing the arithmetic she has been
avoiding doing plainly since the crack first sounded across the Long
Reach cairn.

I watch her from where I lie, not quite asleep myself, too depleted to
properly speak but not so far gone that I miss the specific stillness
of her, sitting there, working through some decision I cannot yet name.

"You're not sleeping," I manage, finally, my voice considerably
rougher than I intend it.

"Neither are you," she says, "which means you should be, rather than
watching me not do it either."

"I don't think either of us is going to sleep properly tonight," I
say. "Tell me what you're deciding. I can see it on your face, even
this exhausted."

She's quiet a long moment, and I understand, watching her, that
whatever she's about to say has cost her considerable effort to
arrive at.

"I'm deciding," she says, finally, "that we can't wait for Ossory's
comfortable pace any longer. That if the choice is between a careful,
properly sanctioned attempt that arrives too late, and an urgent,
imperfect one that arrives in time, I know which one I'm choosing,
regardless of what either of us promised each other about doing this
properly."

"That frightens me," I admit, "hearing you say it."

"It frightens me too," Isla says. "I've spent this whole crisis
insisting on the careful version, the plainly asked version, the
version with proper witness and proper sanction. I still believe in
all of that. I just don't think we have the luxury of waiting for it
to arrive at its own pace anymore. Tonight proved that, whatever else
it proved."

I don't have an argument against her reasoning, mostly because I share
it, entirely, underneath my own considerable fear of whatever urgency
might cost either of us in the attempting.

"Whatever you decide," I say, "I want it decided together. Not you
alone, working it out in the dark while I sleep. Not me, deciding
something at dawn without waking you first."

"Together," Isla agrees, and takes my good hand, holding it the rest
of the night, neither of us sleeping, both of us simply present, in
the low firelight, for whatever the coming days demand of us.

·

Sela arrives before dawn, having received word from Tomas the moment
we returned, and finds her brother sleeping, finally, exhausted past
the point of waking easily, and sits with him a long while before
turning her attention to me.

"You should have woken me," she says, quietly, meaning the cairn, "the
moment you realized he'd gone."

"I didn't know, until I was already running," I say. "The bond told me
something was wrong. It didn't tell me in time to gather anyone else
first."

Sela absorbs this, nodding slowly, and I watch her decide, visibly, not
to press the point further, understanding, I think, that I am already
carrying more guilt about last night than any further reproach could
usefully add to.

"I'm not angry at you," she says, gentler now. "I want that understood.
I'm angry at whatever in him decided going alone was preferable to
waking either of us. I know that's not a fair anger to hold against a
man who's clearly trying, imperfectly, to unlearn a lifetime of exactly
that habit. I'm holding it anyway, for tonight, because I need
somewhere to put the fear."

"I understand," I say. "I've been holding a version of the same anger
myself, since the moment I found him at that stone."

We sit together, the two of us, in the cottage's early morning quiet,
watching Aldric sleep, both of us holding our own private versions of
the same fierce, frightened love, and I find, sitting there, a
considerable comfort in not being the only person in this arrangement
who refuses to let him carry anything alone.

·

Tomas joins the two of us before the morning's properly begun,
carrying a mug of something warm for each of us, and settles quietly
into the third chair without being asked, the particular unassuming
presence of someone who has learned exactly when a room needs company
and when it needs simply to be occupied without demand.

"I've already sent word to Ossory," he says, quietly, so as not to
wake Aldric. "About last night. I told him plainly what happened,
rather than let the account arrive filtered through whatever the
village grapevine does to a story by the time it's traveled three
households."

"Thank you," Sela says. "That was well done."

"I've been thinking," Tomas continues, "about what I said to him
last night. That he wasn't managing this as an abstract problem." He
glances toward Aldric's sleeping form. "I meant it. But I want you both
to know I understand why he did it. I don't agree with it. I understand
it."

"What do you understand," I ask.

"That being the one who's always managed alone doesn't stop just
because you've decided to try differently," Tomas says. "I think old
habits reach back further than a few weeks of good intentions can
properly counter, especially under this much pressure."

Sela considers this, nodding slowly.

"That's rather more grace than I've managed to extend him this
morning," she admits.

"I'm not extending grace instead of the anger," Tomas says. "I think
both are true at once. I'm angry, and I understand, and neither one
cancels the other out."

We sit together a while longer, the three of us, in the quiet
aftermath of a night that very nearly cost all of us considerably more
than any of us were prepared to lose, and I find myself, watching Tomas
navigate his own complicated feelings with more grace than his years
would suggest he owes anyone, grateful for a young man who has learned,
this whole difficult season, to hold contradiction properly rather
than force it into something simpler than it actually is.

·

Cera Voss arrives not long after, summoned by Tomas's own early
message, and examines Aldric properly while he sleeps, her brisk,
practiced hands checking the worst of the previous night's damage
without waking him.

"He's stable," she says, finally, straightening. "Considerably more
depleted than I'd like, but stable. Whatever you did, getting him off
that stone before it finished taking everything, likely saved his
life outright."

"Will he recover," I ask, "properly, or is this simply delaying the
same outcome."

"I don't know yet," Cera admits, with the same honesty she's shown
from our first meeting. "I know delaying it bought you time you didn't
have yesterday. Whether that time is enough depends considerably on
whatever comes next — and I understand, from what Tomas has told me,
that something considerably more significant may be coming next."

"The mutual form," I say. "If Ossory sanctions it."

"Then I hope he sanctions it soon," Cera says, gathering her things.
"I've treated enough hearths on this line to know that borrowed time
has a way of running out faster than anyone budgets for."

She leaves us with careful instructions for the hours ahead, and I sit
back down beside Aldric, considerably more aware than I was an hour ago
of exactly how narrow the margin has become between the crisis we
survived last night and whatever the coming days still have waiting
for us.

·

Aldric sleeps through most of the remaining day, at Cera's own firm
instruction, and I keep watch beside him well past the point either of
us would call reasonable, exhaustion finally catching up with me
somewhere in the deep, quiet hours of the evening.

"You should rest properly," Sela tells me, gently, finding me still
upright. "He's stable. He's not going anywhere tonight."

"I know," I say, though I don't move.

"Isla." My name, from her, in the specific tone of a woman who has
learned, this whole crisis, exactly when insistence is warranted. "You
won't be any use to him tomorrow if you don't sleep tonight."

She's right, and I know it, and I let her guide me, finally, to my own
bed, and sleep finds me almost immediately, deeper and more complete
than any rest I've properly managed since the cairn first cracked —
a sleep so thorough that I do not hear, until Tomas wakes me with the
note already in his hand, that Aldric has risen before dawn and gone.

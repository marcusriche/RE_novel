## Chapter Twenty — What the Record Doesn't Say (Isla)

The house the record came from was a wardwright's, same as my own —
I understand this properly only once we've made camp for the night in
its main room, unwilling to walk the whole distance back to Highstone
in failing light with a discovery this fragile still uncertain in my
hands, and I have had time to look, properly, at the specific shape of
a life lived in rooms very nearly identical to my own.

The wardwright's chair sits where my own chair sits, relative to the
hearth. The record shelf holds its ledgers in the same order I keep my
own — inspection logs first, then correspondence, then, at the very
back, the private notebook every wardwright I have ever heard of keeps
for the things that don't belong in an official record at all.

I find myself, sitting on the cold hearthstone of a house whose
occupant died — walked into the cold, the old euphemism runs, though I
have never once, until tonight, properly considered how literal that
phrase might actually be — considerably longer ago than I've properly
reckoned with, unable to move to anything else until I've sat with the
whole shape of the resemblance.

"You're thinking about your own mentor," Aldric says, quietly, sitting
down beside me on the same cold stone.

"Yes," I admit.

"Tell me about him," Aldric says. "If you're willing. I don't think
you've said his name to me once, in all these weeks."

"Ossian Thale," I say, and it costs me, saying it aloud, considerably
more than I expect a simple name to cost. "He bound me as his apprentice
when I was nineteen. He taught me everything I know about the line —
which cairns fail first, how to read a charge before it's fully gone,
how to hold forty miles of stone together with considerably less
support than the crown ever properly funded. He walked into the cold
seven years ago, when I was twenty-six, and I completed the rite that
spent him."

"You completed it," Aldric says, carefully. "Meaning you were the one
who—"

"I set the final ward," I say. "The one that took what was left of him
and put it into the stone properly, so his death would mean something
rather than simply being a loss with nothing gained against it. That's
the correct way to say it, in the old cadence. Nobody at the ceremony
afterward said it that plainly. They thanked me instead. For a job
well done. Nobody asked whether I wanted to be thanked for it."

Aldric doesn't offer comfort shaped like fixing, which I am, by now,
profoundly grateful for. He simply sits with me on the cold stone and
lets the grief be exactly as large as it actually is, unmanaged, which
turns out, this once, to be the only thing that actually helps.

"I completed the rite," I say, "and then I went back to Highstone, and
I did the next day's inspections, because there was nobody else to do
them, and I have not, in the seven years since, once properly stopped
to feel the whole of what that morning cost me. I've filed it. I've
managed it. I've done the arithmetic on it the way I do arithmetic on
everything, and I have never once let it be simply a grief, unmanaged,
the way it actually is."

"You've been carrying that alone for seven years," Aldric says.

"Yes."

"I know the shape of that particular weight," he says, and I
understand, hearing it, that he means it considerably more literally
than the words alone suggest — a man who has spent his own adult life
carrying costs silently, in the dark, calling the aloneness discipline
rather than what it actually is, recognizing, in me, the exact same
practiced silence.

I let myself, finally, properly, grieve Ossian Thale — not the
managed version, not the version that fits neatly into a morning's
arithmetic before the day's inspections resume, the whole unmanageable
size of it, sitting on a dead stranger's cold hearthstone with a man
who does not once try to make the grief smaller than it actually is.

It does not fix anything. It does not undo seven years of careful
filing, or make the next morning's inspections any less necessary. But
it does, for the first time since I was twenty-six years old, feel like
carrying something in company rather than entirely alone, and when I am
finally, properly finished — spent, in the particular way grief spends
a person, hollowed out rather than resolved — I find that something in
my chest has settled that has not settled properly in seven years.

"Thank you," I say, "for asking me to say his name."

"Thank you for saying it," Aldric says, and holds me, on the cold
stone, in a house that was once, very precisely, exactly like my own,
until the fire we've built catches properly and the room, slowly,
begins to hold something closer to warmth.

·

We stay the night in the predecessor's own house, unwilling to attempt
the full walk back to Highstone in failing light with a discovery this
significant still uncertain in our hands, and I find myself, once the
fire's properly caught and the worst of the cold has been pushed back
from the room, telling Aldric considerably more about Ossian than I
have told anyone since the funeral.

"He used to say the line didn't care how tired you were," I say,
"only whether the stone was properly fed. I hated that saying, at
nineteen. I understand it rather differently now, having spent six
years learning exactly how true it is, and exactly how much it costs to
keep living by it regardless."

"Did he have anyone," Aldric asks, "the way you have me now. Someone to
share the arithmetic with."

"No," I say, and the answer costs me something, saying it, a specific
grief for a man who spent decades exactly as alone as I spent my own
first six years of practice. "He had apprentices. He had the crown's
office, at a professional distance. I don't think he had anyone who
simply sat with him, the way you're sitting with me now, without any
official reason requiring it."

"That's a lonely way to have lived."

"I think it was," I say. "I think it's rather why he never questioned
the rite's own cruelty properly, until it was far too late to matter —
he'd never had anyone close enough to help him see the arithmetic
clearly, from outside his own exhaustion."

Aldric is quiet for a long moment, turning that over.

"I don't want that for you," he says, finally. "Whatever happens with
the mutual rite, whatever it ends up costing either of us — I don't
want you to end up like him. Alone with an arithmetic nobody else ever
properly checked."

"I don't think I will," I say, and find, saying it, that I actually
believe it, for the first time properly since Ossian's death — a belief
built, slowly, out of a fortnight and a half of a stranger mending my
glove before dawn and asking, plainly, whether I actually wanted the
thing sitting between us, and choosing, every single day since, to keep
asking.

We sleep, eventually, in a dead wardwright's own bed, and I wake once,
in the deepest part of the night, to find Aldric's good hand already
holding mine, unconsciously, the way you hold onto something you've
decided, even asleep, you aren't willing to let go of.

·

I wake again near dawn, properly this time, to find Aldric already up,
sitting by the cold hearth, turning something over in his own hands —
a small, worn wooden figure, clearly a child's toy, that must have
belonged to whoever last lived in this house.

"I found it in the corner, under some rubble," he says, seeing me
watch him. "I don't know why I picked it up. It felt wrong to leave it
lying there, forgotten a second time, after however many years it's
already spent forgotten once."

"May I see it," I ask, and he hands it over — a small carved bird,
crudely made, clearly whittled by an amateur hand rather than any
proper craftsman's, the specific loving imperfection of a parent's gift
rather than a purchased toy.

"Do you think it belonged to the wardwright's own child," I ask,
"or a grandchild, perhaps."

"I don't know," Aldric says. "I don't think we'll ever know, properly.
I think that's rather the whole tragedy of this place — an entire life,
reduced now to whatever fragments happen to survive the cold's own
patient erasure, with nobody left to tell us which fragments actually
mattered most."

I turn the small bird over in my own hands, considering.

"I want to bring it back with us," I say. "Not to keep, exactly. To
give it somewhere it can be properly remembered, rather than left in a
dead house nobody visits."

"Where would you put it?"

"The record-room, perhaps. Alongside Ossian's own ledger, and yours,
eventually, and mine, someday. A small reminder of exactly what we're
actually protecting, every time either of us sets a ward — not simply
stone and charge readings. Whole lives. Children who carve birds for
their own children, and never get the chance to see them grown."

Aldric doesn't say anything for a long moment, simply watching me hold
the small carved bird, and I understand, from his expression, that
I've said something that has landed considerably harder than I
intended.

"That's exactly right," he says, finally, quietly. "I think that's
precisely what we're protecting."

We pack the small bird carefully alongside the record box and my own
transcription, and begin the walk back to Highstone properly, at first
light, carrying, between the two of us, considerably more than either
of us arrived at Calder's Reach expecting to carry home.

·

We stop once more at the boundary marker on our way out, the three
interlocking rings still visible under a fresh dusting of frost, and I
find myself, this time, tracing the shape properly, committing it to
memory in a way I hadn't managed on the way in.

"I keep thinking about your grandmother's lintel," I say. "The same
mark, on a door, generations removed from whatever built this marker.
I wonder if there's a whole thread of history here neither of us has
properly traced yet — older markings, older forms, quietly surviving in
places nobody thought to look because nobody expected anything to
survive this far past the line at all."

"Do you think it's connected to the mutual form specifically," Aldric
asks, "or something else entirely?"

"I don't know," I admit. "I think it might simply be evidence that this
village understood something about the world that got lost, the same
way the rite itself got corrupted somewhere between whoever built this
marker and whoever taught me the version I learned at nineteen. I'd
like to come back, properly, and study it further, once we've dealt
with what's currently most urgent."

"I'll come with you," Aldric says, "whenever that eventually is,"
and we continue past the marker, out of Calder's Reach's particular
silence, toward whatever the line still has waiting for us on the
other side of it.

·

We stop, partway along the walk back, at a small rise that gives the
last clear view of the village behind us, and I find myself turning
for a final look, the way you look back at a grave once more before
walking away from it properly.

"Do you think anyone will ever come back here, after us," I ask,
"properly, to study it the way it deserves, rather than simply passing
through once in a crisis?"

"I don't know," Aldric admits. "I'd like to think so. I think Tomas
would want to, if we told him what we found. He's the kind of person
who'd want to catalogue every ledger properly, rather than let it sit
another decade unstudied."

"I think you're right," I say. "I think that's rather why I want him
involved in this, eventually, once the current crisis has settled. This
village deserves more than a single desperate visit. It deserves
someone who'll return, properly, and give it the careful attention
Ossian would have given it, if he'd ever had the chance to know it
existed."

"We'll bring him," Aldric says, "once there's time. I think he'd want
that considerably more than he'd admit, if you simply told him the
option existed."

We take a final look at Calder's Reach, sitting quiet in its shallow
fold of ground, and I make myself a private promise, standing there,
that this will not be the last time either of us properly honors what
this village and its unnamed wardwright have given us, however this
current crisis eventually resolves.

·

Tomas is waiting at Highstone's gate when we finally arrive, having
clearly kept watch since the previous evening, and his relief at
seeing us both upright is visible enough that I understand, watching
him, exactly how long the wait must have felt.

"You were gone considerably longer than planned," he says, careful not
to make it an accusation, though the worry underneath is plain.

"We found more than we expected," I say, "and needed the extra time to
properly gather it. I'll tell you everything, once we've properly
rested. There's a great deal to explain."

"Is it good news," Tomas asks, "or the other kind?"

"Both, I think," I say, honestly. "I found something that might save
Aldric's life. I also found a whole village's worth of ordinary grief
that I don't think either of us is going to properly finish carrying
for some time yet."

Tomas absorbs this with the particular gravity he's shown, this whole
season, toward anything touching the line's deeper losses, and helps
us both inside, fussing over the practical business of food and rest
with the specific focused energy of a young man who has learned,
these past weeks, exactly how to be useful in the aftermath of a
crisis he wasn't present to witness firsthand.

·

Sela arrives not long after, having heard through the village's own
reliable grapevine that we'd finally returned, and takes one look at
both our faces before setting down whatever she'd brought.

"You look like you've seen the whole shape of something considerable,"
she observes.

"We have," I say. "I don't think either of us has properly finished
absorbing it yet."

"Then don't try to explain it all tonight," Sela says, with the
particular practical firmness I've come to expect from her. "Eat
something properly. Rest. The explaining can wait until morning, once
you've both actually slept."

"That's good advice," Aldric says, "though I suspect neither of us is
very good at following it."

"You're going to start being good at it," Sela says, "starting
tonight, or I'm going to sit here and make certain of it personally."

We let her fuss over us properly, grateful, both of us, for a sister
and a household determined to insist on rest even when neither of us
feels entirely capable of taking it for granted, and I find myself,
settling finally into the cottage's familiar warmth, considerably
steadied by the specific, insistent care of people who refuse to let
either of us manage this alone.

·

Sela stays until we've both properly eaten, refusing to leave until
she's satisfied neither of us intends to simply push the food aside
in favor of talking through the whole of Calder's Reach immediately.

"Tomorrow," she says, firmly, gathering her things at last. "Properly
rested, both of you. I'll want the whole account then, and I expect
it told slowly, not rushed through simply to be done with the telling."

"Agreed," I say, and mean it, grateful for a household that has
learned, this whole season, exactly when insistence serves someone
better than sympathy.

She leaves us finally to our rest, and I find myself, watching the
door close behind her, considerably calmer than I've been since we
first crossed back over the last standing cairn, held steady by a
family determined to make certain neither of us faces the aftermath of
anything difficult entirely alone.

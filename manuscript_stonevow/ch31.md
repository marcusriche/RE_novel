## Chapter Thirty-One — What the Rite Would Cost Him (Isla)

No wardwright alive has performed the mutual rite. I have a dead
woman's account of it, faded and incomplete in places where damp has
gotten into the record box over however many decades it sat sealed in
Calder's Reach, and nothing else — no second source, no living
practitioner who's attempted it and survived to describe what happens
if it's tried wrong, or late, or by two people the rite-keepers never
properly sanctioned to attempt it at all.

I have read her account so many times, these past days, that I could
recite whole passages from memory, and still I find myself returning
to it nightly, searching the same faded ink for some reassurance it
was never written to provide.

"You could tell the Keepers everything, formally," Tomas offers, in the
record-room, watching me read the translation for what must be the
twentieth time, searching for a caveat I have not yet found and am
increasingly afraid does not exist to be found. "Let them decide
properly, with more than one record to weigh against the crown's own
concerns."

"The Keepers would take a season to decide properly," I say. "We don't
have a season. We barely have the one cairn Aldric can still stand at
without his knees giving out under him."

I do not tell Tomas the other fear sitting underneath the first one —
that the rite, attempted wrong, could spend us both at once, cost taken
from two hearths simultaneously with no old wardwright's careful
account of how to stop it once begun, and that I am, for the first time
in my entire life, considerably more frightened of losing Aldric than
I am of dying myself.

I have spent six years being the only wardwright standing between forty
miles of cairns and the Rime's own patient advance, and I have never
once, in all that time, been properly frightened for myself. I am
frightened now, sitting in a record-room built entirely out of other
people's endings, of a fear that has nothing to do with my own
mortality at all.

"Do you think it'll work?" Tomas asks. "Properly. Not just — do you
hope it will. Do you actually think it will."

I consider the question with more honesty than I have given myself
permission for since Calder's Reach.

"I think it's genuine," I say. "I think the record describes something
real, a form of the rite that actually existed, actually worked, before
whichever generation of rite-keepers found it easier to spend one
hearth quietly than share the cost between two wardwrights loudly. I
don't know if it survives being attempted this late, by two people who
weren't trained in it from the start, with a hearth already this
compromised."

"That's not a yes," Tomas says.

"No," I agree. "It isn't. It's the honest answer, which is
considerably less comfortable than a yes would be, but which I've
decided, this whole autumn, matters more."

Tomas sits with that for a while, doing his own private arithmetic, the
particular careful sum of a young man calculating what happens to his
own future depending on which way an unproven rite falls.

"Whatever happens," he says, finally, "I want you to know I'd rather
you attempt it and it not work than watch you keep doing what you've
been doing for two years, quietly, until it finally catches up with
you the same way it's catching up with him."

I do not have an answer for being seen, quite so plainly, by a
nineteen-year-old I have spent three years trying to protect from
exactly this kind of clear-eyed accounting, so I simply nod, once, and
let the record-room hold the fear with me a while longer, in the
specific silence of a room built entirely out of endings, waiting to
see whether this particular one turns out to be different from all the
rest.

·

Aldric finds me still there past midnight, the lamp burned low, the
translation spread across the table in the specific disarray of a
woman who has read the same uncertain passages considerably more times
than rereading them will ever resolve.

"You should sleep," he says, gently, though I notice he makes no move
to leave himself.

"I should," I agree, and do not move either.

He sits across from me, and for a while neither of us says anything at
all, simply sharing the particular exhausted quiet of two people who
have run out of easy words for the size of what's currently ahead of
them.

"I keep thinking about the crooked tree," Aldric says, eventually. "The
one that stopped bearing fruit. I kept it because cutting it down felt
like agreeing the cold had won something it hadn't actually earned. I
wonder if that's what I'm doing now. Refusing to accept an ending
simply because accepting it would mean admitting the cold's finally
caught up with me."

"I don't think that's quite the same thing," I say. "The tree stopped
fruiting through no fault of its own. You haven't stopped anything.
You're still here, still arguing with me at midnight in a record-room,
still considerably more stubborn than any reasonable cold has a right
to expect from you."

"For now," Aldric says.

"For now," I agree, because I have decided that false comfort costs
more than honest uncertainty, even at midnight, even exhausted,
"which is the only tense any of us actually get to live in, regardless
of how much either of us would prefer the certainty of a different
one."

He reaches across the table and takes my hand — the good one, still
warm, still entirely his — and we sit like that a while longer, not
solving anything, simply sharing the considerable weight of not
knowing, until the lamp finally gutters and neither of us has the
heart to relight it, choosing instead to sit together in the dark a
little longer before the day's fear properly resumes.

·

Sela finds the two of us there, still in the record-room, as the first
grey light of dawn starts finding its way through the shuttered window,
and does not comment on the fact that neither of us has properly slept.

"I've brought breakfast," she says, simply, setting down a basket that
smells of the orchard's own last stored apples, "on the theory that
neither of you thought to eat anything since yesterday, and that
whatever today requires, it's going to require it from people who've
actually fed themselves properly first."

"Thank you," Aldric says, and means it, and I watch brother and sister
share a look that carries considerably more than the simple exchange of
a meal — the specific, wordless communication of two people who have
weathered enough difficult mornings together to no longer need words
for most of it.

We eat together, the three of us, in the record-room's grey dawn light,
and I find myself, watching Sela fuss quietly over whether Aldric's
actually finished his portion, grateful for a family that has, this
whole strange winter, folded me into its own particular rhythms without
ever once making the folding feel like an imposition on either side.

"Whatever Ossory decides today," Sela says, once the meal's finished,
"I want it understood, by both of you, that this family isn't going
anywhere. Regardless of the outcome. We'll face whatever comes, together,
the way we should have been facing everything, all along, instead of
each of us managing our own private corner of it alone."

"Agreed," I say, and Aldric echoes it, and the three of us finish the
morning's preparations together, considerably more steadied by the
shared meal and the shared resolve than either of us managed alone,
lying awake in the record-room's dark the whole of the previous night.

·

Tomas arrives shortly after, dressed for what he clearly understands to
be a significant day, carrying his own carefully organized notes on the
translation's provenance, ready to argue his portion of the case
alongside the rest of us.

"I've reviewed everything once more," he says, "in case Ossory has
further questions about the verification process specifically."

"You've done everything that could reasonably be asked of you," I say,
"and considerably more besides."

"I want to do more than reasonably," Tomas says, with a seriousness
that reminds me, not for the first time, exactly how much this crisis
has already asked of a nineteen-year-old who never signed up for
quite this scale of responsibility. "This is Aldric's life. I want to
have done everything within my power, properly, rather than simply
enough to satisfy some reasonable standard."

"I understand," I say, and mean it, recognizing in his own fierce
diligence something of my own habit, carefully inherited, of believing
that enough is never quite enough when someone you love is at stake.
"Just remember, whatever happens today, that you've already done more
than anyone could rightfully ask of you. Regardless of Ossory's
decision, that much is already true."

Tomas nods, absorbing this, and we set out together for the rite-hall,
the whole small household moving as one toward whatever the day has
waiting for us.

·

We stop, briefly, at the record-room on the way, because I find I want
one final look at Ossian's own translation before handing the whole
question over to Ossory's judgment, and Aldric waits with me at the
door while I retrieve it.

"Nervous," he asks, watching me tuck the fragile pages carefully into
my satchel.

"Considerably," I admit. "I've spent six years being the person other
people bring their fears to. I'm not entirely practiced at being the
one asking someone else to take a risk on my account."

"You're allowed to be frightened," Aldric says. "I'd be more worried
if you weren't."

"I know," I say. "I think I'm more frightened of what happens if
Ossory says no than of anything the rite itself might cost either of
us. I've built this whole plan around the assumption that he'll hear
it properly. I don't have a version of today prepared for the
alternative."

"Then we'll build one," Aldric says, "together, if it comes to that.
Whatever Ossory decides, we're not facing the aftermath of it alone,
either of us."

I take some comfort in that, more than I expect to, and we continue on
toward the rite-hall together, the fragile translation carefully
tucked away, the whole weight of the morning ahead of us considerably
lighter for not facing it as two separate people making two separate
cases, but as one shared argument, offered jointly, for a future
neither of us is willing to face without the other.

"Whatever happens today," I say, as Highstone's gate comes into view,
"I want you to know that asking for this properly — in front of
witnesses, plainly, together — matters to me considerably more than
whatever Ossory ultimately decides. I'd rather lose this argument
having asked it honestly than win it by any of the old, careful,
managed methods either of us used to prefer."

"I feel exactly the same," Aldric says, and takes my hand as we
approach the gate, and I understand, walking the last stretch toward
whatever today holds, that this alone — the plain, unmanaged asking,
offered together — is already considerably more than either of us had
six weeks ago, regardless of what the day still has waiting for us.

·

Ossory receives us in the record-room rather than the rite-hall proper,
a small mercy I recognize immediately — a formal hall would have made
today's argument feel like a trial rather than a request, and I
understand, seeing the choice, exactly how deliberately he's already
begun trying to make this easier than his position strictly requires.

"Show me everything," he says, without preamble, and I do — the
predecessor's record, Tomas's own careful cross-checking, the whole
patient architecture of proof we've assembled since Calder's Reach.

He reads slowly, methodically, asking precise questions at exactly the
points I'd have asked them myself, and I find, watching him work
through the material with genuine rigor rather than performed
skepticism, a considerable relief in being taken seriously by someone
whose seriousness actually counts.

"This is thorough," he says, finally, setting the last page down. "More
thorough than I expected, given the timeline you've been working
under."

"Tomas did the bulk of the cross-checking," I say. "I want that
properly credited, whatever you decide."

Ossory looks at Tomas for a long moment, something considering in his
expression.

"You'll make a fine wardwright," he says, "assuming this line survives
long enough to need one." He turns back to the record spread across
the table. "I need more time before I can properly sanction an attempt.
I want that understood plainly. But I want equally plainly understood
that I intend to move as quickly as diligence permits, rather than at
my office's usual comfortable pace."

"Thank you," I say, and mean it, though I feel the day's earlier
nervousness settling into something more like cautious relief than
outright celebration.

"Don't thank me yet," Ossory says, echoing what has apparently become
this whole village's standard response to gratitude offered too early.
"Thank me once I've actually delivered something worth the thanks."

·

We leave the record-room together, the three of us, and I find Sela
waiting at the corridor's end, having come to Highstone specifically to
hear the outcome the moment it was available.

"Well," she says, reading our faces. "Tell me."

"He needs more time," I say, "but he's taking it seriously. Properly.
I think that's rather more than we had reason to expect, going in."

"More time," Sela repeats, turning the phrase over. "How much more?"

"I don't know," I admit. "I don't think Ossory knows precisely either.
I think the honest answer is that he's going to move as quickly as his
own diligence permits, and none of us gets to set the pace for him."

Sela absorbs this, nodding slowly, though I can see the specific effort
it costs her not to press further.

"Then we wait," she says, finally, "properly, together, rather than
each of us managing the waiting separately."

"Together," I agree, and the four of us walk out into Highstone's cold
afternoon light, considerably more united in the waiting ahead than any
of us were facing this morning's uncertain hearing alone.

·

Tomas walks beside me on the way back to the cottage, quiet in the
particular way of a young man processing something he hasn't quite
worked out how to say.

"Do you think he'll actually move quickly enough," he asks, finally.

"I don't know," I admit. "I think he means what he said. I think
'quickly enough' and 'quickly by Ossory's own standards' might turn out
to be two different measures, and I don't yet know which one we're
actually going to get."

"That frightens me," Tomas says, plainly.

"It frightens me too," I say. "I don't think there's any use pretending
otherwise, to you specifically, given how much of today you've already
witnessed firsthand."

"Thank you for not pretending," Tomas says. "I think I'd rather be
frightened honestly, alongside you, than reassured falsely and left to
discover the fear on my own later."

"I'll keep choosing honestly, then," I say, "whatever it costs either
of us in the short term," and we walk the rest of the way together, the
whole small company considerably more bound by shared, unmanaged fear
than any comforting pretense could have made them.

·

Aldric is waiting at the cottage when we finally arrive, considerably
steadier than the morning left him, and reads the whole shape of the
day's outcome from our faces before any of us says a word.

"He didn't sanction it," Aldric says.

"Not yet," I say. "He's taking it seriously, properly. I think that's
the most honest thing I can currently say about it."

"That's more than nothing," Aldric says, taking my hand. "I know it's
not everything either of us wanted walking in this morning."

"I know," I say, "and I've decided I'd rather have an honest 'not yet'
than a comfortable lie dressed up as progress."

"So would I," Aldric agrees, and the whole small household settles
into the cottage's evening quiet together, considerably more prepared
for whatever waiting still lies ahead, having faced the day's uncertain
outcome honestly, together, rather than each of them managing a
separate private disappointment alone.

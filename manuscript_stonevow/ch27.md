## Chapter Twenty-Seven — The Mutual Form (Isla)

Ossory listens to the whole of it — the numb hand, the record from
Calder's Reach, the specific arithmetic of a season we no longer have
time to run cautiously — in the record-room at Highstone, with the
particular gravity of a man who has spent considerably longer than I
have watching this line try, and sometimes fail, to hold.

"You want to attempt an unproven rite," he says, when I've finished,
"on the strength of one dead wardwright's private record, with a hearth
already this far gone. Do you understand what you're asking me to
sanction, Wardwright?"

"I understand precisely what happens if we don't attempt it," I say.
"I've watched the alternative up close, for six years. I know its
shape considerably better than I know the shape of what I'm proposing
instead."

I lay the predecessor's record beside my own training, side by side on
the table, and say, finally, out loud, the thing I have been circling
since Calder's Reach, unwilling to voice it until I had proof enough to
survive the saying.

"The rite I was taught takes from one hearth alone," I say. "There's an
older form. It shares the cost between both of us, instead of spending
only him. I believe it's genuine. I believe it's survivable, properly
attempted. I am not certain of either, which is why I haven't said this
plainly until today, and why I am saying it now, plainly, in front of
you, rather than attempting it quietly and reporting only the outcome."

Saying it changes the room. I feel it change — the specific settling
of a discovery finally spoken where it can be tested against the
world, rather than merely turned over privately, alone, in the dark.

"Why has no one used it in generations," Ossory asks, careful, already,
I suspect, guessing at an answer he does not want confirmed.

"Because sharing the cost means the wardwright pays too," I say. "And
every generation of rite-keepers since has found it considerably
easier to spend one hearth quietly than risk two wardwrights loudly."

Aldric goes very still beside me, hearing that, and I understand,
watching him, exactly what he's registering: that I am proposing to
spend myself for him precisely as visibly, precisely as completely, as
he has spent himself, silently, for everyone in his own life since he
was fourteen years old.

"You'd take that cost onto yourself," Aldric says, quietly, "knowingly.
Openly."

"Yes," I say. "That's rather the whole point of the mutual form. Cost
shared, rather than concentrated into a single hearth until it kills
him. I'm not offering you a rescue that costs me nothing. I'm offering
you the only version of this rite that has any chance, at all, of
letting both of us survive the season."

Ossory looks between the two of us for a long moment, weighing
considerably more than the record on the table.

"I'll need to see the full text properly translated," he says,
finally, "and I'll need the crown's ward-commissioner informed, whether
either of you would prefer that or not, because an attempt this
significant cannot be made entirely off the crown's record without
inviting considerably worse consequences than Feld's usual
bureaucratic tedium."

"Understood," I say.

"I am not sanctioning an attempt tonight," Ossory adds, "or this week.
I want the translation checked, and I want time to consider what
you're actually asking this line's whole rite-keeping tradition to
risk. But I will not, this time, simply dismiss it because it's
inconvenient to the crown's preferred arithmetic. That much, I think,
you've earned, bringing it to me plainly rather than attempting it in
secret."

It is not, I understand, walking out of the record-room afterward, a
victory. It is not even, strictly, permission. But it is the first
time, in the whole of my career, that I have said the true, difficult
thing out loud, to someone with the standing to act on it, rather than
managing it quietly, alone, in the dark, and finding that the saying
itself has changed something I did not expect it to change.

"Thank you," Aldric says, once we're alone in the corridor, "for
saying it plainly. I know what it cost you, offering yourself that
visibly."

"I've spent six years offering myself invisibly instead," I say. "I
find I'd rather be seen doing it, if the alternative is watching you
die quietly while I keep careful, insufficient account of the reasons
why."

·

Tomas catches up with us in the corridor outside the record-room,
having clearly been listening at the door for at least part of the
conversation, and does not bother pretending otherwise.

"You're actually going to attempt it," he says, something between awe
and terror in his voice. "The mutual form."

"If Ossory sanctions a proper attempt, once the translation's checked.
Yes."

"I want to help," Tomas says, immediately, with the specific
determination of a young man who has spent weeks feeling useless in the
face of a crisis considerably larger than his own training has
prepared him for. "Not the rite itself — I know I'm not ready for that.
But the translation, the preparation, whatever needs doing before
Ossory's satisfied it's safe to attempt."

"You can help," I say. "I'd welcome it, actually. Cross-checking my own
translation against the fragments in the record box, making certain I
haven't let my own hope color the reading in places where the ink's
gone unclear."

"I won't let hope color it," Tomas says, with the particular gravity
of someone taking an assignment considerably more seriously than the
assignment, on its surface, might warrant. "I'll be as rigorous as you
taught me to be."

"I know you will," I say, and mean it, understanding that handing him
this task is itself a kind of gift — a way of letting him participate
properly in the thing that will determine both his own future and the
future of everyone he cares about, rather than simply waiting, helpless,
on the sidelines of a decision made entirely by the adults around him.

We spend the rest of that afternoon with the record box spread across
the workshop table, the three of us — Tomas, Aldric, and I — checking
every fragment of the translation against each other, arguing, gently,
over a handful of ambiguous phrases, building, together, a version of
the rite's words that all three of us can properly stand behind before
Ossory sees a single line of it.

It is, I think, watching Tomas argue a fine point of grammar with
Aldric over a passage neither of them fully agrees on, the first time
this entire winter that the whole weight of the mutual rite has felt
like something we're building together, rather than something I am
carrying alone and simply reporting on to everyone else.

·

Ossory does not answer immediately, once I've finished laying the whole
of it out — the record, the translation, the plain admission of what
I'm proposing. He sits with the silence for a long while, turning the
predecessor's own pages over in his hands, and I understand, watching
him, that I have just handed him something he is going to need
considerably more than an afternoon to properly weigh.

"I will not pretend this is a small thing to decide," he says, finally.
"I need time to verify the translation properly, against whatever
fragmentary records I can find elsewhere. I need to consider what
sanctioning an unproven form means for every future binding on this
line, not merely the one currently in front of me."

"I understand," I say, though the words cost me, given how little time
Aldric's own condition affords for anyone's proper deliberation.

"I am not refusing you," Ossory adds, seeing my face. "I am telling you
that I intend to take this seriously, which means I cannot decide it
carelessly, however urgent your own timeline has become. Give me what
time you can afford, Wardwright. I promise you I will not waste a single
day of it."

I leave the record-room with considerably less certainty than I'd
hoped for, and considerably more than I feared — not a sanction, not
yet, but a genuine hearing, properly weighed, from a man whose careful
formality has never once, in six years, struck me as cover for
indifference.

·

Aldric is waiting outside, and I tell him the whole of the exchange,
watching him absorb it with the particular steadiness he's shown more
and more, these past weeks, toward news that would once have frightened
him into managing it silently.

"He didn't refuse," Aldric says, turning the outcome over.

"No. He didn't refuse. He also didn't sanction, which I understand,
intellectually, is the responsible position for a man in his role to
take. I find myself frustrated by it anyway."

"You're allowed to be frustrated," Aldric says, "even by a reasonable
caution. I don't think the two things cancel each other out."

"I know," I say. "I think I wanted, somewhere beneath the reasonable
part of me, for him to simply say yes, immediately, and let us begin
tomorrow. I understand why he can't. I'm frustrated regardless."

"For what it's worth," Aldric says, "I think you did exactly what
needed doing. You said it plainly, finally, to someone with the
standing to act on it. Whatever he decides, however long it takes him
to decide it, you've done the part that was actually yours to do."

I lean into him, there in the corridor outside Ossory's record-room,
and let myself, for a moment, simply be held rather than managing the
next step immediately, understanding that the waiting ahead is going
to require considerably more patience than either of us currently
feels equipped to offer.

·

Sela finds us still in the corridor, some while later, having come to
Highstone on an errand of her own and stumbling, by apparent accident,
onto the tail end of the day's much larger news.

"Something's happened," she says, reading our faces plainly. "Tell me."

I tell her, the whole of it — the numb hand's full extent, the record
from Calder's Reach, the mutual form finally spoken aloud to Ossory —
and watch her absorb each piece with the specific gravity of a woman
who has spent this entire winter bracing for exactly this scale of
news.

"You're proposing to spend yourself," she says, to me, once I've
finished, "the same way he's already spending himself. Knowingly."

"Yes," I say.

Sela is quiet a long moment, and I understand, watching her, that she
is weighing something considerably larger than simple approval or
disapproval.

"I don't know whether to thank you or be frightened of you," she says,
finally, with the particular dry honesty I've come to expect from her.
"I think, on balance, I'm going to do both. Thank you, for offering
what nobody else in this family has ever offered him. And be
frightened, because I don't think either of you fully knows yet what
it's actually going to cost."

"Neither do I," I admit. "I don't think anyone alive knows precisely
what it costs. That's rather the whole difficulty of proposing an
unproven rite."

"Then I suppose we're all simply going to have to be frightened
together," Sela says, "rather than each of us managing our own private
version of it separately, the way this family's always preferred to
do things." She looks between the two of us. "For what it's worth, I
trust you. Both of you. I don't think I'd have said that, plainly, a
month ago. I'm saying it now."

"Thank you," Aldric says, quietly, and I watch brother and sister share
a look that carries considerably more warmth than either of them
generally lets show in front of others, and I understand, standing
with them in the corridor, that whatever the mutual form eventually
costs any of us, it has already, in the asking, begun mending
something in this family that has been quietly fraying for
considerably longer than this particular winter accounts for.

·

Sela walks with us partway back to the cottage, unwilling, I think, to
let the evening end without saying one further thing that's clearly
been sitting with her since the corridor.

"I want to help properly," she says, "with whatever the mutual form
ends up requiring. Not simply moral support from the sidelines.
Something actually useful."

"What did you have in mind," I ask.

"I don't know the rite," Sela says, "but I know this family's finances,
and I know the crown's own particular weaknesses better than either of
you, having spent years managing our exemption paperwork against
exactly this kind of institutional resistance. If Ossory needs
convincing, or Feld needs managing, I'd like to be the one handling
that portion of it, so neither of you has to split your attention
between the rite itself and the crown's own bureaucracy."

"That's a considerable offer," Aldric says.

"It's not entirely selfless," Sela admits. "I've spent this whole
crisis feeling useless, watching from the orchard while the two of you
carry the actual weight of it. I'd like something to actually do,
rather than simply worry from a comfortable distance."

"I'd welcome that," I say, "more than you know. I think Ossory takes
the whole matter more seriously with a family's practical voice in the
room, alongside the wardwright's own argument."

"Then it's settled," Sela says, with the particular satisfaction of a
woman who has just been handed a genuine role rather than a comforting
platitude, and the three of us continue on toward the cottage together,
each of us now carrying a specific piece of the work ahead, rather
than one person shouldering the whole of it while the others simply
watch.

·

We stop, on the way, at the small grain-house where Tomas has taken to
waiting when he's finished his own evening reports, and find him
already asleep at the table, exhausted past the point of properly
making it back to his own quarters.

"He's been working himself considerably too hard," Sela observes,
gently.

"He wanted to be useful," I say, "in whatever way he could manage. I
don't think either of us has properly told him it's alright to rest
too, alongside all the effort."

"Then let's tell him," Sela says, and we wake him gently, and I watch
her fuss over him with the same practical, unshowy care she extends
her own brother, insisting he come back to the cottage for a proper
night's rest rather than sleep sitting upright at a grain-house table.

"I didn't mean to fall asleep here," Tomas says, embarrassed, gathering
his things.

"You're allowed to rest," I say. "Properly. You've done everything
that could reasonably be asked of you today, and considerably more
besides."

"I keep forgetting that," Tomas admits, "that rest is allowed, not
simply a failure to keep working hard enough."

"We're all still learning that lesson," Sela says, "at every age, apparently,"
and the four of us walk the rest of the way together, considerably
more settled for the small, necessary reminder that even the most
willing hands need proper rest to keep being useful.

·

Tomas walks beside Sela for the final stretch, still visibly
embarrassed about being caught asleep at his post, and I catch the two
of them talking quietly, easily, in a register that tells me their own
friendship has grown into something considerably warmer than simple
acquaintance.

"They get along well," Aldric observes, to me, walking a few paces behind.

"They do," I agree. "I've noticed it building for weeks now."

"Do you think it's more than friendship," he asks, "or am I simply
projecting my own hopeful mood onto two people who are, in fact, simply
friendly?"

"I don't know," I say, considering it properly. "I think it might
be more, eventually, if it isn't already becoming that. I think we
should let them find their own pace with it, though, rather than
speculating too loudly where either of them might overhear."

"Fair," Aldric agrees, and we walk the rest of the way in comfortable quiet,
both of us quietly hopeful about a small, tender possibility neither
of us intends to rush.

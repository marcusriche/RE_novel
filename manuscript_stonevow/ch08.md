## Chapter Eight — What the Ledger Doesn't Say (Aldric)

I bring Isla up to the orchard for the first time nine days after the
binding, ostensibly to inspect a cairn nobody's checked since the
snow started in earnest, actually because I want her to see where I'm
from before the rite decides that for her some other way, on its own
schedule, without either of us having had any say in the introduction.

The orchard sits above the line the way it's always sat above the
line — the last living rows of grafted trees before the ground gives
over entirely to the kind of cold that doesn't grow anything anymore,
a fact I have lived with long enough that I've mostly stopped noticing
it as strange, the way you stop noticing the specific shape of your
own house once you've lived in it long enough.

I watch Isla take in the whole shape of it as we climb, cataloguing
the rows the way she catalogues everything, and understand, watching
her, that I am showing her considerably more than an orchard this
morning — the whole quiet architecture of a life I have never properly
described to anyone the rite hadn't already bound to me.

Sela is in the grain-house when we arrive, records spread across the
counting table in the particular organized chaos of a woman who knows
exactly where everything is despite appearances suggesting otherwise.
She looks up at Isla the way a sister looks at the woman her brother's
been bound to for someone else's sake — carefully, assessing, not
unkindly, but not yet willing to spend more warmth than the
introduction strictly requires.

"So you're the wardwright," Sela says, by way of greeting.

"Isla," Isla says, offering the correction plainly, without any
particular insistence behind it, in the way she offers most
corrections — a fact stated once, and then left alone to do its own
work.

"Sela." A pause, brief, assessing. "He's told me almost nothing about
you. Which is either very good manners or very bad ones, depending on
which brother I'm currently annoyed at."

"Good manners," I say. "I didn't think it my place to describe her
before she'd had the chance to describe herself."

"That's a very diplomatic answer," Sela says, "for a man who's never
once in his life been diplomatic about anything that actually mattered
to him." She turns back to Isla, considering. "Would you like to see
the grain record? I don't generally show it to visitors. But you're not
quite a visitor anymore, are you."

She shows it to Isla without further prompting, once Isla agrees, laying
out the whole ledger of what the exemption actually cost the household —
grain traded away in the specific lean years after the crown tightened
its terms, the particular silence that fell over the orchard the week
I put my own name forward instead of letting Sela's stand exposed on
the roll.

"He never told me the rest of it," Sela says, meaning the binding,
meaning whatever the two of them currently are to each other, watching
Isla read the numbers with the specific stillness of a woman doing
arithmetic she already suspects the shape of before she's finished the
sum. "He never tells anyone the rest of anything. It's his worst habit,
and I've watched him practice it since we were children."

"I've noticed," Isla says, and there is something in the way she says
it — dry, exact, not unkind — that tells me she has, in fact, noticed a
great deal more about my particular habit of managing costs quietly
than I have yet admitted to her directly.

I leave them to the ledger and go to check the cairn at the orchard's
northern edge, mostly because I suspect the two of them will speak more
freely without me standing there managing how the conversation lands,
and because I have found, over twenty-eight years of being Sela's
brother, that she tells strangers things about me she would never say
to my face, on the theory that strangers deserve accurate information
even when I'd rather they didn't have it.

The cairn reads low, same as the last three inspections, the stone's
charge thinner than it ought to be for this early in the season. I note
it in the small record book I've started keeping myself, an
apprentice's habit I picked up without meaning to simply from watching
Isla keep hers, and stand a while longer looking north, toward the line
proper, at the specific flat grey nothing that marks where the Rime has
already taken more ground than any ledger I've seen accounts for.

When I come back down, Isla and Sela have moved from the grain record
to something considerably less formal — an old story, apparently, about
a Marrow cousin's disastrous wedding, both of them laughing in the
particular unguarded way of two people who have decided, somewhere in
the last hour, that the other one is worth actually liking rather than
merely tolerating for someone else's sake.

"You've corrupted my sister," I say, mildly, coming through the door.

"She corrupted herself," Sela says. "I merely provided the material."

Isla looks, for a moment, genuinely lighter than I have seen her look
since the binding, and I file the sight away carefully, the way I file
everything about her that I don't yet have anywhere proper to put.

·

We walk back down toward Highstone in the last of the light, Isla quiet
in the particular way that means she's turning something over rather
than simply being silent.

"Your sister's grain record doesn't balance," she says, eventually.
"Not this season. Not by a margin she can afford to keep absorbing
quietly for much longer."

"I know," I say.

"You've known for a while."

"Since before the binding," I admit. "It's part of why I put my own
name forward when the crown tightened the exemption terms. The
household couldn't have absorbed both the grain shortfall and losing
Sela's protection at once."

"You never told her that's part of your reasoning."

"No," I say. "She'd have argued. She'd have insisted on some
arrangement where the cost fell more evenly between us, and I'd
already decided, by the time the terms tightened, that I'd rather carry
it myself than watch her carry any part of it at all."

Isla is quiet for a long moment, and I understand, from the specific
quality of the silence, that she has recognized something in what I've
just said — the exact shape, perhaps, of her own habit of feeding the
failing wards her own heat and calling it, silently, nothing worth
mentioning.

"That's not generosity," she says, finally. "Not entirely. I don't
mean that unkindly. I mean it's a flaw wearing generosity's coat, and
I recognize the coat because I own one exactly like it."

I do not have a ready answer to that, mostly because I suspect, hearing
it, that she is entirely correct, and I am not yet prepared to examine
how correct.

"We should get you home before the cold gets worse," I say instead, and
she lets me change the subject, this once, though I feel her filing the
observation away all the same, for whichever later evening either of us
finally runs out of room to keep managing it quietly.

·

Sela catches up with us at the orchard gate before we've fully left,
breathless, having apparently run the whole distance from the
grain-house.

"You forgot the seed count," she says, handing Isla a folded sheet,
"for the crown's grain office. I know it's not strictly your concern,
Wardwright, but Aldric mentioned you'd offered to look it over, given
you've a better head for the crown's particular arithmetic than either
of us."

Isla takes the sheet and reads it properly, right there at the gate,
the specific focused stillness she gives any column of numbers, and I
watch Sela watch her do it, a small, considering look passing over my
sister's face that I recognize, having grown up with it aimed at every
person I've ever brought home, as the beginning of genuine approval
rather than merely polite tolerance.

"This is wrong," Isla says, after a moment, tapping a line near the
bottom. "The crown's office undercounts your winter stores by a
margin that would leave you short by spring, if they went by this
figure alone. You'll want to resubmit with the actual total, properly
itemized, or they'll base next year's exemption allotment on a number
that doesn't reflect what you actually have."

"I told him it looked low," Sela says, to me, with the specific
satisfaction of a woman whose own instincts have just been vindicated
by someone she trusts to know better. "He said the crown's office
wouldn't quibble over a small discrepancy."

"The crown's office quibbles over everything," Isla says. "That's
rather the whole of what their office does. I'd resubmit properly,
if I were you, and I'd keep a copy of the correction, in case they try
to claim the original figure stands."

Sela looks between the two of us, something settling in her expression
that I don't fully have words for yet, though I suspect, watching her,
that it's the specific relief of a woman who has spent years being the
only one in the household paying close enough attention to the crown's
particular tricks, finally finding someone else willing to share that
particular vigilance.

"Thank you," Sela says, to Isla, plainly, without any of the careful
distance she kept at their first meeting. "Properly. Not just for the
correction. For noticing it was wrong at all, when neither of us
thought to look twice."

"It's what I do," Isla says, though I notice her looking, for a moment,
genuinely pleased by the thanks rather than simply accepting it as her
professional due.

We walk the rest of the way down from the orchard with the corrected
seed count folded carefully in Isla's own coat pocket, and I find
myself, watching the easy warmth that's grown between her and my
sister in the space of a single visit, considerably more hopeful about
the shape of whatever we're all building together than I have let
myself be in longer than I can properly place.

·

We stop, on the walk back, at the small shrine near the orchard's own
boundary — not a proper cairn, simply a weathered marker where the
family has, for generations, left small offerings for whatever
half-remembered spirits the old folk once believed watched over the
last living trees this far north.

"My mother used to bring us here," I say, "every first frost, without
fail. I haven't come regularly since she died. Sela does, I think, more
often than she lets on."

Isla looks at the marker properly, reading whatever weathered carving
remains legible on its surface.

"What did she believe it did," Isla asks, "the offering?"

"I don't think she believed it did anything, precisely," I say. "I
think she believed in the doing of it. The specific discipline of
stopping, once a year, to acknowledge that the orchard's survival
wasn't entirely within our own control, regardless of how carefully we
tended it."

"That's not so different from what a ward actually is," Isla says,
considering. "An acknowledgment that some things are genuinely beyond
a single person's management, dressed up in whatever ritual language a
given tradition happens to prefer."

I leave a small offering at the marker, this time, the first time I've
done so since my mother's death — a strip of grafting wax, the only
thing I currently have on me that feels properly mine to give — and
find, doing it, that the gesture costs me considerably less than I
expected, and returns, somehow, considerably more.

"Thank you," I say to Isla, "for making me want to stop and actually do
this, rather than simply walking past it the way I have for years."

"I didn't make you do anything," Isla says. "I think you'd have gotten
here eventually, regardless."

"Perhaps," I say, "but I don't think I'd have gotten here today,
without you standing beside me while I did."

·

We find Tomas waiting at the cottage when we finally return, an
unusual evening visit that tells me, before he's said anything at all,
that something's on his mind he's been saving up the nerve to raise. He
has clearly been waiting some while, judging by how thoroughly he's
already organized the evening's reports into their proper stacks.

"Can I ask you both something," he says, once the pleasantries are
managed, "about the whole arrangement. Not the practical parts. The
actual — feeling of it."

"Go on," Isla says.

"Do you ever worry," Tomas asks, careful, "that the rite made this
happen, rather than the two of you actually choosing it? I've read the
old accounts. The bond's meant to create considerable pressure toward
attachment, regardless of whether the two people involved would have
chosen each other freely."

It's a fair question, and I understand, watching Isla consider it
properly rather than dismissing it, that she's given it real thought
herself.

"I worried about exactly that, early on," she says. "I still check
myself against it, some days. I think the honest answer is that the
rite gave us proximity, and proximity gave us the chance to actually
see each other properly. But the choosing — the actual wanting, once we
knew each other — I don't think that's the rite's doing. I think that's
simply what happened, given the chance to happen at all."

"How do you know the difference," Tomas presses, "between the rite's
pull and your own actual want?"

"We asked each other," I say, "plainly, more than once, whether the
wanting was ours. We keep asking. I think that's the only honest answer
available — not a certainty, exactly, but a practice, repeated often
enough that we've both come to trust it."

Tomas considers this, nodding slowly, filing the answer away with the
particular seriousness he gives most things that matter to him.

"Thank you," he says, "for actually answering that properly, instead of
simply telling me not to worry about it."

"You're allowed to worry about it," Isla says. "I'd rather you ask hard
questions than simply assume everything's settled because it looks
settled from outside."

·

Tomas lingers after the main question's been properly answered,
turning something else over, and I recognize, watching him, the
specific hesitation of a young man who has one more thing to ask and
isn't entirely certain it's his place to ask it.

"Can I say something," he says, finally, "that isn't really a
question?"

"Go on," Isla says.

"I think watching the two of you has changed what I expect from my own
future," Tomas says. "Before this winter, I assumed being a wardwright
meant being alone, eventually, the way you were alone before Aldric
arrived. I've started thinking, watching this, that maybe it doesn't
have to. That maybe I'm allowed to want something like this for myself,
someday, rather than assuming the job requires giving it up."

"You are allowed," Isla says, gently. "I don't think I understood that
myself, until fairly recently. I'd like you to learn it considerably
sooner than I did."

"I intend to," Tomas says, with a seriousness that makes me smile,
watching him. "I've decided I'd like an ordinary life alongside the
wardwright one, eventually, rather than choosing one at the expense of
the other, the way I always assumed the job demanded."

"I think that's exactly right," I say. "I spent most of my own life
assuming duty and happiness were somehow opposed, that choosing one
meant sacrificing the other. I don't believe that anymore. I don't
think either of you should have to believe it either."

Tomas nods, absorbing this, and takes his leave for the evening,
considerably lighter, I think, for having said the thought aloud rather
than simply carrying it privately, and Isla and I sit together a while
longer, grateful for a young man determined to build a different kind
of future than the one this line has always, until now, quietly
assumed was the only one available.

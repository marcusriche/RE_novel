## Chapter Nine — Held Ground (Aldric)

A fortnight and a half in, and the cottage has quietly, thoroughly rearranged itself
around two people instead of one, in a hundred small ways neither of us
has discussed and both of us have apparently agreed to without ever
voting on it formally: a second chair pulled permanently to the fire, a
particular mug that has become, without a single conversation about it,
mine, a boot-rack by the door that now holds two pairs instead of one
without either of us having built it larger to accommodate the
difference.

I notice the rearranging the way I notice most things about this
cottage now — sideways, in passing, the specific accumulation of small
facts that add up, eventually, to something considerably larger than
any single fact would suggest on its own.

"You've stopped flinching when I come in," I say, one evening, mild,
not making a particular thing of it, mostly because I have learned, in
a fortnight and a half, that Isla answers observations offered lightly
considerably more honestly than she answers ones offered as though
they matter.

"I've stopped expecting you to cost me something every time you do,"
she says, and I hear, in the way she says it, exactly how much she has
just admitted without quite meaning to.

Neither of us corrects the sentence. The fire holds the room's whole
attention for a while after that, which is its own kind of agreement,
the specific comfortable silence of two people who have each, privately,
decided that the thing sitting between them doesn't need naming yet to
be true.

"Can I ask you something," I say, eventually, "that isn't strictly
practical."

"You may."

"Do you regret it. The rite naming me instead of some other hearth. I
know it's early to ask. I've been wondering anyway."

Isla considers the question with the specific seriousness she gives
most things I ask her plainly, turning it over rather than answering
reflexively.

"No," she says, finally. "I was frightened of it, the night of the
binding. I'm still frightened of parts of it. But I don't regret the
name the rite read. I've caught myself, more than once this fortnight,
grateful for it, which is not a sentence I expected to have available
to me a month ago."

"That's more than I expected you to say."

"I've decided," Isla says, "that half-truths cost more between us than
whole ones do. I'm trying to act accordingly, even when the whole truth
is inconvenient to admit."

I think about that for a long moment, watching the fire do its slow,
steady work, and find myself, without quite deciding to, offering the
same honesty back.

"I decided the same thing, the night of the binding," I say. "Watching
you not let your face do anything, standing in front of the whole
assembly. I thought — there's a woman who's spent her whole life
managing what she lets people see. I'd like to know what she looks
like when she's not managing anything at all."

"And do you," Isla asks. "Know, now."

"Some of it," I say. "More than I expected to, this early. I suspect
there's considerably more you haven't shown anyone yet, including me."

"There is," she admits, quietly, and does not elaborate further, and I
let the sentence stand exactly where she's left it, because I have
learned, watching her, that some doors open slowly, on their own
schedule, and pushing on them only teaches a person to keep them
shut more carefully next time.

We sit a while longer without speaking, the specific companionable
silence that has become, in a fortnight and a half, considerably more
comfortable than either of us, I suspect, would have predicted the
night the rite-cord first closed around our wrists.

"Tomas thinks we're courting," I say, eventually, mostly to see how
she'll take the observation.

"Tomas thinks a great many things," Isla says, "most of which he keeps
to himself until he's decided the moment's ripe for maximum effect."

"Is he wrong?"

Isla doesn't answer immediately, which is, I am learning, its own kind
of answer — the specific pause of a woman deciding whether a truth is
ready to be spoken aloud or whether it needs a while longer sitting
quietly, unexamined, before it can survive being said.

"I don't know yet," she says, finally, honestly. "I know the arrangement
has stopped feeling like an arrangement. I haven't decided what that
means it's become instead."

"That's fair," I say, and mean it, and do not push for more, because I
have decided, watching her navigate this fortnight with considerably
more honesty than I suspect comes naturally to her, that the not-yet-
knowing is itself a kind of progress worth respecting on its own terms.

The fire burns down slowly, and neither of us moves to build it back
up, and the wariness that opened this whole strange arrangement has, I
notice, become something closer to ground held rather than ground
defended — neither of us naming it first, both of us, apparently,
content to let it stand exactly as it currently is for one evening
longer.

·

Isla asks me, later that same evening, once the fire's gone properly
low, about my own mother — a subject I have not, in three weeks,
volunteered, and one she has clearly been waiting for the right
moment to raise.

"Sela's mentioned her twice," Isla says, carefully, "always in passing,
always changing the subject quickly after. I've noticed you do the
same. I'm not asking to press on something painful. I'm asking because
I'd like to actually know you, and I suspect this is a part of you that
matters."

"She died when I was nine," I say. "Sela was six. A fever, nothing the
line or the rite had any hand in — an ordinary sickness, the kind that
takes people regardless of how carefully anyone's managed their own
particular arithmetic of risk. My father raised us alone after that,
and I think, looking back, that's rather where I learned the whole
habit properly — watching him manage two children and an orchard and
his own grief, all at once, without once letting either of us see the
managing cost him anything."

"That's a heavy thing to learn at nine."

"I don't think I understood it as heavy, learning it," I say. "I
understood it as simply how a person who loves someone behaves. It
took considerably longer than nine years old to understand that my
father's silence about his own grief wasn't strength, exactly. It was
simply the only tool he had, and he handed it to me and Sela both,
without meaning to, the way you hand down anything you've never
examined closely enough to know it's worth questioning."

Isla is quiet for a long moment, turning that over with the particular
gentleness she reserves for things that matter to her considerably more
than she generally lets show.

"I think I understand you rather better now," she says, finally. "The
orchard. The exemption. All of it. You didn't simply decide, as an
adult, to start carrying costs alone. You were taught it, early, by a
man who loved you and didn't know another way to show it."

"I think that's accurate," I say, surprised, as I often am with her, by
how precisely she's named something I'd never quite managed to
articulate to myself in those exact terms.

"For what it's worth," Isla says, "I think you're allowed to unlearn
it. Slowly. Imperfectly. I don't think either of us is going to manage
it in a single winter. But I'd like to try unlearning it alongside you,
if you're willing to let me."

"I'm willing," I say, and mean it more than the simple words let on,
and we sit together a while longer in the low firelight, two people
each quietly working at unlearning the exact same inherited habit,
neither of us expecting to finish the work quickly, both of us,
apparently, willing to keep at it regardless.

·

Tomas asks me, the following morning, in the careful sideways manner he
reserves for his most genuine curiosities, whether I've ever considered
that the rite itself might have been designed, originally, with
precisely this kind of shared unlearning in mind.

"What do you mean," I ask, setting aside the morning's reports.

"Well," Tomas says, working the thought out as he speaks, "the whole
point of binding a wardwright to a hearth is supposedly to give the
wardwright someone to draw cost from. But what if the actual original
point — before whatever corruption happened to it — was to give the
wardwright someone to be honest with. Someone who'd notice, properly,
if she started hiding things. Not simply a battery to draw on. A
witness."

I sit with that observation for a long moment, turning it over with
considerably more seriousness than I expect its source to warrant.

"That's a remarkably astute theory," I say, "for a nineteen-year-old
who's spent three years mostly sorting reports and drilling cadence."

"I've had considerable time to think, this whole winter," Tomas says,
with a dry precision that reminds me, not for the first time, exactly
how much of my own manner he has absorbed simply through years of
proximity. "And I've watched you change, since the binding. Not just
softened. Actually witnessed, in a way I don't think you'd ever
properly let anyone witness you before."

"You witness me constantly, Tomas. You've been doing it for three
years."

"That's different," Tomas says. "I witness the wardwright. Aldric
witnesses you. I think that's rather the distinction the old rite was
always meant to draw, before whoever corrupted it decided a witness
was a luxury the crown's arithmetic couldn't afford to fund."

I do not have a ready response to being handed, so plainly, by my own
apprentice, an observation about the rite's own possible history that I
have never once heard voiced by anyone else at Highstone, so I simply
thank him, and go back to the day's business, carrying his words
carefully alongside everything else I am slowly, privately, beginning
to wonder about a rite I had, until this exact conversation, never once
questioned the origins of.

·

I mention Tomas's observation to Isla that evening, curious what she
makes of a theory this unorthodox coming from so young a source.

"He might be onto something," Isla says, considering it properly. "I've
spent six years assuming the rite's current shape was simply how it had
always been. I'm troubled, hearing his theory, that I never once
properly examined that assumption myself, given how thoroughly I've
studied everything else about this line."

"That's rather what unsettles me too," I admit, "though from the
opposite direction — that a practice meant to protect a line would be
designed to isolate the two people most responsible for holding it,
rather than bringing them closer together, and nobody along the way
apparently thought to question it."

"Do you think there's a way to find out? Properly, I mean. Not just
Tomas's theory, however astute."

"I don't know," I say, honestly. "The crown's own archive has nothing.
Ossian's private journal only hints at doubt, not answers. I suppose
if there's a fuller record anywhere, it would have to be considerably
older than anything currently kept at Highstone."

"Then perhaps that's worth looking for," I say, "properly, when
there's time for it."

I don't know, that evening, how soon that time is actually going to
arrive, or how urgently. I only know, turning the whole conversation
over before sleep, that some part of me has already begun quietly
searching, in the back of my mind, for wherever such an older record
might be waiting to be found.

·

Sela visits the cottage the following evening, and I find myself,
watching her settle in by the fire with Isla, turning Tomas's theory
over once more, curious whether my sister might have some fragment of
family history that could shed further light on it.

"Did Father ever mention anything," I ask her, "about the rite's
history. Where it came from, originally. Whether it was always
practiced the way we know it now."

Sela considers this, surprised by the question.

"Not directly," she says, "though I remember him saying, once, that
Grandmother kept old habits about the line that he never fully
understood himself. Some mark she kept over the door. Some phrase she
used, about hearths and witnesses, that he always assumed was simply
an old woman's superstition rather than anything with real weight
behind it."

"A mark over the door," I say, feeling something settle into place
that I can't quite name yet. "Do you remember what it looked like?"

"Rings," Sela says, after a moment's thought. "Several of them,
interlocking. I haven't thought about it in years. It came down when
Father repaired the lintel, sometime after Mother died. I don't think
either of us thought to ask why it was there in the first place."

Isla goes very still, hearing this, and I understand, watching her,
that whatever Sela's just described has landed considerably harder
than a simple family memory should.

"That's worth remembering properly," Isla says, carefully. "I don't
know yet what it means. But I don't think it's nothing."

"Should I be worried," Sela asks, looking between the two of us.

"Not yet," Isla says. "Possibly not at all. I simply think there might
be more to this line's history than either of us currently understands,
and I'd rather gather every fragment of it now, while people still
remember them, than realize later we let something important go
unrecorded."

Sela nods, accepting this, though I catch her filing the whole exchange
away with the same careful attention she gives everything that might
eventually matter to this family's safety, and I find myself, watching
the two women I trust most in the world turn this small mystery over
together, considerably more certain than I was this morning that
Tomas's theory deserves rather more serious pursuit than either of us
initially gave it credit for.

·

Sela lingers after Isla has gone to fetch her own notebook, turning
something over privately before she speaks again.

"Do you think there's more of Grandmother's own history we've simply
never thought to ask about," she says, quietly, "beyond the mark over
the door?"

"I don't know," I admit. "I don't think either of us properly knew
her, growing up. She died before I was old enough to ask anything that
mattered."

"I keep thinking," Sela says, "about how much of this family's own
history has apparently just been sitting unexamined, waiting for
someone to finally ask the right question. First your hand. Now this.
I wonder what else we've simply inherited without ever properly
understanding."

"I don't know," I say again, "but I find I want to find out, properly,
alongside everything else this winter's already asking of us."

"So do I," Sela says, and we sit together a moment longer before Isla
returns, both of us turning over the specific strange comfort of
discovering that a family's history holds considerably more mystery,
and considerably more inheritance worth claiming, than either of us
ever thought to look for.

·

Isla returns with her notebook, and the three of us spend the rest of
the evening carefully reconstructing whatever fragments of family
history either Sela or I can properly recall — half-remembered
phrases, small habits neither of us thought to question at the time,
the specific shape of a grandmother neither of us knew well enough to
ask the right questions of while she was still here to answer them.

"This is considerably more than I expected to find, simply by asking,"
Isla observes, reviewing her own careful notes.

"I think that's rather the whole lesson of this entire winter," I say.
"Considerably more is usually available, once someone finally asks."

We finish the evening with a fuller, if still incomplete, picture of
whatever older tradition my grandmother once quietly kept, and I find
myself, watching Isla carefully file the notes away, grateful for a
season that keeps handing this family new inheritances to properly
claim, one honest question at a time.

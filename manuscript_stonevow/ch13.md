## Chapter Thirteen — The Kiss (Isla)

Full dark, the following evening, the cairn field gone silver under a
sky clear enough that every standing stone throws its own particular
shadow, longer than daylight ever lets them manage.

I am the one who closes the distance.

It costs me something, visible, to do it — a woman who has spent a
decade taking costs silently, in private, without ever once letting
anyone watch the taking, for once taking one out loud, deliberately,
where it can be seen by the one person whose seeing of it actually
matters. I feel my own pulse do something unfamiliar, crossing the
last few feet of frozen ground between us, and I let it, rather than
managing it into something calmer the way I manage most things that
frighten me.

Aldric's hand at my jaw, when it arrives, is careful in a way that has
nothing to do with the cold and everything to do with a man who has
spent his whole life being careful with things he's afraid of breaking,
handling a graft, handling a wound, handling, now, apparently, me,
with the same exact attention he gives anything he's decided is worth
the trouble of not damaging.

The kiss itself is not scripted, not negotiated moment to moment beyond
the plain yes already given the evening before at the cairn field's
edge. It simply happens, the way a held breath finally happens into an
exhale — inevitable, once you've stopped holding it, and considerably
gentler than the anticipation of it led me to expect.

I have kissed exactly two people in my adult life before tonight,
neither of them recently, neither of them with anything like the
specific attention Aldric is currently giving the moment, as though I
am not simply a woman he's decided he wants but an actual, particular
person whose particular preferences are worth learning properly rather
than assumed.

Neither of us says anything, after. There isn't a sentence built yet
that would improve on the silence, and I find, standing in the cold
with his hand still at my jaw and my own hand fisted, without my having
decided to put it there, in the front of his coat, that I do not
especially want there to be one.

"That wasn't the bond," Aldric says, eventually, quiet, not quite a
question.

"No," I agree. "That was considerably too specific to be the bond. The
bond gives me sensation. It doesn't give me opinions about the exact
shape of your mouth."

He laughs, once, low, surprised out of him, and it is the first time I
have heard him laugh properly since the binding, unguarded, entirely
without the careful management he gives most of his own reactions.

"You have opinions about the shape of my mouth," he says.

"I have several," I say, "which I am not currently prepared to
enumerate, on the grounds that doing so would require considerably more
composure than I currently have available."

"That's fair," Aldric says, and kisses me again, unhurried, and I let
myself, for the length of that second kiss and the several after it,
stop running any arithmetic at all, which is, I understand even as I'm
doing it, the single rarest thing I have allowed myself in six years of
careful, constant counting.

·

We walk back to the cottage slowly, hand in hand, which is its own
small revolution — I have not walked anywhere holding another person's
hand since before my mentor's death, and I find the specific ordinary
warmth of it considerably more disarming than the kiss itself managed
to be.

"I want to tell you something," Aldric says, partway down the track,
"and I want you to hear it exactly as plainly as I mean it."

"Go on."

"I don't know what this is going to cost either of us, in the end. The
line's still failing. The rite's still taking more from me than either
of us has fully reckoned with yet. I don't have a version of tonight
where I promise you it all works out cleanly, because I don't believe
in promises I can't actually guarantee."

"I don't want that kind of promise," I say. "I've never trusted the
clean ones. I'd rather have the true one."

"Then here's the true one," Aldric says. "Whatever this costs either of
us, I'd rather spend it choosing you than have it taken from me some
other way, without my consent, by a rite neither of us built. That's
the whole of what I can honestly offer tonight."

"It's enough," I say, and mean it, and we walk the rest of the way to
the cottage in a silence that has stopped, entirely, feeling like the
wary quiet of two strangers bound together by circumstance, and started,
instead, to feel like something considerably closer to home.

·

Tomas is, predictably, waiting up when we finally return, ostensibly
finishing the evening's reports, actually, I suspect, simply curious
enough about how the evening went to have manufactured a reason to
still be at the cottage table when we walked in.

He takes one look at both of us — at whatever expression neither of us
has quite managed to smooth back into professional neutrality — and
sets down his pen with the specific ceremony of a young man determined
not to make a scene of the moment while making, in fact, rather a
scene of it.

"I'm not going to say anything," he announces.

"Good," I say.

"I'm simply going to note, for my own records, that you both look
considerably more settled than you did this morning, and that I take
full credit for whatever arrangement of the schedule allowed for a
private evening at the cairn field."

"You had nothing to do with tonight's schedule, Tomas."

"I had everything to do with it," he says, with enormous, entirely
unearned confidence. "I finished the inspection reports early
specifically so neither of you would have an excuse to stay late at the
record-room instead."

I don't have a ready argument against that particular claim, mostly
because I suspect, examining it, that it might even be partially true —
Tomas has grown considerably more attentive, these past weeks, to
exactly which small logistical mercies might give the two of us more
unsupervised time together, without ever once commenting on it directly
until he's decided the moment has properly arrived to take his credit.

"Go to bed, Tomas," I say, without any real heat in it.

"Going," he says, gathering his reports with theatrical slowness. "I
simply wanted it on the record that I noticed, and that I approve, and
that I intend to keep noticing, loudly, until one of you actually
confirms what the whole of Highstone has already worked out for
itself."

He goes, finally, still entirely too pleased with himself, and Aldric
and I are left alone in the cottage's quiet, and I find myself,
watching Tomas's retreating back, considerably more grateful than
irritated for a nineteen-year-old who has appointed himself, without
being asked, the whole of Highstone's unofficial keeper of our
particular happiness.

·

"Can I ask you something," Aldric says, once the cottage has properly
settled into its evening quiet, "about the cairn field. Not what was
said. How it felt, saying it."

I consider the question properly, because it deserves more than a
reflexive answer.

"Frightening," I say. "Considerably more frightening than setting a
ward at a failing cairn, which I recognize is a strange comparison, but
it's the truest one available to me. A cairn's cost is at least
predictable, within a margin. This wasn't."

"What frightened you specifically?"

"That you'd say no," I admit. "Not because I doubted what I felt. Because
I've spent six years believing that wanting something plainly, out
loud, is the surest way to be told you can't have it. I'd rather never
ask than ask and be refused. I've built my whole adult life around that
particular avoidance."

"And now you've asked anyway."

"And now I've asked anyway," I agree, "twice, actually, since the
cairn field — once there, and again tonight, in a considerably smaller
way, simply by letting Tomas tease us both without immediately
deflecting it into something safer."

Aldric takes my hand, turning it over in his, tracing the specific
calluses six years of ward-work have left across my palm.

"I think that's rather the bravest thing I've watched you do," he
says, "braver than Hollow Farm, braver than anything you've spent
yourself on quietly. Asking plainly, and letting the answer actually
matter to you, in front of witnesses."

"I don't feel brave," I say. "I feel considerably more exposed than
brave usually feels, in the stories anyway."

"Perhaps that's what actual bravery generally feels like," Aldric
says, "rather than the tidy version the stories prefer," and I find,
hearing it, that I believe him entirely, and we sit together a while
longer in the cottage's low firelight, both of us considerably more
exposed, and considerably less afraid of the exposure, than either of
us was a season ago.

·

Word of the cairn field conversation reaches Sela before I've had a
proper chance to tell her myself, which does not surprise me in the
slightest, given how efficiently news apparently travels the short
distance between Highstone and the orchard.

"You asked her properly," Sela says, the next time I see her, not
quite a question, though there's a warmth in it that tells me she
already knows the answer.

"I did."

"And?"

"And she said yes," I say, "and asked me the same question back, and I
said yes to that too, and I don't think either of us has fully worked
out yet what happens next, beyond simply choosing to keep choosing each
other, one day at a time."

Sela considers this, nodding slowly.

"That's rather all any of us actually manage," she says, "if we're
honest about it. I don't think anyone knows what happens next, not
truly. I think the ones who do well are simply the ones willing to
keep asking the question, the way you two apparently have started
doing."

"When did you get so wise about relationships," I ask, "for someone
who's spent our whole adult lives claiming she has no interest in one
herself?"

Sela's expression does something complicated at that, a flicker of
something she's clearly not ready to discuss, and I recognize it,
filing it away the same careful way she filed away my own reticence for
years, understanding that some doors open on their own schedule, and
that I owe her the same patience she's always extended to me.

·

I tell Aldric about the exchange that same evening, curious what he'll
make of his sister's sudden, complicated flicker of expression.

"She gets that look sometimes," he says, "when relationships come up.
I've never pressed her on it. I assumed, for a long time, that she
simply preferred her own company, the way some people genuinely do.
I'm starting to wonder if it's something else."

"I didn't press either," I say. "I don't think it's mine to press. But
I noticed it, and I wanted you to know I noticed, in case it's
something you'd rather ask her about yourself, properly, the way
you've been practicing asking things properly lately."

"I might," Aldric says, considering it. "Though I suspect she'll tell
me in her own time, the way she's always done things, rather than being
asked into an answer before she's ready to give it."

"That's probably wise," I agree. "I've learned, this whole winter, that
asking properly doesn't always mean asking immediately. Sometimes it
means simply making it known that you're willing to hear it, whenever
the person's actually ready to say it."

"You've become considerably wiser about this than either of us
expected, six weeks ago," Aldric says, with the particular warmth that's
become, these past days, one of my favorite things about him.

"I've had an excellent teacher," I say, "in exactly the opposite
direction — watching what happens when things go unasked for years at
a time, and deciding, deliberately, not to repeat the pattern."

We sit together a while longer, turning over the small mystery of
Sela's own guarded heart, both of us agreeing, without needing to say
it plainly, that whatever she's carrying deserves the same patient,
unhurried care either of us would want extended to our own guarded
places.

·

Tomas arrives the following morning considerably more subdued than his
usual self, and I understand, watching him settle at the record-room
table, that something's weighing on him beyond the ordinary business
of reports.

"You're quiet," I observe.

"I've been thinking about what Sela said," he admits, "about wanting
something worth building a life alongside, rather than settling for
something that merely fills the space. I don't think I'd ever
articulated that distinction to myself before she said it."

"Does it worry you?"

"A little," Tomas says. "I'm nineteen. I don't know yet what I actually
want, in that particular direction. I think I'd been assuming, before
this winter, that I simply hadn't gotten around to wanting anything yet.
Watching the two of you, and now hearing Sela's own history, I'm
starting to wonder if I've been avoiding the question rather than
merely not yet arriving at it."

"You don't have to have an answer at nineteen," I say. "I certainly
didn't."

"I know," Tomas says. "I think I simply wanted to say the uncertainty
out loud, rather than let it sit unexamined the way apparently everyone
in this family used to let their own uncertainties sit."

"That's a wise instinct," I say, "regardless of when you eventually
find the answer."

We sit together a while longer over the morning's reports, and I find
myself, watching Tomas work through his own quiet uncertainty with more
honesty than most adults manage, grateful for a young man determined
to practice this whole family's hard-won lesson properly, from the
very start, rather than waiting years to unlearn the alternative.

·

Aldric arrives partway through the morning, having heard some version
of the conversation secondhand from Tomas himself on the walk over, and
settles beside me once Tomas has gone to deliver the day's inspection
notes.

"He's been thinking about the future rather a lot lately," Aldric
observes.

"I noticed," I say. "I think this whole winter's forced everyone in
this family to examine questions they'd otherwise have comfortably
postponed for years."

"Is that a bad thing," Aldric asks, "forcing the examination early?"

"I don't think so," I say, considering it properly. "I think the
questions were always going to need asking eventually. I'd rather
everyone ask them now, honestly, while there's still time to actually
live differently because of the answers, than wait until circumstance
forces the asking the way it forced ours."

"That's a fair point," Aldric agrees, "though I confess I wouldn't
have chosen quite this particular crisis as the occasion for everyone's
collective self-examination."

"Neither would I," I say, "though I find I'm grateful for the
examination regardless, whatever occasioned it," and we sit together a
while longer, both of us turning over the whole shape of a winter that
has, in its own difficult way, handed this entire small family
considerably more clarity than any of them arrived with.

·

Sela stops by later that same day, curious about Tomas's own quiet
uncertainty, having apparently heard some account of it from him
directly.

"He asked me, plainly, how I knew what I actually wanted," she tells
me. "I didn't have a tidy answer for him. I told him the truth instead
— that I'm still working it out myself, most days, and that not
knowing at nineteen, or even at my own age, isn't a failing so much as
simply the honest state of things."

"That's a generous answer," I say.

"It's the only honest one I had," Sela says. "I think he appreciated
the honesty more than he'd have appreciated a comforting fiction."

"I think you're right," I say, and we sit together a while longer,
both of us grateful for a young man wise enough to ask the question
plainly, and for a family finally practiced enough to answer him
honestly rather than with whatever reassurance felt easiest.

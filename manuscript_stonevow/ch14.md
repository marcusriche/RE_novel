## Chapter Fourteen — Grafting Season (Aldric)

I take her back up to the orchard a second time, a few days after the
cairn field, in considerably less official capacity than the first
visit — no cairn to inspect this time, no grain record to review,
simply an afternoon neither of us has anywhere more urgent to spend.

I name every tree by the season I grafted it, a private calendar
nobody else has ever bothered to properly learn, and walk Isla through
the whole of it the way a man shows someone his actual handwriting for
the first time, rather than the careful, legible version he uses for
anyone he hasn't decided yet to trust.

"That one's a hard frost graft," I say, of a stunted, twisted specimen
near the eastern wall. "Season before last. I grafted it badly — too
cold, the wax set wrong, the join never quite took the way it should
have. My father would have cut it down. I kept it anyway."

"Why?"

"Because a mistake you own outright is worth more than a straight line
you never earned," I say, which is a thing my father used to say about
considerably smaller mistakes than the ones I've made since, though I
have found it holds up regardless of scale.

We reach the tree near the northern wall, the one I mentioned once
before without fully explaining, and I find myself, walking up to it,
considerably less composed than I expected to be.

"That one stopped fruiting the week the line moved," I say. "Two
winters back. The frost came further south than it had any right to,
that particular season, and took the whole northern row with it,
except this one. It survived. It just stopped bearing anything. I kept
it anyway. Didn't seem right, cutting down a thing for failing at
something the cold did to it rather than something it did to itself."

Isla looks at the tree for a long moment, and I understand, watching
her, that she has worked out exactly which tree I actually mean.

"You're not talking about the orchard," she says, quietly.

"No," I admit. "Not entirely."

"You kept yourself standing," she says, "after the line took something
from you that wasn't your fault to lose. And you've been calling it a
tree ever since, so nobody has to look at it directly and ask what it
actually cost you."

I don't have a ready answer for being so precisely understood, so I
simply stand beside her, looking at a crooked, barren tree I have never
once, in two winters, properly grieved in front of another living soul.

"You'd have liked the orchard whole," I say, eventually, mostly to give
us both somewhere else to put the moment.

"I'd have liked you whichever way I met you," Isla says, and I feel
that land on me harder than I expected a simple sentence to manage —
harder, in its own way, than the ward-cost the bond hands me every time
I stand too close to a working stone.

I put myself between her and the wind without noticing I've done it,
the same reflex that put my own name forward at the rite instead of
Sela's, and Isla, watching me do it, doesn't comment, though I catch
her filing the observation away, carefully, the way she files
everything she's learning about me that I haven't said outright.

"You do that constantly," she says, eventually. "Put yourself between
other people and whatever's coming. I don't think you've noticed how
often."

"It's not something I decide to do," I say. "It's simply where my body
goes, given the choice."

"I know," Isla says. "That's rather what worries me about it."

We walk the rest of the orchard slowly, in the particular unhurried
quiet of two people who have run out of urgent business and found,
underneath the urgency, something considerably more worth the time —
an afternoon spent simply being known, a little more thoroughly than
either of us has ever let anyone manage before, by someone who seems,
against every reasonable expectation either of us walked into this
binding with, inclined to keep the knowing carefully rather than spend
it carelessly.

·

We find Sela in the grain-house on our way back down, counting the
season's stored apples with the particular focused patience of a woman
who has done this exact count every winter of her adult life and has
never once trusted anyone else's tally over her own.

"You two look considerably more comfortable together than you did a
fortnight ago," she observes, not looking up from her count.

"We've had a productive afternoon," I say.

"I'm sure you have," Sela says, with the same dry precision I've come
to recognize as a family trait, shared between her and Aldric both,
though Aldric's version comes out considerably more guarded than hers.
"He showed you the whole orchard, I take it. Including the crooked
tree."

"Including the crooked tree," I confirm.

Sela finally looks up, something shifting in her expression — not
quite surprise, closer to a specific recalibration, the look of a woman
revising an estimate she'd made and finding the actual figure larger
than expected.

"He doesn't show that tree to people," she says, to me, plainly. "I
don't think he's shown it to anyone since our father died. I certainly
haven't seen him take anyone up to it on purpose."

I glance at Aldric, who has gone slightly still at his sister's
observation, the specific stillness of a man caught having done
something he hadn't fully registered as significant until someone else
named it as such.

"I didn't realize," I say.

"I don't think he realized either," Sela says, "until just now, watching
your face when I mentioned it." She goes back to her count, apparently
satisfied with whatever conclusion she's drawn, though I catch her
smiling, slightly, at the apples, in a way that tells me the
conclusion is a favorable one.

We walk the rest of the way down from the orchard in a companionable
quiet, and I find myself turning Sela's observation over the whole
descent — the specific weight of having been shown something Aldric has
apparently kept from everyone else in his life, without either of us
fully realizing, until his own sister named it plainly, exactly how
much that showing actually meant.

·

Tomas has news waiting when we finally reach Highstone, delivered with
the specific breathless urgency of a young man who has been sitting on
something significant for the better part of an afternoon.

"A messenger came through from the eastern waystones," he says. "There's
a second village reported past the line, considerably further east
than Calder's Reach. Nobody's checked it in years — it wasn't on any
active record until the messenger happened to mention it in passing to
one of the market traders."

I feel the specific cold settle that any mention of the line's further
losses always brings, though I notice, this time, that Aldric's hand
finds mine almost immediately, a small, steadying anchor against the
particular dread of yet another confirmed loss.

"Do we know anything else about it," I ask.

"Only the name," Tomas says. "Fenhollow. And that it's been past the
line for at least a decade, possibly longer, judging by how casually
the messenger mentioned it, as though its loss were simply an
established fact rather than news."

I add the name to my own growing ledger of the line's confirmed losses,
a considerably longer list than I'd like it to be, and feel, writing
it down, the whole weight of exactly how much ground the Rime has
already claimed before I ever properly took up this post.

"We should go, eventually," I say, "and see what it holds. There may be
something worth learning from a village that's stood past the line long
enough for its own loss to be treated as simple fact rather than news."

"Eventually," Aldric agrees. "Not this week. I don't think either of us
has room, right now, for an expedition that far past the line, on top
of everything else the line's already asking of us."

"No," I agree, "not right now," though I feel, adding Fenhollow's name
to the ledger, the specific unease of a woman beginning to understand
that the line's losses run considerably deeper and further back than
even her own six years of careful record-keeping has properly
accounted for.

·

Tomas asks, later that same evening, whether he might take on the task
of properly cataloguing every lost settlement the crown's own records
mention, however briefly, on the theory that a complete list might
eventually prove useful, whatever shape "useful" eventually takes.

"That's considerably more work than your current duties require," I
say.

"I know," Tomas says. "I want to do it anyway. I've been thinking, since
Fenhollow came up, about how little this line's own history is actually
written down properly, in one place, rather than scattered across
whichever administrative note happened to mention a loss in passing. I
think someone should be keeping that record properly, the way you keep
the cairn ledger, and I'd like it to be me."

I look at him properly, understanding, watching his face, that this
isn't simply an apprentice looking for extra work to prove his
diligence. This is a young man beginning to understand, on his own
terms, exactly what kind of wardwright he wants eventually to become.

"Do it," I say. "Properly, at your own pace, alongside your other
duties. I think it's exactly the kind of record this line has been
missing, and I think you're precisely the person who should be the
one to build it."

Tomas's face does something bright and unguarded at the permission,
and he sets to the task that same evening, pulling old administrative
notes from the archive with the specific focused energy of a young man
who has just been handed something he genuinely wants to do, rather
than merely something he's been assigned.

·

Isla finds me still in the workshop, later that night, turning
Fenhollow's name over in my own head long after everyone else has gone
to bed.

"You're still awake," she observes.

"I keep thinking about the crooked tree," I admit, "and about Fenhollow,
and about how much of this line's own history has apparently just been
allowed to quietly disappear, unrecorded, the same way I very nearly
let my own hand disappear without telling anyone."

"That's not entirely the same thing."

"Isn't it though," I say. "A loss nobody names properly eventually just
becomes background. Ordinary. The kind of thing a messenger mentions
in passing to a market trader, rather than something anyone actually
grieves. I don't want that happening to anyone else, the way it very
nearly happened to my own hand."

Isla sits beside me, considering this properly, the way she considers
most things I bring to her that matter.

"I think that's precisely why Tomas wanting to build the record matters
so much," she says. "Not simply as an administrative exercise. As a
refusal to let anything else disappear quietly, the way Fenhollow
apparently has, the way your own hand very nearly did."

"I hadn't thought of it in those terms," I say, "but I think you're
right."

"I usually am, about grief specifically," Isla says, with a dry humor
that doesn't fully mask the truth underneath it. "It's rather my
particular specialty, this winter."

We sit together in the workshop's low lamplight a while longer,
neither of us in any hurry to end the evening, and I find myself,
watching her in the particular quiet of a house finally settling
toward sleep, considerably more grateful than I know how to properly
say for a woman who has taught me, in the space of a few short weeks,
that naming a loss plainly is its own kind of mercy, however painful
the naming turns out to be.

·

"Can I tell you something I haven't told anyone," I say, before either
of us moves toward bed, "about the crooked tree specifically. Not the
grief part. Something else."

"Go on."

"I used to think keeping it alive was a kind of penance," I say. "For
surviving the frost that took the rest of the row. I've started
wondering, lately, whether it was actually something closer to hope —
that I kept it because some part of me believed, even without evidence,
that a thing that survives can eventually bear fruit again, given
enough patient tending."

Isla considers this, turning it over with the particular gravity she
gives most things I bring her that matter.

"I think both can be true at once," she says. "Penance and hope aren't
always opposites. Sometimes the tending itself is the bridge between
them — you start out doing it because you feel you owe the thing your
attention, and somewhere along the way, without deciding to, you start
doing it because you actually believe it might still become something."

"When did you get so wise about trees," I ask, "for a woman who spends
her whole career reading stone instead."

"I've had a rather thorough education in stubborn, half-alive things
that refuse to give up entirely," Isla says, with a small, dry smile, "given the company I currently keep."

I laugh, properly, surprised by it, and we finally head toward bed
together, the workshop's lamp banked low behind us, both of us carrying
a little more hope into the night than either of us arrived at the
conversation with.

·

Sela mentions the crooked tree herself, unprompted, a few days later,
catching me at the grain-house before I've properly greeted her.

"I've been thinking about that tree," she says, "since you told Isla
about it. I wonder if I might have a look at it properly, this spring,
with you. I've done considerably more reading on frost-damaged grafts
than you'd expect, these past few winters."

"I didn't know that about you."

"There's a great deal you don't know about me yet," Sela says, with
the small, private smile that's become familiar these past weeks. "I
started reading about grafting properly after Father died, on the
theory that understanding his one genuine passion better might help me
understand him better too. I don't know that it worked, precisely. But
I learned a fair amount about reviving damaged trees in the process."

"I'd welcome your look at it," I say, surprised and moved by the offer.
"I think I've been too close to it, too long, to properly assess
whether it's actually salvageable or whether I've simply been keeping
a monument."

"Perhaps it's both," Sela says. "Perhaps that's alright too." She
considers the tree's location, visible from where we stand. "I'll come
by properly, once the thaw's far enough along to examine the wood
without freezing my hands off in the attempt."

"I'd like that," I say, and find myself, watching her walk back toward
the grain-house, considerably more hopeful about the spring ahead than
I've allowed myself to be in longer than I can properly place.

·

I tell Isla about Sela's offer that same evening, still turning over
the specific surprise of my sister's private study of grafting.

"I don't think I properly appreciated," I say, "how much she's quietly
carried of our father, all these years. Reading about his one genuine
passion, alone, without ever mentioning it to me."

"People carry their grief in whatever shape actually fits them," Isla
says, "not necessarily the shape anyone else would predict."

"I think you're right," I say. "I think I've spent considerably too
long assuming I knew the whole shape of my own sister, without
properly checking that assumption against who she's actually become."

"None of us know the whole shape of anyone," Isla says, gently, "not
even the people we've loved our whole lives. I think that's rather
one of the better lessons this winter's taught me — that there's
always more to learn, about anyone, if you're willing to keep asking."

"I'm willing," I say, and we sit together a while longer, both of us
grateful for a season that keeps handing this whole family new rooms
to discover in people they thought they already knew completely.

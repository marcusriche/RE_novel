## Chapter Twenty-Three — What He Hasn't Said (Isla)

I have started noticing the pattern rather than the incidents, the way
six years of reading failing stone has taught me to read failing
people — not
merely the cup that fell at dinner, but the accumulation around it: the
left hand kept in a pocket more evenings than not, a specific flexing
he does when he believes no one is watching, the careful way he has
started avoiding any task that requires fine, delicate use of that hand
at all, grafting wax measured out now with his right hand alone, a
detail I would never have caught if I hadn't spent six years training
myself to notice exactly this kind of small, deliberate compensation.

I have watched people manage a failing body before, over the years —
a handful of the crown's own aging inspectors, an old farmer near
Hollow Farm who kept working a bad knee until it finally gave out
entirely. I recognize the specific shape of the managing. I have never
before recognized it on someone I love this much.

"Show me," I say, one evening, finally, plainly, the same register I
used the night I asked him, at the cairn field, whether he actually
wanted this.

Aldric goes very still, in the specific way of a man who has been
caught at something he wasn't ready to be caught at, and for a long
moment I think he intends to manage the request the way he manages
most things — a partial admission, carefully bounded, offered to
satisfy the asking without actually answering it.

He doesn't. He holds out the hand instead, and peels back the glove,
and I see, for the first time, properly, what six years of a
practitioner's training should have let me guess considerably sooner
than tonight: two fingers gone entirely numb, colorless in a way that
has nothing to do with the evening's actual temperature, a third
finger clearly going the same way, the whole hand cold in a manner that
does not thaw, the way the rest of him thaws, simply by sitting near a
proper fire.

"It's not the whole of it," I say, quietly, because I have learned, by
now, to read the specific shape of a partial truth even when it's
offered as though it were complete.

"I know it isn't," Aldric says, and for once doesn't try to make the
admission smaller than it actually is, which costs him, visibly,
considerably more than the admission itself.

"How much further has it spread, since the cup."

"Some," he says. "I've stopped keeping precise count. I decided, a
while back, that counting wasn't going to change the number, only make
me more afraid of it."

I want, badly, to argue with that particular logic — I have spent my
own entire career believing that precise counting is the one reliable
defense against an arithmetic that would otherwise simply run away
unchecked — but I understand, looking at his face, that arguing would
simply teach him to hide the counting from me the way he's clearly been
hiding it from himself.

"I need you to let me keep the count, then," I say, "if you won't keep
it yourself. Not to frighten either of us further. So that when the
number reaches whatever threshold makes the mutual rite urgent rather
than merely theoretical, we'll actually know it's arrived, instead of
guessing."

"Agreed," Aldric says, and I understand, from the way he says it, that
this small concession — handing me the counting he's refused to do
himself — costs him something close to what the actual numbness costs,
a different kind of surrender, considerably harder for a man built the
way he's built.

Neither of us says the words HOW MUCH LONGER. They sit in the cottage
with us regardless, taking up exactly as much room as if we had spoken
them aloud, and I find myself, sitting across from him with his numb
hand still resting, uncovered, on the table between us, doing the
arithmetic anyway, silently, the way I do arithmetic on everything,
and arriving, this time, at an answer I am not yet prepared to say out
loud to either of us.

"Thank you," I say, instead, "for showing me. Even the part of it you'd
rather I hadn't seen."

"You'd have found it eventually," Aldric says. "You notice everything,
eventually. I'd rather you found it because I showed you than because
you caught me hiding it a second time."

"There won't be a second time," I say, and mean it as a promise to
both of us — that whatever this costs, going forward, it will be
counted plainly, between the two of us, rather than managed quietly by
either one alone.

·

I go to the healer at Highstone the following morning, a woman named
Cera Voss who has treated frostbite and cold-sickness on this line for
longer than I have been wardwright, and lay out the whole of what I've
seen in Aldric's hand, asking her the plain question I am not
otherwise equipped to answer.

"How fast does it usually spread," I ask, "once numbness sets in
properly. In your experience. Not the rite's theoretical accounting.
What you've actually watched happen to people."

Cera considers the question with the particular gravity of a woman who
has, I suspect, watched this exact progression more times than either
of us would like to count.

"It varies," she says, "depending on how deep the underlying cost runs.
If it's simple cold exposure, it can stabilize on its own, given proper
warmth and rest. If it's something feeding on the person continuously —
which is, I understand, precisely what you're describing — it doesn't
stabilize. It progresses, at whatever rate the underlying drain
demands, until either the drain stops or there's nothing left for it to
take."

"How long," I ask, "before someone in that condition—"

"I can't give you a precise number," Cera says, gently, understanding
exactly what I'm asking without my needing to finish the sentence. "I
can tell you that the progression you're describing, from a fortnight's
partial numbness to a whole hand failing entirely, suggests
considerably less time than a full season. Weeks, most likely. Possibly
fewer, if the underlying draw accelerates the way these things
sometimes do once they've properly taken hold."

I sit with that number — weeks, not a season — for a long moment,
feeling the whole shape of my own remaining time contract considerably
faster than I had let myself believe.

"Is there anything that slows it," I ask, "short of stopping the drain
entirely?"

"Warmth. Rest. Reducing whatever's actively feeding on him, as much as
you're able." Cera looks at me carefully. "You understand, Wardwright,
that reducing it isn't the same as stopping it. If the underlying cause
is what I suspect it is, based on what little you've told me, the only
genuine cure is removing the cause itself."

"I understand," I say, and thank her, and walk back to the cottage with
a considerably clearer, considerably more frightening timeline than I
had this morning, understanding that whatever the mutual rite is going
to require of Ossory's careful, deliberate consideration, we do not, in
fact, have the luxury of his usual pace to work within.

·

I do not tell Aldric the precise number Cera gave me, that evening,
though I tell him the general shape of it — weeks, not a season — and
watch him absorb the news with a steadiness that surprises me, given
everything else he's had to absorb these past days.

"I think I already knew," he says, quietly. "Not the precise number.
The general shape of it. I've felt the difference, this past week,
between the ordinary cold I've lived with my whole life and whatever
this is instead."

"Does knowing it plainly change anything, for you?"

"It makes the arithmetic considerably harder to avoid," Aldric admits.
"I think I've been telling myself, quietly, that there was more room
than there actually is. Hearing an actual number — even a rough one —
makes that harder to keep believing."

"I'm sorry," I say. "I wish I had better news to bring you."

"Don't be sorry for the truth," Aldric says. "I'd rather have this,
however frightening, than another comfortable guess. You've taught me
that much, at least, these past weeks — that the true thing, however
much it costs to hear, is worth more than the managed version."

We sit together with the weight of the timeline a while longer, neither
of us pretending the fear isn't considerable, both of us, at least,
sharing it properly rather than either one carrying it alone.

"What do we do with the time we have," Aldric asks, eventually.

"We spend it properly," I say. "Pressing Ossory, finishing the
translation, doing everything within our power to make the mutual rite
ready before the timeline runs out. And in between all of that, we
keep living, properly, rather than simply surviving toward whatever
comes next."

"I can do that," Aldric says, and I believe him, and we spend the rest
of that evening doing precisely that — living, properly, in the time
we have, rather than merely counting down the time we fear we don't.

·

We walk to the cairn field, that same evening, not for any inspection,
simply because it has become, these past weeks, the place we both
return to when a conversation needs the particular clarity the standing
stones seem to offer.

"I want to ask you something," Aldric says, once we've reached our
usual spot. "Not about the timeline. About after. If the mutual rite
holds, if we actually get the years the healer wouldn't quite promise
us — what do you want, for the two of us, once this crisis has
properly passed?"

I consider the question carefully, because it deserves more than a
reflexive answer, and because I have not, until this exact moment,
properly let myself imagine a future that isn't shaped entirely by the
current emergency.

"I want ordinary evenings," I say, finally. "Considerably more of them
than we've had so far. I want to watch Tomas become the wardwright I
already know he's going to be, and I want to be there, properly, for
whatever that eventually looks like. I want your hand in mine, however
marked it ends up being, for as many years as this line and the crown
between them are willing to grant us."

"That's not a small want."

"No," I agree. "I've spent six years being frightened of wanting
anything this size, in case wanting it invited the specific pain of
losing it. I've decided, this winter, that the fear isn't worth the
cost of never wanting anything at all."

"I want the same things," Aldric says, quietly, "and I want to be
around to actually build them with you, properly, rather than simply
hoping for them from a distance."

We stand together at the cairn field a while longer, the standing
stones holding their usual patient silence around us, both of us
choosing, deliberately, to want a future neither of us can yet
guarantee, rather than let fear talk either of us out of wanting it at
all.

·

Tomas finds the two of us still at the cairn field when the evening
properly closes in, having apparently come looking once neither of us
returned by the expected hour.

"I was starting to worry," he admits, slightly out of breath from the
walk.

"We're fine," I say. "Simply talking. About after — whatever after
turns out to look like."

"Can I ask what you decided," Tomas says, settling in beside us,
"or is it private?"

"It's not private from you," I say, and mean it, understanding, saying
it, exactly how thoroughly he's become part of this family's inner
accounting rather than merely its apprentice. "We decided we want
ordinary evenings. Considerably more of them. We decided we want to
watch you become the wardwright I already know you're going to be."

Tomas's face does something unguarded at that, the particular
vulnerable pleasure of a young man who still isn't fully used to being
included this plainly in the people he serves.

"I'd like that too," he says, quietly. "Ordinary evenings. I don't
think I properly understood, before this winter, how much I wanted
that for myself as well, rather than simply the crisis-to-crisis
existence I assumed being a wardwright required."

"It doesn't have to be crisis to crisis," I say. "I think that's rather
the whole lesson underneath everything this winter's taught me. The
line can be held properly, urgently when it needs to be, and gently,
ordinarily, the rest of the time. I don't think I understood that
balance was possible, myself, until fairly recently."

"I'm glad you're teaching me that too," Tomas says, "alongside the
cadence and the ledgers. I think it might turn out to be the more
important lesson."

We walk back to Highstone together, the three of us, in the particular
easy quiet of a found family that has, somewhere in this difficult
season, learned to want an ordinary future together rather than simply
survive toward whatever crisis comes next.

·

Sela catches us at the orchard gate on our way past, having apparently
been watching the road for our return, and falls into step beside
Tomas with an easy familiarity that tells me their own friendship has
grown considerably since the season began.

"You three look like you've settled something," she observes.

"We were talking about after," Tomas says. "What we want, once this is
all behind us."

"And?"

"Ordinary evenings," Tomas says, with a small, pleased smile. "Isla and
Aldric want them. I find I want them too, rather more than I expected
to, given how much of my apprenticeship has felt like one long
emergency."

"I could use a few ordinary evenings myself," Sela admits. "I don't
think I've had one in longer than I can properly place, between the
grain counts and the exemption paperwork and worrying about my brother's
various self-sacrificing habits."

"Then we'll have them together," I say. "All of us. Once the current
crisis actually resolves."

"I'll hold you to that," Sela says, "the whole lot of you," and the
four of us continue on toward Highstone together, already, in some
small private way, beginning to practice the ordinary future we've
just finished naming aloud.

·

Aldric lingers with me a moment at the cottage door that evening, once
Tomas and Sela have both gone their separate ways, watching the last
of the day's light fail over the cairn field in the distance.

"I keep thinking about what you said," he tells me, "about wanting
ordinary evenings, considerably more of them. I don't think I properly
understood, before tonight, how rare a want that actually is, for me.
I spent most of my adult life assuming ordinary was something other
people got to have, not something I was permitted to want for myself."

"You're permitted now," I say. "As many ordinary evenings as either of
us can manage to build."

"I'd like to start tonight," Aldric says, "if you're willing. Nothing
urgent. Simply an evening, properly ordinary, the two of us."

"I'd like that very much," I say, and we go inside together, into the
cottage's low firelight, choosing, deliberately, to spend the evening
on nothing more urgent than each other's company — a small, quiet
practice of the future we've just spent the whole walk home describing
aloud.

·

We spend the evening on nothing more consequential than a shared meal
and a slow, unhurried conversation about the small, ordinary things
neither of us usually has time to properly discuss — a favorite tree
from Aldric's own childhood, a half-remembered song from mine, the
particular satisfaction of a well-mended tool.

"This is nice," Aldric says, simply, some while into the evening.

"It is," I agree. "I don't think I've properly appreciated, before
tonight, how much practice ordinary conversation actually requires,
after six years of only ever discussing cairns and crises."

"I'll help you practice," Aldric says, "as often as you'll let me,"
and we spend the rest of the evening doing exactly that, two people
slowly, deliberately, learning the particular unhurried art of simply
enjoying each other's company.

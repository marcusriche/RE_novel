## Chapter Fourteen — Two Miles

She got two miles.

Two miles north up the approach road with the yellow copy of the delivery
receipt on the passenger seat, and then the bridge came up ahead of her —
the whole blue span of it lit from underneath, going over into another
country — and Mira Dalca put on her indicator, which she noticed herself
doing, on an empty road, at twenty past eight at night, and pulled onto
the shoulder under the approach and stopped.

She did not turn the engine off.

That was the first thing she noticed about herself, sitting there: that
her hands had done all of it correctly. Indicator, mirror, shoulder,
handbrake, flashers. Fourteen years of doing the correct thing with her
hands while the rest of her was somewhere else, and here it was again,
working perfectly, at the worst hour of her life.

The diesel idled. The bridge traffic went over the top of her, four
lanes of it, a sound like water.

She sat with both hands on the wheel and did the arithmetic.

She did it because it was the only thing she had ever been able to do
when a thing was too large to look at directly. You put it in a column.
You put what it costs on the left and what it buys on the right, and you
add it, and whatever the total says, that is the answer, and you drive.

*Cost of going back in:* one truck, one woman, four men, sixty yards of
gravel, and a road boss with a manifest signed at eleven minutes past
eight.

*What it buys:* nothing. It does not buy one thing. She had run it three
times in a diner lot and it had come out at nothing three times, and a
total that comes out the same three times is a true total.

*Cost of driving on:* eleven hours to Chicago. A licence hearing on the
nineteenth. A shed with a soft plank in it and a coffee tin under the
plank. Eleven people with apartments and jobs and children in schools.

*What it buys:* all of them. Every one. Alive and unlooked-for and never
knowing there had been a night when somebody added them up.

The column was not close. It was not even interesting. It was the
clearest arithmetic she had done in fourteen years and it said, in the
plainest possible terms, *drive*.

Mira Dalca sat on the shoulder of the bridge approach with the engine
running and looked at the total and said, out loud, in an empty cab:

"No."

·

The word surprised her.

She heard it in the cab the way you hear something a stranger says in the
next booth, and she sat absolutely still with her hands on the wheel and
waited to find out what she had meant by it.

What came was not an argument. It was a sentence she had been saying her
whole adult life, arriving with its meaning showing.

*I don't lose freight.*

She had said it to Benny in 2015 when the Portage yard got a camera. She
had said it to a mate on a boat in 2021 and to a frightened woman in a
container in Melvindale in 2019, and she had said it four days ago in a
truck stop outside Bay City to a man with no papers, and every single
time she had meant it as a promise, and she heard now, at twenty-four
minutes past eight on the seventeenth of May, what it actually was.

It was the whole architecture, in five words.

*Freight.* Because freight is a thing you are responsible for and not a
thing you know. Freight does not ask you how you are. Freight does not
sit in your passenger seat for seventeen days and put your mirrors back
exactly where they were. Freight goes in the back, and you route it, and
you deliver it, and then it is gone and you are still the one driving,
which is the entire point, which is the only way she had found in
fourteen years to be near a human being and survive it.

She had been calling people freight since she was nineteen.

She had been doing it because she had been freight. Because a person who
has been priced and boxed and moved has exactly two positions available
to them afterward, and only one of them has a wheel in it, and she had
taken that one with both hands at twenty-one and never once let go.

And it worked. That was the thing nobody ever said about it. It had
worked for fourteen years. Eleven people were alive because of it.

Mira sat with the engine running and thought, with total clarity: *and
tonight the method delivered a man to a yard to be killed, on time, in
good order, with a signature on the receipt.*

Because that is what the method is for. The method does not know the
difference between a border and a grave. It knows the road and the clock
and the terms, and it had done all three of them beautifully.

The bridge traffic went over.

She looked at the passenger seat, where there was a yellow carbon copy of
a delivery receipt and nothing else, and where for seventeen days there
had been a man sitting with his hands on his knees who could not stop
counting exits.

"You're not freight," she said.

·

She took the folded paper out of the inside pocket of her coat, over her
ribs, where it had been for a day and a half.

Ten digits in a driver's blocky capitals, and under them a name.

She looked at it for a long time and understood that she was not
hesitating about whether to call. She had stopped hesitating about that
somewhere in the last ninety seconds, in a way that had happened below
the level she could watch.

What she was doing was something else. She was standing at the edge of
the actual thing, the one she had spent fourteen years building a yard
and a business and a personality on top of so that she would never have
to stand at the edge of it, and she was working out how to make her
mouth do it.

Because a call to that number is not a call about Adam Marek.

You cannot report a homicide in progress at a border yard and stop there.
The first question is *who is speaking*. The second question is *how do
you know*. And the true answer to the second question is fourteen years
long and has eleven names in it and starts in a container off Constanța
in April 2012 with a nineteen-year-old girl in it who was priced at
eleven thousand dollars against future work.

Every time she had imagined telling anybody any part of it — and she had
imagined it, at four in the morning, more often than she would ever
admit — she had run into the same wall, and the wall was this: *if I say
the first sentence, I have to say all of them.*

She sat on a shoulder under a bridge with a phone in her hand.

And what she thought was not *I have to*. What she thought, in her own
voice, in Romanian, which was the language that the true things had
always happened in:

*I have spent fourteen years making sure that no one would ever be able
to say what I am. And they were never going to. And it did not save one
single person tonight.*

She looked at her watch. Eight-thirty.

The gate had shut at eight. He had got down out of the cab at about ten
past. Traversa had handed her the yellow copy at eleven minutes past and
told her it was dark out.

Twenty minutes. Nineteen minutes.

She had absolutely no idea how long a thing like that took, and she
understood with a lurch that this was itself a piece of information about
her life — that she had spent eleven years in the freight arm of an
organization that killed people and had arranged her entire existence so
as to never once have to know the schedule.

Mira Dalca dialed the ten digits at 8:31 p.m.

It rang twice.

"Duty line, Northern District of Illinois."

A woman's voice. Awake. Not young.

Mira found that her mouth was open and nothing was coming out of it, and
that her hand on the wheel had gone white, and she did the only thing she
had ever been able to do in that condition, which was to start with a
number.

"There is a man being killed right now at a container yard on the water
south of the Blue Water Bridge in Port Huron, Michigan," she said. "It's
eleven acres, one gate, off the approach road on the east side. He has
been there for thirty-one minutes."

A change in the quality of the line. The particular silence of somebody
picking up a pen.

"Ma'am, I need—"

"His name is Adam Marek," Mira said. "He is a federal witness with a
testimony date on the nineteenth in front of an AUSA named Priya
Raman, and he was taken out of a protective house in Berwyn on the
first of May, and the reason nobody has found him in seventeen
days is that he has been in the passenger seat of my truck."

The pen stopped.

"Who am I speaking to?"

And there it was, the whole of it, one question wide.

Mira Dalca sat on the shoulder of the bridge approach with the flashers
going and the diesel idling and a yellow carbon copy on the seat beside
her.

"My name is Mira Dalca," she said. "I own a yard on Torrence Avenue in
Chicago. I want to report a homicide that is happening right now, and I
want to tell you who I am, and I am going to start in 2012."

·

It took twenty-two minutes.

She would not remember most of them in order. What she would remember,
for the rest of her life, was the flashers ticking, and the underside of
the bridge, and the extraordinary discovery that once you begin at the
beginning, the rest is only work.

The duty officer put her through inside ninety seconds. The second voice
was a man who said his name was Weiss and asked her to say the location
again, twice, and read it back to her both times. The third voice was a
woman.

"This is Priya Raman."

Mira had been braced for a hurry. There was no hurry in it at all. There
was a woman on the end of a telephone at twenty to nine on a Sunday night
who sounded exactly like a person who has been sitting beside a phone for
eleven months waiting for one specific kind of call and has already
decided not to frighten it.

"Ms. Dalca. Weiss has the yard. There are people moving. Keep talking to
me."

"How long."

"Not yet. Keep talking to me."

So she talked.

She gave the yard first, because the yard was the only part that was
urgent: the fence lines and the gate and the scale house and the shed and
where the light poles were and which lamps were out, all of it off a
napkin she had drawn four hundred yards away in a diner, and she heard
Raman relaying it in pieces to somebody in the room with her, and heard
that somebody say, distinctly, *she's got the light poles*.

She gave the four men and where they stood.

She gave the truck, four lengths in, nose to the shed, and the slip on
the north side, and the sixty yards from the X to the water, and said,
"That's why it's that yard. That's the only reason it's that yard," and
Raman said, "Yes. We know," and did not say anything else about it, which
Mira was grateful for in a way she could not have explained.

And then the yard was given, and there was nothing left that was urgent,
and Priya Raman said:

"Now tell me the rest."

·

Here is the part Mira Dalca would think about for a long time afterward.

She had assumed — for fourteen years, without ever once examining it —
that the government wanted the eleven.

It was the load-bearing assumption of her whole life. It was why the tin
was under a plank. It was why she had never gone to a lawyer, never gone
to a priest, never in fourteen years said one true sentence to anybody
who could write it down. Because the eleven were the only thing she had
that was worth anything, and the moment she opened her mouth they would
be taken off her and spent.

And it turned out to be true. Raman asked for them at minute nine.

"You said eleven people."

"I did."

"I want to be straightforward with you, because you've been straightforward
with me." No pressure in it whatsoever, which was worse. "The eleven
passages are the corroboration. Your word about Traversa is one woman's
word. Eleven documented movements, with dates and yards and routes, in
your hand, is a pattern, and a pattern is a case. I'd want their names."

Mira looked at the bridge.

"No," she said.

"Ms. Dalca—"

"I want to say this properly, because I've only got one go at it." Her
voice was doing something strange. It was completely steady. "Those
eleven people did not agree to be moved. They agreed to *live*, and I
gave them a route, and that's not the same thing, and I have known it
isn't the same thing since about the third one. Every single one of them
got in the back of a vehicle because there was no other door. That's not
consent. That's arithmetic."

"I understand—"

"You don't yet, but you will in a second." She put her palm flat on the
wheel. "They didn't agree to be moved. And they have not agreed to be
evidence. And I have already done the first one to those people without
asking. I'm not doing the second one."

Silence on the line.

"Then what are you giving me?"

And Mira Dalca, on a shoulder, under a bridge, at eleven minutes to nine
in the evening, gave the only answer she had.

"The roads," she said. "Every route the transport arm has run out of
Chicago since 2015 — the lanes, the yards, the crossings, the four
unlisted ones, and who keeps which gate. And eleven years of Gino
Traversa's manifests, which are all in my yard, in my hand, because I've
been reconciling them for him since the year Moretti went to trial, and
there is four hundred and six thousand dollars of freight on them that
never went on the family's books.

"And me," she said.

"Meaning what, specifically."

"Meaning I'm the twelfth," Mira said. "April 2012, Constanța to Toronto
in a container and Toronto to Chicago in a truck, nineteen years old,
priced at eleven thousand dollars against future work by a man I never
met, and every single day since then I have paid it in road. That's not a
witness statement, that's a confession, and I'll sign it. I know exactly
what it does to my licence and my yard and I've done the column.

"I'm not giving you the people," she said. "I'm giving you the roads and
I'm giving you me. If that's not enough, hang up and I'll go back in
alone and you can read about it."

The flashers ticked.

"Ms. Dalca," Priya Raman said, "it's enough."

·

It was enough. It was also forty minutes from enough, which is a
different problem entirely.

"Talk to me about your team," Mira said, and heard her own voice go
completely flat and professional, which was a relief so enormous it was
almost physical.

"Port Huron is not Chicago." Raman did not soften it. "We have two
agents in Detroit who are moving now and a resident agency contact and
the state police, who are good, and who are also being asked at ten to
nine on a Sunday night to go through a locked commercial gate on the
word of a woman on a telephone. There is a process for that. The process
is not slow, but it is not nothing."

"Give me the number."

"Best case, a vehicle at that gate at nine thirty-six."

Mira Dalca looked at the dashboard clock and did the only arithmetic she
had ever been genuinely gifted at.

Eight fifty-two.

Forty-four minutes.

And a man who had got down out of a cab on his own feet at ten past
eight, thirty-eight minutes ago, in a yard with sixty yards between the
gravel and the water.

"That's too long," she said.

"I know it's too long."

"He doesn't have forty-four minutes. He didn't have twenty."

"Ms. Dalca." And here at last there was something in Priya Raman's voice
that was not calm. "I am not going to lie to you about this. If it has
already happened, it has already happened, and nothing you or I do in the
next forty minutes changes it. If it has not — and there is a real reason
it may not have, which is that a yard like that is a place where things
are done at a particular hour for particular reasons and eight o'clock is
early — then the only thing on this earth that matters is whether
somebody is still standing in that gravel at nine thirty-six."

"Forty minutes," Mira said.

"Forty minutes. Can you give us forty minutes?"

And Mira Dalca — who had spent fourteen years in the business of
producing exactly one commodity, which was time, in hostile yards, on
other people's clocks, for people who had nothing else — sat up straight
in her seat and felt something arrive in her that she recognized, with a
kind of horror, as *pleasure*.

"Yes," she said.

"How?"

"By going back in and arguing about a bill."

"Ms. Dalca, I am obliged to tell you—"

"Tell me while I'm driving," Mira said, and put it in gear.

·

·

Priya Raman talked to her for the whole two miles, which Mira understood
perfectly well was a technique and did not mind in the least.

"I'm obliged to tell you that we are not asking you to re-enter that
property and that we are advising you against it, and I'd like that on
the recording."

"It's on the recording."

"And now I'd like to say the other thing." A change in the line — a door,
a room with more people in it. "Ms. Dalca, do you have any training of
any kind?"

"I've got fourteen years of yards."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the only answer I've got and it's a better one than you think,"
Mira said. She had the phone against her shoulder and both hands on the
wheel. "I have stood at scale houses in four states and produced time out
of nothing in front of men whose entire job was not giving me any, and I
have never once been wrong about what a yard was going to do next, and
tonight I have four pages of true arithmetic in my coat about a man who
has never in forty years walked away from a wrong number."

"Give me the shape of it."

"He'll let me in because I'll give him a reason that flatters him, and
he'll listen because I'm going to be right about a figure he can check.
That's it. That's the whole plan, and it's got about forty minutes in
it, which is exactly what you asked for."

"And when the forty minutes are up?"

"Then I'll be standing in a scale house with a man who's worked out he's
been held," Mira said, "and I'd very much like your people to be at that
gate."

There was a pause with a room behind it.

"What do you need from us?"

"Two things. One, nobody comes down that approach road with a light bar
going until they're inside four hundred yards, because that yard's got
one gate and a slip on the east side and sixty yards of gravel in
between, and a man who sees a light bar at a mile has time to make a
decision.

"Two." She checked the mirror out of pure habit. "There's a container
office on the north side with the light on. That's where he is. The
first thing through that gate goes to the office and not to the scale
house, because I'm the one in the scale house and I'll keep. He won't."

Another pause.

"Ms. Dalca," Raman said, "how long have you been doing this?"

"Doing what?"

"Producing time for other people."

Mira Dalca drove down an approach road toward a gate.

"Since I was twenty-three," she said.

·

She did not go in fast.

That was the whole thing. Everything she knew, all fourteen years of it,
came down to the difference between arriving and *arriving*, and she took
the two miles back down the approach road at thirty-one miles an hour
with the radio off, because a truck that comes down a yard road at speed
is an emergency and an emergency gets a decision made about it at the
gate.

She thought about Adam at the light on the way over, saying *it's the
last flat surface*.

She thought about the fact that she had not answered him.

She put it away — not gently, not with any sort of peace about it, just
put it in the place where she put things she would have to carry later —
because the gate was coming up and there were four men on the other side
of it and she needed all of herself.

Two hundred yards out she turned the headlights on full.

At a hundred she put the window all the way down, and the cold came in
off the river, and she could smell the water and the diesel and the iron
smell that container yards have everywhere in the world.

At fifty she put both hands up on top of the wheel where they could be
seen from anywhere in the yard, and drove the last of it that way, with
her wrists in the light and her face lit up by her own dash.

That is how you enter a yard you are not welcome in. Slow, lit, hands
showing, window down. She had learned it at twenty-three from a man in
Melvindale who had learned it in Beirut, and she had used it four times
in her life and had never once expected to use it in a place where she
had signed a receipt fifty minutes earlier.

The gate was shut.

A man came out from the scale-house side, and it was the same one, the
pleasant one, and he had his hand somewhere she chose not to look at, and
she stopped the truck eleven feet short of the fence line and let the
engine idle and did not open the door.

"Ms. Dalca," he said. "You've made a wrong turn."

"Tell Gino the manifest is wrong."

She said it in the voice she used at scale houses, the one that has no
performance in it at all, the one that says *I am tired and you are tired
and there is a form to fix*.

"He's signed his receipt," she said. "He's got the white copy and I've
got the yellow and I'm telling you that piece of paper does not discharge
anything, because the price on it was set in 2012 against future work,
and nobody in eleven years has ever run the interest on it. I've run it.
It's in my coat. It's four pages.

"Now, I can turn around and drive to Chicago," Mira said, "and this
becomes a thing that gets discussed by people above him about why a
delivery he took receipt of on a Sunday night came apart over an
accounting error. Or you can walk over there and tell him, in these
words: *she says the arithmetic's wrong and she's got it itemized*."

The man stood in the road with his hand where it was.

"He'll want to know why you didn't say it inside."

"Because I hadn't finished adding it up," said Mira Dalca, "and I don't
bring a number to that man until it's right."

Something happened in the man's face that she catalogued and would think
about later, which was that he believed her instantly, and that the
reason he believed her instantly was that it was the single most
Traversa-shaped sentence available in the English language, and he had
worked for Gino Traversa for a long time.

He walked away toward the scale house with a phone at his ear.

Mira sat with the engine running and the window down and her hands on top
of the wheel and looked, past the fence, past the shed, at the long low
container office on the far side of the yard where there was a light on.

*Be alive,* she thought. *Be alive for forty minutes.*

The gate rolled open at eight fifty-six.

She drove in with her lights on and her hands showing, and the gate
rolled shut behind the truck for the second time that night, and this
time she had counted the doors on the way in.

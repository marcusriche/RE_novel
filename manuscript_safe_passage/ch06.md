## Chapter Six — What She Carries

The second yard was a shuttered marine hardware lot on the Saginaw Bay
side, four acres of cracked concrete behind a building with its windows
whited out, and the manifest said seventeen hundred hours on the seventh
day.

They came down the approach at half past four with the bay flat and
pewter on the left, and Mira saw it from a mile out and understood the
whole thing before she was within four hundred yards of the gate.

Three vehicles in the lot.

The manifest said one contact. One man, one vehicle, forty minutes, and
the same non-transaction as Portage: hand in a page, get looked at, get
phoned about.

There were three cars, and a man standing at the gate who was not
standing the way a yard man stands, and a second one visible at the
corner of the building with his hands in his jacket pockets in
sixty-degree weather.

She did not slow down.

She went past the exit at sixty-two miles an hour with her eyes forward
and her hands at ten and two, and she counted eleven seconds, and then
another eleven, and then she was around the curve and there was nothing
in the mirror at all.

Neither of them said anything for nine minutes.

She did not want to know what he was doing with his hands or his face and
did not look, and drove, and the nine minutes were the longest of the
whole first week.

What went through her head in them was a single sequence, repeated, the
way a machine repeats when the input does not change.

*One contact, forty minutes, hand in a page.* That was the manifest. That
was the shape of Portage and it was the shape of every yard on the sheet,
and it had been true at Portage twice, and the truth of it twice was the
reason she had come down that approach road at half past four with the
bay on her left and nothing in her chest at all.

Three cars.

The arithmetic was not difficult. Three cars is two more than one, and
two more than one is not a change of plan, it is a *plan*, because two
extra vehicles do not assemble themselves in a shuttered lot on the
Saginaw Bay side at four in the afternoon by accident.

She thought about the fact that she would have driven in.

That was the part she kept coming back to, at sixty-two miles an hour on
a shore road: that if she had come down that approach on the second day
instead of the seventh, before the walk and the water and the eleven
dollars and the man who counted her mirror checks, she would have taken
the gate at eight miles an hour and gone and stood at a scale house
window with her manifest page in her hand.

"That was the yard," Adam said, finally.

"That was the yard."

"How many?"

"Three cars. Two men outside." She let out a breath. "The sheet says one
man."

"So the sheet's for you and the yard's for something else."

"The sheet is a *receipt*," Mira said. "It's not for me. It's so that
afterward there's a piece of paper that says the Dalca woman came through
on time and everything was normal." Her jaw was tight. "I've been reading
it wrong for a week. It's not a route. It's a chain of custody."

Adam Marek looked out at the bay.

He had worked that out on the second day.

He said, "Yeah," and let her have it, because there is a way of already
knowing a thing that helps nobody, and because the only useful gift he
had left was letting her arrive at things in her own truck at her own
speed.

Benny called at eight that night, on the burner, and this time he called
her, which he had never done.

"Two things," he said. "You'll want to sit down for the second one."

"I'm sitting. I'm always sitting. I'm a driver."

"That's true." A pause. "First: they came back. Same two. Yesterday
afternoon, and this time they had a name. Yours. They said they were
following up on the lane survey with the *owner* this time, and could
they speak to Ms. Dalca, and I said she's out on a run, and they asked
which lane."

"What did you say?"

"I said I don't discuss client lanes with insurance consultants who won't
produce a certificate of authority, and then I asked the taller one what
his surname was, and he told me, Mira, he actually told me, and I wrote
it down and I put it in the safe. I don't know if it's real. But he told
me, which means he's twenty-eight years old and somebody sent him out
without telling him what he was doing."

Mira stood at the fence of a rest area on M-25 with her back to the wind.

"And the second thing."

"The state opened a review on the licence yesterday at four."

The wind came across the lot.

"That's not — Benny, the renewal packet's not due for ninety days—"

"It's not the renewal. It's a review. It's a different animal and it
comes off a complaint." His voice did not change at all, which was how
she knew. "It's a Chicago complaint about a 2019 filing discrepancy, and
I've had the file up on the screen for four hours, and Mira, there *is* a
discrepancy in a 2019 filing. It's a transposed digit in a trailer
registration. It's been there for seven years. Nobody on earth was ever
going to find it."

"How long does a review take?"

"Eleven days," Benny Osei said, "give or take. And they can suspend
pending, and if they suspend pending, every truck in that yard stops
rolling that day."

She closed her eyes.

Three cars in a lot on the bay. A twenty-eight-year-old with a clipboard
who did not know what he was for. A transposed digit from 2019 that had
sat in a database for seven years waiting to be an instrument.

"He's going to call me," she said.

"He's going to call you," Benny agreed.

"Benny. If it goes to suspension—"

"Then I'll be a mechanic with a very tidy licence file and no yard to
apply it to, and I'll be fifty-eight, and I'll be fine." He said it
absolutely evenly. "That is not the thing to be doing arithmetic about
tonight, and you're doing arithmetic about it, and I can hear you doing
it. Stop."

"Benny—"

"Twelve's got a noise," Benny said. "I'll call you Thursday," and hung
up.

·

Adam found the compartment at six the next morning, in a motel lot in
Standish, changing a tire.

It was not a dramatic discovery. The inside rear on the driver's side had
picked up something on the haul road and had been going down for two days
at a rate that only became a problem overnight, and he had said *I can do
that* at half past five in the way of a man who has been sitting in a
seat for seven days and would pay money to lift something heavy.

She had let him.

That was, in retrospect, the whole event: she had let him.

He got the truck up and the wheel off, and while he was in there he
noticed the plate.

The false floor was not amateur work. That was the first thing he
registered, professionally, with a kind of unwilling admiration: it was
built into the structure of the box behind the bulkhead, framed properly,
with the plate sitting flush in a rebate so that a hand run along the
floor would find nothing. It was not a smuggler's box, the kind he had
seen fifty of, cut in with a grinder and screwed back badly. Somebody had
built this with a plan and a square.

The catch was under the lip. He found it with two fingers because he had
spent eleven years finding things with two fingers, and the plate came up
on a piano hinge without a sound, and he looked in.

It was long enough for an adult to lie flat and about eleven inches deep.

There was a vent — not a hole; a proper louvered vent, ducted, so that
what came in was air and not exhaust.

There was a bottle holder, screwed to the side wall, of the kind that
goes in a boat.

And there was a light.

A little battery reading light, the kind you clip to a book, mounted to
the underside of the plate on a bracket somebody had made by hand out of
strap steel and painted with a brush.

Adam Marek knelt on the concrete of a motel parking lot in Standish,
Michigan, at four minutes past six in the morning, and looked at a
hand-made bracket holding a reading light in the roof of a hiding place,
and his legs stopped working properly.

Because the vent was survival. The bottle holder was survival. Any
competent criminal builds those, because a body that suffocates is a
problem and a body that dehydrates is a problem.

But nobody in the history of the trade had ever built a *reading light*.

Somebody had lain in that box, in the dark, with a woman driving, and had
gotten out at the other end, and the woman had gone home and thought
about the dark.

He knelt there for a while.

What he was thinking about, and what he would tell exactly one person in
his life, three years later, was a fuel stop north of Sarnia in November
2019 and a knock in three.

Because he had stood at the back of a trailer that night and run every
option a man can run, and not one of the options he ran had been *is
there air in there.* It had not occurred to him. It had genuinely never
crossed his mind, not that night and not once in the seven years since,
and he had spent four hours a week for six months describing that night
to a federal prosecutor and had never said the word air.

Somebody had built a duct.

Somebody had sat in a shed with a piece of strap steel and a hacksaw and
bent a bracket, and had painted it with a brush because a bare bracket
rusts, and had screwed a two-dollar clip-on reading light to the inside
of the roof of a hole in the floor of a truck.

He put the plate back down.

He heard it click into the rebate, and he ran his hand over the floor to
be sure it was flush, and then he finished the tire, and torqued the nuts
in the right sequence, and dropped the truck off the jack, and put the
tools away in the order they went in the box.

Mira came out of the motel office at twenty past six with two coffees.

"Done?"

"Done. It'd picked up a chunk of something on the haul road. Inside
rear's your spare now, so you'll want a real one before the border."

"There's a place in Bay City."

"That'll do."

She handed him a coffee. He took it.

And Adam Marek said nothing at all about the reading light — not then,
not at the fuel stop, not at eleven o'clock when they crossed the bridge,
not at two in the afternoon when she pulled over to listen to the
alternator, not once for four days.

Mira knew he had found it. She had known within about ninety seconds of
coming out of the office, because the tool box was closed properly and
because a man who has been under a truck for forty minutes without
finding a false floor comes out different than a man who has found one.

She did not mention it either.

They drove north up the Huron shore with the water on their right and a
thing sitting in the cab between them that had a shape and a weight and
no name, and it was, she thought — and it was, he thought — the most
intimate exchange either of them had had with another human being in
about a decade.

·

They drove north up the Huron shore with the water on their right and a
thing sitting in the cab between them that had a shape and a weight and
no name.

It changed how they used the truck. That was the part neither of them
would have been able to describe, and both of them noticed.

He began handing her things. Not saying anything about it — a coffee at
the exact moment she came back to the cab, the atlas open at the right
page before she asked for it, a sandwich in four pieces on a napkin on
the console at the point in the afternoon when she was going to forget to
eat.

She began giving him the route.

Not the destination — she had been giving him the destination since
Chicago, because a passenger who does not know the destination is a
passenger who panics at a turn. The *route.* The legs, the alternates,
the reasons. By Tawas he knew four of her fallbacks and the shape of a
fifth, and could have driven the next two hundred miles without her, and
neither of them said one word about what that meant.

At about four in the afternoon, north of Oscoda, he said, "There's a
thing I'd like to say and I'd like to say it badly and get it over with."

"Say it badly."

"Whoever built that floor thought about the dark," Adam said.

Mira Dalca kept both hands on the wheel and her eyes on the road and did
not answer for about a mile and a half.

"It's a two-dollar light," she said.

"Yes."

"It's a clip-on light from a hardware store in Hegewisch and a bracket I
made out of strap because I didn't have anything else, and it took about
forty minutes, and I painted it because bare strap rusts."

"Yes," Adam said.

"So don't."

"I'm not going to," he said. "I've said it. That's the whole of it and I
won't say it again."

And he did not — not that afternoon, not at the fuel stop, not for four
more days — and Mira Dalca drove ninety miles with her jaw set and her
eyes hot and could not have told anybody why, and it was, she would
decide much later, the exact hour in which the whole thing became
unmanageable.

·

The men were waiting at the Tawas yard, which was not on the manifest.

That was the part that took the floor out from under her. Not the two of
them standing by a car in a gravel lot at nine in the morning; men stand
by cars. It was that she had chosen the Tawas yard herself, an hour
earlier, off a list in her own head, because she needed a scale and a
place to swap the spare and because it was a yard she had used exactly
twice in seven years and had told nobody about.

Somebody in her own supply chain had been bought.

"Stay in the cab," she said. "Whatever happens. Both hands where they can
be seen and don't get out."

"Mira—"

"Both hands. I'm serious. If they see you move you become a decision."

She got down.

They were pleasant. That was the thing about Traversa's people that she
had never been able to explain to anybody who had not met one: they were
*pleasant*, they were mannerly, one of them held a hand out and she shook
it because not shaking it was an event.

"Mira Dalca."

"That's right."

"Gino says hello." The older one — fifty, heavy through the shoulders, a
good coat — reached into his jacket, unhurried, so she could watch him do
it, and brought out a photograph and held it out at arm's length.

It was a print, on paper, from a phone. Benny Osei on a Chicago sidewalk
in his good jacket, walking up the steps of a building with a folder
under his arm, taken from across the street that morning.

She looked at it for four seconds.

"That's a licensing office," the man said. "That was seven this morning.
He's a very punctual gentleman."

"I know what it is."

"Gino says you're eleven hours behind the manifest." He put the
photograph back in his jacket. "He says he's a patient man and he's got a
lot of respect for a driver who runs her own roads. He also says the
seventeenth is the seventeenth, and he'd hate for anybody to have a bad
month over eleven hours."

He said it the way you'd tell somebody about a weather front.

Never a threat. Always a forecast. It was the entire dialect and it had
been designed over sixty years for exactly this: to be repeated, later,
by a frightened person, to a lawyer, and to evaporate on contact.

"Tell Gino I'm on the seventeenth," Mira said.

"I'll tell him."

"Tell him the road went to gravel for a hundred miles and it cost me a
tire, and if he wants to run it faster he's welcome to come and drive
it."

The heavy man smiled with real appreciation.

"That's the exact thing he said you'd say," he told her, and they got in
the car and went.

·

She stood in the middle of the lot for a while after they left, with the
gravel dust settling.

Nine mirror checks a mile was the steady number. She got in and pulled
out onto the road and did not need Adam Marek to tell her that it had
gone to sixteen, because she could feel her own neck doing it, and
because a person who has spent fourteen years managing her own fear knows
exactly which instrument is reading high.

She thought about who it could have been.

That was the actual problem and it was worse than the men. The Tawas yard
was on nobody's list. It was a scale and a shop run by a man named
Kowalczyk's cousin — no relation to her Kowalczyk, a coincidence she had
found funny in 2019 — and she had used it twice, in seven years, and had
never written it down anywhere, and had chosen it that morning at
half past five out of her own head while a man slept beside her.

So: four possibilities.

One, the yard itself, which meant a man she had met twice had been bought
for a sum somebody thought was worth spending on a maybe.

Two, the truck, which meant a device, which meant they had been on the
box since Chicago and the whole business of the Malibu and the leapfrog
had been theatre for her benefit.

Three, Benny, which was not a possibility and which she made herself
consider for eleven full seconds anyway, because a person who will not
consider a thing is a person who can be surprised by it.

Four, and this was the one that had teeth: that they had not known at
all, and had put men at four yards on the Huron shore that morning
because there were only four, and that she had walked into a net that had
been cast wide and not a hook that had been aimed.

Four was the likeliest. Four was also the worst, because a hook can be
avoided and a net cannot, and because it meant that the family was
prepared to spend eight men on a Tuesday to keep a woman on a schedule,
and nobody spends eight men on a delivery.


Then she got back in the truck, and Adam Marek said, in a voice that was
absolutely flat:

"What was the photograph?"

"How do you know it was a photograph?"

"Because he held it out at arm's length so you could see it and not touch
it, and because you looked at it for four seconds and didn't take it."
His hands were still on his knees where she had told him to keep them.
"Who is it?"

Mira put both hands on the wheel and did not start the engine.

"His name is Benny Osei," she said. "He's fifty-eight. He's been my
dispatcher and my mechanic for eleven years and my licensee for eleven
years, which means my entire company is legal because of a piece of paper
with his name on it, and there's a review open on it in Springfield since
Wednesday, and they photographed him walking into the office this
morning."

"So they're not threatening you."

"No."

"They're threatening the only person in the world you'd stop for."

"Yes," Mira said.

Adam Marek looked out through the windscreen at a gravel yard on Lake
Huron.

He had spent seven days building an entire architecture out of not
saying things. Not saying that he knew what the walk was. Not saying that
the yards were a chain of custody. Not saying that he had found the
reading light, or that a woman had told him she came out of Constanța in
April of 2012 and he had spent four days doing arithmetic he could not
stop.

And sitting in that lot he understood, with total clarity, that the
architecture was finished. That there was a thing he was going to have to
say out loud, and that it was going to cost him whatever this was, and
that he was going to say it anyway — because the alternative was to keep
riding in the passenger seat of a truck belonging to a woman who was
being crushed, and to let her be crushed without the one piece of
information he had.

"How far to the next fuel," he said.

"Sixty-one miles."

"Do it in sixty-one," Adam said. "Because there's something I've got to
tell you, and I want to be stopped when I do it."

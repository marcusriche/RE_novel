# SAFE PASSAGE

### The Moretti Family, Book Two

**Sable Voss**

---

*For everyone still paying off a road they never chose.*

---

**Content notes:** organized crime and gun violence · explicit sexual
content · coercion and threats by third parties · human smuggling and
exploitation · an off-page death.

---

I have moved people the family wanted gone and people the family never
knew existed, and I have never once asked which was which. That is the
price of the passage they bought me at nineteen, and this week they came
to collect it.

One man. One route. Nineteen days to the border, and orders not to
deviate.

Except the man in my passenger seat is a federal witness with a date in
court, the route runs through three yards where nobody expects him to
arrive breathing, and somewhere between the lake and the line I stopped
counting him as cargo.

I can deliver him or I can keep him.

There is no manifest for both.

## Chapter One — Wisniewski Cartage

The fog came up off the Calumet at half past three and by four it had
taken the whole yard, so that the sodium lights stood in it like things
burning underwater.

Mira Dalca walked the fence line first. She always walked the fence line
first. Eleven acres of cracked asphalt and gravel on Torrence Avenue with
the river on one side and a rail spur on the other, forty-one trailers,
nine tractors, a shed, a shop, and a chain-link perimeter that any
determined child could get through in under a minute — and she walked it
every morning anyway, at four, in whatever the lake was doing, because
the walking was not about the fence.

Two gates. The main one on Torrence, and the service gate at the north
corner that opened onto a service road that opened onto the frontage that
opened onto the ramp. Two ways in. Two ways out. She counted them the way
other people crossed themselves.

The shop light was already on.

"You're late," Benny Osei said, without turning around.

"It's four."

"You're here at three-fifty. It's four." He had a wrench in a bell
housing and half his forearm somewhere it shouldn't have been. Fifty-eight
years old, hands like tool leather, a Ghanaian mother and a Manchester
childhood and thirty years of Chicago on top, and a voice that had never
once in Mira's experience gone above conversational. "Twelve's got a
noise."

"Twelve had a noise in March."

"Twelve has a *different* noise. In March it was a bearing. Now it's a
lie." He came out from under the truck. "Kowalczyk's out at five with the
Hammond load. Ricky's out at five-thirty. And there's a call on the board
from a man who wants two pallets moved from a storage unit in Blue Island
to a storage unit in Harvey, and I want you to look at the note I wrote
under it."

She looked. Benny's handwriting was small and square and entirely
readable, which was more than anyone could say for hers.

*Nope,* it said.

"What's wrong with it?"

"Everything about it. He asked what our insurance covers before he asked
what it costs." Benny wiped his hands. "Nobody asks that first, Mira.
Nobody in the history of freight has ever asked that first."

"So we don't take it."

"So we don't take it." He picked up his coffee. "Which you knew, which
is why you're standing there letting me say it out loud, which you also
know I know."

She let that go, because letting Benny Osei's observations go was the
only method she had ever found for surviving them.

The office was a fourteen-by-twenty box off the shop with a window onto
the yard and a door onto the lot, and she stood in the doorway for the
three seconds it took her to do the thing she had done in every room she
had entered since she was nineteen years old. Two doors. One window,
painted shut in 2011, which she had unstuck herself with a screwdriver in
2012 and had checked every month since. The desk between her and both
exits, which was wrong, but the desk had been there since 1979 and moving
it would have looked like something.

Then she sat down.

The sign outside said WISNIEWSKI CARTAGE, in white on green, in a
typeface nobody had used since the Nixon administration.

Ted Wisniewski had been dead ten years. He had sold her the yard in
2015 for eighty percent of what it was worth and a handshake, and had
died the following spring, and had told her twice while the paperwork was
being drawn that she should put her own name on the board out front.
*It's your place now, sweetheart. Put your name on it.*

She never had.

She told people it was because a sign costs four thousand dollars and a
repaint costs goodwill — half her customers had been Ted's customers, and
half of those were the kind of men who did not enjoy discovering that
things had changed. It was a good reason. It was even true.

The actual reason was that a name on a sign is a name on a sign, and
Mira Dalca had spent fourteen years in a country that had never once been
sure it wanted her in it, and there was a species of person who read
boards, and she had no intention of putting anything up there for them to
read.

At four-forty the fog began to thin over the river and the trucks started
going out.

She loved this hour. She would not have said so aloud to anyone alive,
including Benny, but she stood at the window with a coffee she had let go
cold, in a building she owned, watching her own trucks pull out through
her own gate onto a public road, going to places she had told them to go,
and there was a feeling that came with it that she had no name for in
either of her languages.

Kowalczyk flashed his lights at the gate. She raised two fingers. He was
sixty-one and hated her, roughly, in the manner of a man who would have
died for her, and he had worked for Ted for nineteen years before he
worked for her.

At six, Benny brought the licence file in and put it on the desk.

"Renewal packet came."

"Already?"

"It's the ninetieth day out. They send it at ninety." He tapped it with
one finger. "You want to fill it out this year?"

"You fill it out. It's your licence."

"It is my licence," Benny agreed.

It was. It had been his licence since 2015, because the property broker's
authority for the yard required a licensee with a clean history and a
social security number that went back further than 2012, and Mira Dalca's
history was not a thing you put in front of the state of Illinois, and
had not been for a long time. So the yard was legal because Benny Osei
was, and the trucks rolled under his authority, and the whole eleven
acres of it, every trailer and every contract and every dollar she had
made in a decade, rested on a piece of paper with another man's name on
it in a filing cabinet in Springfield.

She had asked him once, in 2016, whether he understood what he was
holding.

*I understand exactly what I'm holding,* Benny had said. *That's why I'm
the one holding it.*

They had never discussed it again.

"Fill it out," she said. "Same as last year."

"Same as last year," Benny said, and did not move, and after a moment
added, "You could file your own now, you know. Six years, no priors, the
yard's clean on paper, the money's clean, the whole thing's clean. I've
been saying it since—"

"Benny."

"—2020, and I'll say it in 2027, and one of these years you'll hear it."
He picked up the file. "Twelve's still got a noise."

The dispatch shed sat apart from the office, a twelve-by-twelve wooden
box on blocks at the north end of the yard by the service gate, from
which Ted Wisniewski had run this operation for thirty-two years with a
CB radio and a coffee can full of pencils. It had a plywood floor with a
soft plank at the north-east corner that Mira had never repaired.

At twenty past six she went out to the shed to pull the week's board, and
she was alone, and the fog was going, and before she pulled the board she
knelt down and put two fingers under the soft plank and lifted it.

The coffee tin was where the coffee tin was.

She did not take it out. She did not open it. She looked at it for as
long as it takes to breathe twice, which was as much as she permitted
herself, once a week, in the same way that other people check that a door
is locked after they have already locked it.

Eleven names, folded twice, in her own hand.

Eleven dates, eleven places, and a column on the right that ran from 2019
to last October with an entry in it that had almost certainly cost a man
in Melvindale his life and had definitely not cost the woman he had been
sent to collect hers.

*Burn it,* Benny said, every time it came up. Which was twice a year.

She had a set of answers for that, and she had used all of them, and none
of them was the reason.

The reason was that at four o'clock in the morning on the sixth of
February 2019 — she could give the date, which told her something about
herself she preferred not to examine — she had been kneeling on the
plywood floor of this shed with a pen and a scrap of paper, writing down
a Kosovar woman's first initial and the age of her son, and had realized
that if she did not write it down there would be no evidence anywhere in
the universe that it had occurred.

Not evidence for a court. She had never once meant it that way and had
told herself she did for six years.

Evidence for *her*. Because a woman who is moved from one country to
another in a box at nineteen learns, in a way that does not come out
again, that the things which happen to a person who is not on any list
did not happen. There is no record. There is no receipt. There is a woman
who was in Constanța and then was in Chicago, and nothing in between
except a number.

So she had bought a pen and started a page.

Eleven lines in seven years, and every one of them written in this shed,
at four in the morning, on the floor, with the plank up.


She put the plank back and stood and pulled the week's board, and went
back across the yard through the last of the fog with her coat collar up,
and by seven the day was a day.

It was a good day, as days went. That was the part she would think about
afterward and would never quite be able to make peace with: that the last
ordinary morning of her life had been a good one.

A broker in Bedford Park who had been trying to get a rate out of her
since February gave up and took hers. Ricky called in from the Hammond
run with a hose fitting weeping and she talked him through wrapping it
with tape and a jubilee clip over the phone, badly, both of them
laughing, and he made the delivery. Benny found the noise in twelve — it
was a heat shield, and he was wrong about that, and it would take another
five months and a different man to establish it — and shouted about it
across the yard in the way of a man who has been proved right about
something at seven in the morning.

She did the payroll at half past eight. Fourteen people. She did it by
hand because the software had been wrong in 2021 and she had never
forgiven it, and it took forty minutes, and she wrote the figures in a
column and added them twice and got the same total both times.

That was the thing. That was the whole of the life, and she had built it
with her own hands out of nothing, and it was worth about eleven acres
and forty-one trailers and fourteen people's mortgages, and it stood
entirely on top of two pieces of paper: a licence in Springfield with
Benny Osei's name on it, and a debt in a man's head with hers.

Neither of which she owned.

She had known that for eleven years, in the way you know a thing about
your own roof.


At nine-forty a black Chevrolet Suburban came in through the main gate at
walking pace and stopped in the middle of her lot, and Mira, watching it
through the office window with a pen in her hand, understood in the
length of time it took the vehicle to travel eleven feet that the eleven
years were over.

·

The house in Berwyn had a cribbage board on the kitchen table, and that
was the thing Adam Marek would think about afterward, at odd hours, for
the rest of his life.

Six months. A brick two-flat on a street of brick two-flats, a rented
first floor with somebody else's grandmother's furniture in it, and a
rotating pair of deputy marshals of whom two were fine, one was a bore,
and one — a woman named Halvorsen who had grandchildren and a
weightlifting habit and no patience at all for self-pity — had taught him
cribbage in November and had beaten him one hundred and eleven times
before he took his first game off her in March.

He had a bag packed. He had had a bag packed since the second week, not
because anyone told him to but because a man who has spent eleven years
around transport does not sit in a room without a bag packed.

He had counted the exits on the first day: front door, back door through
the kitchen, and a basement stair to a window well that a man his size
could get through with about ninety seconds and a broken collarbone. So:
two. Both covered.

The date was the nineteenth of May. It had been the nineteenth of
May since January, when the trial had been severed and the transport
counts had been set for their own calendar, and Adam had built the entire
architecture of six months around a single number the way a man in a cell
builds it around a release.

Everything he had to say fit in about four hours. He had said it three
times already, in proffer, to a woman named Raman with reading glasses
pushed up in her hair, and each time it had taken longer, because each
time she had asked the same questions in a different order and found
something new in him.

*You drove the Constanța line.*

*I ran the Constanța line. Montreal yard, containers to Toronto, trucks
down to Chicago. Four years.*

*How many people.*

*I don't know.*

*Guess.*

*I'm not going to guess. I'll tell you the runs and the dates and the
plates and you can count them. I'm not going to sit here and make up a
number about human beings.*

She had liked that, he thought. Or she had noted it. It was hard to tell
with Raman, which was one of the things he had come to respect about her.

On the first of May, a Friday, at four minutes past nine in the
evening, the doorbell rang.

Halvorsen's shift had ended at eight. The change-of-post window at the
Berwyn house ran a hard eleven minutes because of the drive from the
lot on Ogden — Adam had timed it in his first month, out of habit, the
way you time a gate — and the doorbell rang at 9:04, which was minute
four.

He knew before he opened it.

He opened it anyway, because the alternative was a door being taken off
its hinges in a residential street where the neighbours had children.

Two men. Neither of them large. The one in front had a jacket zipped to
the throat and the specific flat courtesy of a man who has done a thing
often enough to find it dull.

"Adam," he said. "It's a ride."

"Whose ride?"

"Gino's."

And Adam Marek stood in the doorway of a rented house with a cribbage
board on the table behind him and did the arithmetic in about a second
and a half.

Two exits, both covered. A street with children on it. A body — his — that
had lost eighteen pounds in six months of not moving and was forty-one
years old and had never in its life been good at this part. And a date on
the nineteenth of May that was the only thing he had ever made that
was not freight.

If he fought here, he lost here, on a residential street, with the sort
of noise that gets a neighbour hurt.

If he went, he was in a vehicle, and vehicles were the one subject on
earth about which he knew more than almost anyone, and vehicles took
time, and time was thirteen days.

"Let me get my coat," Adam said.

"Get your coat."

He got his coat. He did not get the bag, because taking the bag would
have told them he had one packed, which would have told them something
about how he had spent six months, and a man gives away nothing he does
not have to. He left the cribbage board out on the table with the pegs in
it from Thursday, because Halvorsen would come back on Monday and would
see it and would know, at minimum, that he had not walked out on his own
feet.

They put him in the back of a panel van with a bench welded along the
wall, and one of them sat with him, and it was, he registered with a kind
of professional disgust, a Ford Transit with about a hundred and ninety
thousand miles on it and a differential that was going to grenade before
autumn.

"Where are we going."

"Out," the man said. "You're getting helped out. Before they can make you
talk."

Adam looked at him.

It was not a good lie. It was not even a well-constructed one — nobody
extracts a federal witness on the eve of his own testimony as a *favour*
— but it was the shape of a story they intended him to repeat if he got
the chance to repeat it, which told him that somewhere in the next
nineteen days there was a leg of this that required him to have gone
willingly.

He did not correct it.

He said, "Okay," and sat where he could see out the back windows, which
were painted over, and closed his eyes, and started the only work
available to him, which was counting.

Turns. Stops. The change in road noise from asphalt to concrete to the
long ribbed hum of the Skyway. Ninety minutes. South and east and then
south again, and the smell coming into the van at the end of it — river
water and diesel and the specific iron-and-rot of a working yard on the
Calumet — told him within a mile where he was before the doors opened.

They put him in a truck bay to wait.

Concrete, a floor drain, a roll door, a work light on a stand. A place
where things got loaded. He sat on an upturned crate and looked at the
drain and thought, with total clarity, that he had spent eleven years
delivering people into rooms exactly like this one, and had never once
sat in one, and that there was an argument that the arrangement was
overdue.

At five in the morning a man came and told him to stand up.

At twenty to six, in fog, they walked him into a yard he did not
recognize — and then, three steps in, did.

·

Gino Traversa did not get out of the car.

He never had. Not in eleven years. Not the day he first came to this lot
in 2015 to explain what Ted Wisniewski's sale meant and did not mean, and
not the nine or ten times since. He rolled the window down and he
conducted his business at the height of a seated man, and the person he
was conducting it with stood in the cold, and the arrangement was so
consistent that Mira had come to think of it as a doctrine rather than a
habit.

She had asked Benny about it once.

*He's fifty-five and he's been shot twice,* Benny had said. *Rooms have
corners.*

*So does a car.*

*A car has a door he's already holding,* Benny had said, and that had
been the end of that.

She went out to the lot with her coat on. Behind the Suburban's glass the
morning made her a shape she did not recognize.

The window came down.

"Mira."

"Gino."

He looked well, which annoyed her. Fifty-five, in a grey topcoat, with
the healthy colour of a man who had spent the winter somewhere else and
the settled bulk of a man who had never in his life had to hurry. Two
years ago Gino Traversa had been the fourth or fifth most important man
in an organization that ran half the freight on the south side and a
number of other things besides. Then a bookkeeper had put a green ledger
in front of a federal prosecutor and the whole architecture had come down
in a single winter, and Salvatore Moretti was seventy-nine and in a
federal facility in Terre Haute, and the road boss was what was left.

Everybody knew that. It had been in the paper for a year. The woman who'd
done it had testified under her own name — Mira had read every word of
that coverage twice, standing up, in this office, with a feeling she had
never satisfactorily named.

"You're busy," Traversa said.

"It's Monday."

"It's Monday." He nodded pleasantly at the yard. "You've done well here.
I say that to people. I say, there's a woman on Torrence with eleven
acres and forty trailers and not one thing wrong with her paperwork. It's
a compliment."

"Thank you."

"It's also an observation."

There it was. Nine seconds in.

A second man had got out of the passenger side and gone around and was
now standing in the doorway of her office — *in* it, one foot on the sill,
in a way that was not blocking and was absolutely blocking — with a
manila folder in his hand. He was perhaps thirty. He had the folder held
in both hands like a hymnal.

"I want to talk about the passage," Traversa said.

And there it was.

Fourteen years. Nineteen years old and a container out of Constanța and a
truck out of Toronto and a man in a Chicago yard who had given her a
mattress in a room over a shop and a job wiping down trailers, and a
figure — eleven thousand dollars — that had been named to her once, in
that first week, in a language she barely had, by a person she never saw
again.

Nobody had ever mentioned it since. Not once. Not in fourteen years. That
was the elegance of it: an unmentioned debt is not a debt, it is a
climate. It had been the air she had built a business inside of.

"There's no passage, Gino. That was 2012."

"There's a ledger," Traversa said, mildly, "and I've got a *hell* of a
sense of humour about ledgers this year."

He let that sit.

"Eleven thousand dollars, priced against future work. You've done the
work. Nobody's saying you haven't — you've moved what we asked, when we
asked, and you've never once asked what it was, and Sal used to say that
about you, before." A small movement of the hand: *before*. "But the
principal was never against work. The principal was a passage. And a
passage gets discharged by a passage."

"Say the thing you came to say."

"One man." Traversa's eyes had not moved from her face. "One route.
Nineteen days. Three yards, and it's all in the manifest, and there's not
one thing in it you haven't driven before. You take him to the border and
you hand him to the people at the last yard and you drive home and you
never hear from me again as long as you live."

The man in her doorway held out the folder.

She did not take it.

"Who is he?"

"A man who wants to be somewhere else."

"Who is he, Gino."

"Somebody who used to work for us," Traversa said, "and who is going to
be a great deal of trouble to a number of people if he stays in this
country, and who has come around to the view that he'd rather not be
here." He shifted, a man settling into an upholstered seat. "You've moved
forty people for us. This is forty-one. What's the question?"

The question, she did not say, is that in fourteen years you have never
once come to this lot for a passage, because passages come through the
produce channel and the produce channel is a phone call, and you are
sitting in my yard at nine-forty in the morning with a second man in my
doorway and a folder with four pages in it.

"And if I say no."

For the first time Gino Traversa looked away from her, out through his
windscreen at eleven acres of asphalt with her trucks on it.

"Mira," he said, "you were carried."

The fog had gone off the river. Somewhere behind her a compressor kicked
on in the shop, and the sound of it was the most ordinary sound in the
world.

"This is the carry back," he said. "That's all this is. Everybody who
gets carried carries. It's the whole business. Nobody gets to be the only
one who was ever helped."

She took the folder.

Four pages. A route sheet, two pages of yard contacts with times, and a
fuel and mileage allowance schedule that she registered, at a glance, as
being unusually detailed for a job that was supposedly a favour to a
friend.

"Seventeenth," Traversa said. "Port Huron. Eight in the evening. He's
handed at the yard, you sign the receipt, you come home. Anything before
that is your business — you're the driver, you know the roads, I've never
told you how to drive. But you don't deviate from the yards, and you
don't put him on anything with a schedule, and he doesn't touch a phone.
Those aren't mine. Those are the people at the other end."

"And the debt's finished."

"Discharged on delivery," Traversa said, "and I'll say it in front of
anybody you like, which is more than I'd do for most people."

She looked at the manifest.

"Where is he?"

Traversa raised two fingers off the wheel, and the man in her doorway
turned and made a small gesture toward the shop, and two more men came out
of the fog at the north end of her yard with a third between them, and
walked him across her lot.

He was tall. He had a jacket that did not fit him any more and a face
that had been indoors for a long time, and he came across the asphalt at
his own pace, not theirs, in a way that suggested nobody had needed to
push him and everybody had thought about it.

Eleven feet from her he stopped, and looked around, and did not look at
her at all.

He looked at the shop's roll door. Then the service gate at the north
corner. Then the office, and the office's second door on the far side,
and the frontage, and the fence line, and back to the main gate — in
order, the way you read a page.

Counting the exits.

Mira Dalca stood in her own yard with a four-page manifest in her hand
and watched a stranger perform, in eleven seconds, in front of two of
Gino Traversa's men and God, the exact private ritual she had performed
in every room she had entered since she was nineteen years old.

Then he stopped, and found her, and something went over his face that she
would spend a long time deciding about — she thought at the time it was
fear, and it was, and it was also, underneath, the specific arrested look
of a man who has walked into a place he has been before.

"This is Marek," Traversa said, from the car. "He's quiet. You'll like
him."

The window went up.

The Suburban turned in her lot in a wide unhurried circle and went out
through her gate, and the two men went with it in a second car, and then
there was nothing in the yard but the fog burning off and forty-one
trailers and a compressor cycling in the shop.

"Ma'am," the tall man said.

"Don't."

"Alright."

"Can you drive?"

"Yes," he said. "But I'd rather you did."

She looked at him for a moment.

"Get in the truck," she said.


## Chapter Two — Terms of Delivery

The truck was a 2016 Freightliner day cab with a twenty-eight-foot box, a
Cummins with four hundred and ten thousand on it, and a passenger seat
that Mira Dalca had not had occupied in six years.

She ran the rules at the Torrence gate, because she ran the rules at the
gate, always, before the wheels turned, in the same order, in the same
words. She had said them in three languages and to forty people and she
had never once changed them, and the not-changing was the point: a person
who is frightened remembers a rhythm when they cannot remember a sentence.

"No phone. If you have one, it goes in the box now and I don't care whose
it is."

"I don't have one."

"Then that part's easy. No names at fuel stops — not mine, not yours, not
anybody's. If somebody talks to you, you're my brother-in-law and you're
riding down to help me unload. You don't have to be good at it. You just
have to be boring."

"I can be boring."

"You sleep when I say. Not because I'm the boss of your sleeping, because
I'm going to be driving for eleven hours and I want you awake for the
part where I'm not." She looked at him for the first time since the
gate. "You eat what I hand you. If you need something, you say it before
we're rolling, not forty miles in."

"Understood."

"And you don't get out unless I open the door."

He was quiet for a second. In the mirror the yard was going small behind
them, the sign with its green field and white letters, Benny at the shop
door with a rag in his hand, watching them out.

"Say that one again," Adam Marek said.

"You don't get out unless I open the door."

"Okay." He put his hands on his knees. "I want you to know that I heard
it. That's all."

She pulled onto Torrence and headed for the Skyway, and did not answer,
because there was no answer, because the rule was the rule and it had
never once in six years occurred to her that it was worth remarking upon.

·

The manifest was four pages and she had read it three times before the
wheels turned, standing at the front bumper in the dark with a torch in
her teeth, and she read it a fourth time at the first red light on
Torrence with it flat against the wheel.

It was a good document. That was the first thing wrong with it.

Nobody in the transport arm produced four clean typed pages for a
one-vehicle move. What she had had from them for eleven years was a phone
call, or half a page in somebody's block capitals, or a fuel card and a
yard name and a time — because paper is a witness, and the whole business
is built on not producing witnesses.

Four pages meant somebody had decided this run was going to be
*documentable*. It meant that if anybody ever came asking, there would be
a folder, and in the folder would be a legitimate carrier, licensed in
Illinois, with eleven years of clean interstate operating history and a
signature at the bottom of page four.

Her signature.

She had noticed that at four in the morning, in the yard, with Traversa's
second standing in her doorway, and had signed it anyway, because there
was no version of the morning in which she did not sign it, and because
noticing a thing and being able to do something about it are two entirely
separate departments.

Page one was the route. Three yards, nineteen days, arrival at the fourth
on the seventeenth of May, eight p.m., and a line at the bottom that said
NO DEVIATION in the same typeface as everything else, which was somehow
worse than capitals.

Page two was the fuel and the allowances.

Page three was the schedule.

Page four was where she signed, and where a man whose name was given only
as *the passenger* was described in four lines: height, approximate
weight, no medical requirements, no personal effects.

*No personal effects.* She had turned that over at the wheel for eleven
miles. A man being moved out of a country carries something. A bag, a
coat with a lining, a photograph, papers he should not have. That is the
first thing a carrier deals with and it is on every job, and it has never
once in fourteen years said *none*.

Which meant either that they had taken everything off him, or that they
had never expected him to need any of it.

·

Benny had stood at the shop door with a rag in his hand and had not come
over, which was itself a message.

He had come over at four, when the car was still in the lot with its
window down, and had said, in a low voice, standing where he could be
seen from the car and heard by nobody in it: "You don't have to do this."

"Benny."

"I'm aware that's not true. I'd like it in the record that I said it."

"Noted."

"And I'd like something else in the record." He had wiped his hands on
the rag without there being anything on them. "You'll come back through
that gate on the twentieth and you will not tell me anything about the
nineteen days in between, and I will not ask, and we'll both go back to
being extremely good at pretending that's a normal way for two people to
work. And I want you to know that I know it's not, and that I've known
for eleven years, and that I do it because you need it and not because
I'm a fool."

Mira had looked at a sixty-year-old sign in the fog.

"Twelve's got a noise," she had said.

"It has got a noise. It has got a noise since March." Benny Osei had put
the rag in his pocket. "Go on, then."

·

He asked how long, somewhere around Gary.

She gave him a distance. It was automatic; she did not think about it at
all until she heard herself do it.

"Three hundred and forty road miles to the first yard," she said. "It's a
four-hour drive. I'm not going to do it in four hours."

"How long, then?"

"Eleven. Maybe twelve." She checked the mirror. "We'll stop twice for
fuel and once for nothing, and I'll take the frontage road east of
Michigan City instead of the interstate, and if the wind stays like this
I'll want a break at Sawyer because that stretch beats up the box."

Adam looked out at the flat grey water going past on their left, the
steel mills stacked up along it like a country's worth of iron laid on
its side.

"That wasn't an answer to my question," he said. Not unkindly.

"It was the accurate one."

"It was a distance."

"Distance is what I've got." She said it flatly, and it came out harder
than she meant it, and she let it stand rather than repair it. "You asked
how long. I could say *a while*, which means nothing, or I could say
*eleven hours*, which is a guess, or I could tell you the actual shape of
the day. You want to know how long. I told you what's between here and
there. That's the same information with the guessing taken out."

He thought about that for a mile.

"Yeah," he said. "Alright. That's fair."

·

She did not want to know anything about him.

This was not indifference. It was method, and it was the oldest and most
carefully maintained piece of equipment she owned, and she had assembled
it in the first year and had never once had cause to revise it.

You do not learn the passenger.

You learn the *route*. You learn where the county line runs and which
scale house is manned at what hour and which of the three ways over a
river has a camera on it. You learn the weight and the fuel and the
distance. And you keep the person in the seat beside you at exactly the
distance at which they remain a logistics problem, because a logistics
problem can be solved and a person cannot, and because — this was the
part she would not have said aloud to anyone alive — if you learn them,
and then something happens to them, you have to carry that too, and she
had capacity for a great many things but not for that.

Eleven people. Eleven. She could recite the names, and did, once a week,
under a plank in a shed, and she could not have told you the face of a
single one of them, and that was on purpose, and it had cost her
something she had made a decision not to price.

So: cargo. Not cruelty. Method.

She had been doing it for eleven miles when he said, "You keep checking
the mirror."

"That's the job."

"Nine times in the last mile," Adam said. "I counted."

Mira's hands did not move on the wheel.

"Why," she said, "would you count that."

"Habit." He was still looking out the window. "You can't do anything with
a number until you've got one."

·

They came off the interstate at Michigan City and ran the frontage road
east in the last of the light, which added nineteen minutes and took them
past two truck stops she did not use and one she did.

She fueled at the one she did.

He was, as it turned out, genuinely good at being boring.

She had expected the opposite. In her experience the frightened ones
overperform — they make eye contact, they are helpful, they say good
evening to a cashier with the specific warmth of a man who wants to be
remembered as harmless, and being remembered as anything at all is the
one thing you cannot afford. She had lost a route in 2018 because a
twenty-year-old from Kandahar had helped a woman pick up a dropped
purse.

Adam Marek got down out of the cab when she told him to, went inside when
she told him to, bought a coffee and a packet of crackers with the ten
dollars she handed him, stood at the end of the counter looking at
nothing with the particular slumped patience of every driver in North
America, and got back in.

He did not look at the cameras. He did not avoid looking at the cameras.

"You've done this," Mira said, pulling out.

"I've watched four hundred men do it," Adam said. "From the other side.
I was the one in the yard office deciding whether the driver looked
wrong." He drank some of the coffee. "It's not hard. You just have to be
tired, and I am tired."

She took the ramp.

"The trick everybody gets wrong," he said, after a while, "is that they
think the job is to look innocent. It isn't. Innocent is a *performance*
and performances get watched. The job is to look like a man who's forty
minutes behind on a run that doesn't pay enough."

Mira Dalca drove for a mile and did not say the thing she was thinking,
which was that she had spent eleven years teaching exactly that sentence
to frightened people in the dark and had never once heard it said back to
her.

·

The first yard was in Portage, Indiana, in the flat industrial sprawl
under the Burns Harbor stacks — nine acres of gravel with a chain-link
fence, a scale house, a portable office on blocks, and forty containers
stacked three high in rows that had not been re-inventoried since the
Obama administration.

They got there at twenty past seven in the evening. The manifest said the
yard contact would be available between seven and nine, and it also said,
in a footnote so specific that Mira had read it four times at a fuel stop
in Michigan City, that the truck was to be inside the fence for the
duration of the wait.

"Forty minutes," she said, killing the engine. "Maybe more. Stay in the
cab."

"What's the stop for?"

"Nothing. That's the point. There's no cargo here, there's no paperwork
here, and there's nobody here who needs to see us." She swung down. "It's
a check. Somebody looks at us and calls somebody. Every yard on this
route is going to be that. It's how they know I'm doing what I said."

She stopped with her hand on the door.

"That's the first time I've told you something I didn't have to," she
said. "Don't make a thing of it."

The scale house had a light on. She went and stood at the window and
handed in the manifest's second page and a man in his fifties with a
clipboard came out and copied a number off it, and made conversation
about the wind, and did not once mention the load, because there was no
load.

Then he walked the truck.

He came down the driver's side at an unhurried pace with his clipboard
against his thigh. At the rear he stopped and looked at the box, and then
past the box, out through the fence toward the slip, where the water lay
grey and flat under the last of the light and a barge was tied up with
nobody on it.

He looked at the water for four seconds. Then he came back up the
passenger side, and glanced in at the man in the seat, and said something
pleasant about the wind again, and went back into the scale house.

Mira, standing at the front bumper, did not register any of it. She was
looking at the manifest.

·

Inside the cab, Adam Marek had gone entirely still.

He knew that walk. He had done that walk. He had done it in this yard and
nine others, for eleven years, on eleven hundred nights, and he could have
described it in a deposition and had, in fact, described it in one, to a
woman named Raman with her glasses up in her hair, in February.

*What's the walk for?*

*You're checking the load and the plates and the tag, if there's a load.*

*And if there isn't a load?*

*Then you're not checking the truck.*

*What are you checking?*

*Where it's going to happen. And how much of it there's going to be.*

He watched the scale-house man look at the water and understood two
things in the same second: that the man was estimating the distance from
this fence line to that slip, and that the entire remainder of Adam
Marek's life was a scheduling question.

He did not move. He kept his hands on his knees and his eyes forward and
he thought, very clearly: *if you say it out loud right now, she is going
to think you are a man trying to save himself, and she is going to be
right, and she is going to stop listening to you for the rest of the
route.*

*Wait until she sees something herself.*

The wait was forty-one minutes and he spent all of them counting.

It was the only thing available. He could not get out — that was a rule
and he had heard it and he had meant what he said about hearing it — and
he could not warn her without becoming, in her eyes, the sort of cargo
that talks.

So he did the yard, the way he had done a yard every working day for
eleven years.

Nine acres. One vehicle gate on the west side and a personnel gate beside
the scale house that was chained but not locked, which he noted and knew
he would keep. Forty-one containers in the stacks and only nine of them
with seals he could see from where he sat, which meant the yard was
mostly empty and mostly theatre. Four light poles. Two lamps out.

A slip on the east side with a barge in it that had weeds in the cleats.

And no cameras. He looked for eleven minutes and found none — not on the
scale house, not on the poles, not the domes that every insurer in
America had been requiring since about 2016.

A commercial yard nine acres in size, inside a fence, on made ground, in
Porter County, Indiana, with no cameras on it.

That was not a saving. Cameras are cheap. That was a decision somebody had
made and had gone on making every year since about 2016, and had made for
one reason, and Adam Marek sat in a truck in the middle of it and worked
out what the reason was at about the same moment the man with the
clipboard came down the driver's side.

·

She came back and got up into the cab and started the engine and said
nothing for eleven minutes, and then, at the on-ramp, changed her mind
about the fuel stop she had told him about at Michigan City and took a
different exit.

Adam looked out at the road going by and did not say a word about it.

He noticed, though, that she did it without announcing it, and that she
did not explain it afterward, and he sat with that for four miles and
found that it had done something to his breathing.

Because she had not seen the walk. He had watched her the whole time and
she had been at the front bumper with her head down over four pages of
somebody else's typing, and she had not seen one second of it.

Which meant she had changed the fuel stop off something else. Off the
wind, or the way the clipboard man said good evening, or forty other
things a person accumulates in fourteen years and cannot itemize.

*She doesn't know what she knows,* he thought. *She just does the next
correct thing.*

It was, he decided somewhere east of Michigan City, the most reassuring
quality he had ever encountered in another human being, and he had no
intention whatsoever of telling her about it.

·

He asked her, at the second fuel stop, at ten at night, standing outside
the truck in a wind coming off the lake, because it was the only question
that mattered and because he had spent four hours deciding how to ask it.

"What happens if they change their minds about where I'm going?"

Mira Dalca was holding the fuel nozzle and watching the numbers turn over.

She did not look at him. In her experience the ones who asked were the
ones who had already worked something out, and the correct answer was
always the same, and she had given it thirty-odd times, and it had been
true every single time she had given it.

"Nothing I carry gets left on the road," she said. "You arrive on the day
I said, on your own feet, with your own name on you."

Adam Marek stood in the sodium light of a truck stop outside Benton
Harbor and something went through him that took him a second to identify,
because he had not felt it in six months, and had not expected to feel it
from a woman who had been handed him like a pallet.

"Say it again," he said.

She looked up.

"Why?"

"Because I want to be able to repeat it back to you later," he said,
"word for word, and I'd like to get it right."

The pump clicked off.

"Nothing I carry gets left on the road," she said, slowly, the way you
read out a number to somebody writing it down. "You arrive on the day I
said. On your own feet. With your own name on you."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank a carrier before delivery." She racked the nozzle. "It's
bad luck and it's bad arithmetic. Thank me on the eighteenth."

"Alright."

"And Marek—" She stopped, because she had used his name, which she did
not do, and because she was about to explain herself, which she did less
often than that. "That's not a comfort. That's a term. It's what I'm
selling. If I don't deliver it I'm not a business, I'm a woman with a
truck. So don't take it as kindness. Take it as the thing I'm actually
paid for."

"I'll take it as a term," Adam said.

He said it in exactly her register, without any irony in it at all, and
she looked at him for a second longer than she needed to and then got up
into the cab.

·

She gave him the seat rule at the second fuel stop, which was the only
rule she had ever invented on the spot for one passenger.

"You keep sitting like that."

Adam looked down at himself. He was in the passenger seat with his hands
on his knees and his shoulders square, which is how he had sat since
Torrence Avenue.

"Like what?"

"Like a man at a table waiting to be told something." She pulled her door
shut. "You've been in that seat for eleven hours and you've moved twice.
It's going to put your back out by Thursday and I've got nineteen days of
this."

"I'm being careful."

"I know exactly what you're being," Mira said, "and I'm telling you to
stop, and I'm telling you as an operational matter and not as a kindness,
so don't get anything out of it.

"A man who sits like that at a fuel stop is a man somebody looks at
twice. Nobody in the trade sits square. Drivers slump. Passengers put
their feet on the dash and get told off for it. If you want to be nothing
at all in a parking lot in Indiana then you have got to occupy that seat
like a person who is bored of it."

Adam Marek considered this.

Then he put his left elbow on the window sill and let his shoulder drop
about an inch and a half, and turned his head toward the glass, and became
— instantly and completely — a man on the fourth hour of a run he did not
care about.

"Better?"

"That's the most alarming thing I've seen all day," Mira said.

"I've watched four hundred of them."

"You've watched four hundred of them and you can do it from the *inside*
after eleven hours." She started the engine. "Do it all the time. That's
the rule. Even in the truck. Especially in the truck, because you can't
put a thing on at a fuel stop that you haven't got on already."

"Understood."

"And Marek—"

"Yeah."

"You can put your feet on the dash," Mira said. "I'd rather you did.
Nobody's dashboard has ever been the point."

·

Two hours later they were on US-12 in the dark with the dunes on the left
and nothing on the right, and he started talking.

Mira had seen this before. It came, always, at the same hour and under
the same conditions: dark cab, road noise, four or five hours of a person
sitting in one place with nothing to look at, and the fact that a
windscreen at night is very like a confessional in that both parties face
the same direction.

She had a policy. The policy was: let them.

"I drove for them eleven years," Adam said.

She kept her eyes on the road.

"I started at twenty-three. My uncle got me on. It was — you have to
understand, it was a *job*. It was the best job anybody in my family had
ever had. Benefits, actual benefits, through a leasing company, and I ran
a board out of a yard in Melrose Park with fourteen trucks on it by the
time I was twenty-eight." A pause. "And I never asked what was in the
back. Not once in eleven years."

"Nobody asks," Mira said.

"That's what I told myself."

"It's also true."

"It's both." He shifted. "And once it was a person."

The road went under them. She did not slow down and she did not speed up
and she did not turn her head, and afterward she would think about the
fact that she had done none of those things, and about how much practice
it must have taken to be able to do none of those things.

"In 2019," he said. "November. I had a run out of the Montreal yard that
should have been a container of machine parts, and it wasn't, and I found
out about forty minutes outside of Sarnia, and I kept driving." He said
it flatly and in order, the way he said everything, the doing first and
the reason nowhere near it. "I kept driving for six hours. I delivered
it. I signed for it. I went home and I slept, because I'd been up for a
day and a half, and that's the part—"

He stopped.

"That's the part I can't—"

"Don't," Mira said.

"What?"

"Don't do the next bit."

He turned his head. In the dash light she was a shape with two hands on a
wheel.

"You're going to tell me you couldn't sleep afterward," she said. "Or
that you drank. Or that you went to a church, or you didn't, or whatever
the next line is. And then I'm going to say something, because there's
only about four things a person can say, and then you'll feel better."

Silence in the cab.

"I'm not doing that," she said. "I'm not a priest and I'm not a
girlfriend and I'm not a bartender, and you don't get to put that down in
my truck and get out lighter at the other end. It doesn't work like that.
It's not a load."

Adam Marek sat in the dark for eleven miles.

"You're right," he said, at last.

"I know."

"No — I mean you're actually right, and I've never had anyone say it."
He was looking out the windscreen. "That's what I do. That's exactly what
I do. I say the worst thing about myself in the first four minutes so
that whoever it is has to decide about me right away, and then it's
theirs. It's not confession. It's — offloading."

Mira said nothing.

"Six months of proffer sessions," he said. "A federal prosecutor with her
glasses in her hair, three times, and it took a woman in a truck two days."

"It's a hundred and ninety miles to the next fuel stop," Mira said.

"Okay."

"There's a coffee in the door."

"Okay."

And that was all, for a hundred and ninety miles.

But in her head, in the dark, at eighty kilometres an hour on a road she
had driven forty times, Mira Dalca did an arithmetic she had never once
permitted herself and could not now stop.

*2019. November. Montreal to Sarnia.*

She had come in 2012, out of Constanța, in a container to Toronto and a
truck to Chicago.

Somebody had driven that truck.

She had spent fourteen years not wondering who, on the specific and
deliberate grounds that it was the single most useless piece of
information in the world, and that a woman who starts wondering that has
begun a project with no end in it.

She looked at the road, and the road was the road, and behind her in the
box was nothing at all, and beside her a man who had run the same line
sat with his hands on his knees, looking at the mirror, watching the
road behind them.


## Chapter Three — The Way They Count

They came back through Portage on the third day, because the manifest
said so.

Adam had not understood that at first — why a route from Chicago to the
Ontario border should double back on itself two hundred miles into the
run — and had spent most of day two working it out, and by the time they
turned south off I-94 he had it, and hated it.

It was not a route. It was a *ledger*. Every yard on the manifest was a
place where somebody looked at the truck and made a phone call, and the
phone calls were the product: three yards over nineteen days, at fixed
windows, on a fixed schedule, so that when it was over there would be a
sequence of independent human beings who could say *yes, the Dalca woman
came through, on time, with the load, exactly like she was supposed to.*

They were not tracking her. They were *witnessing* her. Building a
record. Which meant that somewhere at the end of it there was going to be
a need for a record.

He kept that to himself too.

·

Day two had ended in a lay-by outside Baroda with the seats back and a
blanket she produced from behind the bulkhead that smelled of the inside
of a box, and Adam Marek had discovered at about two in the morning that
he could not sleep sitting up.

It was not nerves. He had established that by lying awake analysing it,
which was itself the problem. His body had spent six months in a rented
house doing nothing and had lost the ability to be uncomfortable, and a
reclined truck seat is a machine for being uncomfortable in fourteen
separate places at once.

She was out inside four minutes. That was the thing he could not get
over. A woman with a manifest in her door pocket that had a fixed
delivery date on it and four hundred and ten thousand miles of habit in
her hands, and she went down like a switch had been thrown, and woke at
ten to four without an alarm.

"You didn't sleep," she said, in the dark, before her eyes were open.

"I slept."

"You didn't sleep, because you've moved eleven times and every time
you've done it in stages so I wouldn't hear it." She sat the seat up.
"You can't sleep sitting up."

"No."

"Right." She started the engine. "Then you're driving nothing and you're
sleeping on the second leg, because from Baroda it's four hours of flat
and I'll take the wheel and you'll go under about twenty minutes in when
the noise gets constant. That's how it works. It's not a personality
thing. It's a noise thing."

She was correct about that, as it turned out, and he did not forgive her
for it for two days.

·

The other thing he learned on day two was that she would not eat while
driving.

Not a sandwich, not a bag of anything, not so much as an apple. She would
drink coffee — she drank an astonishing amount of coffee, in the specific
manner of a person who had learned to treat it as fuel rather than
pleasure — but the moment food entered the cab she pulled over, and if
there was nowhere to pull over she went hungry for forty miles until
there was.

He asked about it at a gravel turnout east of Sawyer, on the third
morning, because it had begun to bother him in a way he could not
identify.

"Why not?"

"Because eating is a thing you do with your hands," Mira said. She was
eating a boiled egg out of a paper napkin, standing at the front of the
truck, looking at the road. "And I can't do a thing with my hands and
watch the mirror."

"Nobody watches the mirror that hard."

"I do."

"For eleven hours a day."

"For fourteen years," she said, and put the shell in her pocket, because
she did not litter, which he also noticed.

·

The second visit ran differently from the first, in the way that all
second visits run differently, and Adam noticed every part of it because
noticing was, at this point, the entire content of his life.

The scale-house man came out before they stopped rolling.

That was the first thing. On the first visit he had made them wait eleven
minutes, which was correct — you make a driver wait, always, on principle,
because a driver who is not made to wait starts to think of himself as a
guest. On the second he was out the door with his clipboard while the air
was still bleeding off the brakes.

The second thing was that he did not look at the paperwork.

Mira handed the second page in through the window and the man took it and
folded it in half without reading it and put it in his back pocket, which
was a thing that no yard man on earth does with a document he intends to
copy a number off.

The third thing was that he walked the truck again.

Same walk. Down the driver's side at that unhurried, professionally bored
pace, a hand on the box at the halfway mark the way you'd steady yourself
on a fence you did not need steadying on. At the rear he stopped.

And this time Adam Marek watched him in the mirror and counted, because a
number is the only thing you can do anything with.

Six seconds at the rear of the box. He did not look at the doors. He did
not look at the seal, and there was a seal, and a yard man who is
checking a load looks at the seal within the first two seconds because
that is what a seal is for.

He looked past the truck, through the fence, at the slip.

Eleven seconds.

Then he turned around and came up the passenger side, and Adam kept his
eyes forward and let the man look at the side of his face, and heard him
say something about the wind, and heard Mira answer something about the
wind, and then the man went back inside the scale house.

He was in there for four minutes. When he came out he did not have the
clipboard.

·

Mira climbed up and shut the door and sat for a second with her hand on
the key.

"Say it," she said.

"Say what?"

"You've had a face on you since Michigan City and you've got a worse one
now." She turned the key. "You've been sitting there working on something
for two days like a man doing sums under a desk. Say it or stop doing it,
because I can't drive with somebody thinking that loud beside me."

Adam looked out at the fence going past.

He had planned this. He had had eleven hours to plan it and he had
planned it as carefully as he had ever planned anything, and every
version he built came apart on the same problem, which was that he was a
man in her custody and everything he said was contaminated by that fact.

*He's going to kill me* is what a frightened passenger says. It is also,
sometimes, true, and the trouble is that the sentence is identical in
both cases and there is no way to tell them apart from the outside.

So he did the only thing he had left, which was to give her the
observation and not the conclusion.

"He didn't look at the seal," Adam said.

Mira's hand stopped on the shifter.

"What?"

"Twice now. Both walks. He came down the driver's side, he stopped at the
back of the box for six seconds, and he never once looked at the seal."
Adam kept his voice absolutely level. "The first time I thought I'd
missed it. I didn't miss it."

"There's no load. He knows there's no load."

"Then why walk the truck at all?" He turned his head. "You don't walk a
truck for the pleasure of walking it. You walk it to check the load, the
plates, the tag and the seal. If there's no load, there's nothing to
check, so you don't walk it — you take the paper, you make your phone
call, you go back inside where it's warm. That's the whole job. I ran
that job for eleven years."

Mira sat very still.

"So what was he doing."

"He was looking at the water," Adam said.

He watched her take it. He watched her *not* take it — watched a woman
who had spent a decade building a professional apparatus specifically
designed to prevent her from having thoughts like this one put her hands
back on the wheel and pull out of the gate at a steady eight miles an
hour.

"That's a walk and a look," she said. "That's not a fact."

"No. It's not."

"People look at water."

"They do."

"It's the *lake*, Marek. There's water on every side of everything for
four hundred miles."

"That's right," Adam said. "And I've been in forty yards that had water
next to them, and I know exactly what it looks like when a man is
enjoying a view, and exactly what it looks like when he's measuring a
distance, and I'm telling you what I saw. That's all I'm telling you. I'm
not telling you what it means."

She drove.

She got them onto the ramp and up to speed and out into the flat
grey-green of northern Indiana, and for eleven minutes she said nothing
at all, and then, at the interchange, she did a thing that Adam Marek
would remember for the rest of his life, because it was the first
evidence he had that he was in the truck of a person and not the truck of
a policy.

The manifest said the day's fuel stop was the Pilot at exit 34.

She went past exit 34 at seventy miles an hour, took 39, came off, ran
four miles north on a county road, and fuelled at an independent station
with two pumps and a hand-lettered sign, where a woman in her seventies
took cash and did not have a camera.

She did not explain it.

Adam did not ask.

They stood on either side of the truck in the wind while it filled, and
neither of them said one word about what had just happened, and both of
them understood that they had just done something together.

·

It cost her all afternoon.

Not the four miles and not the nineteen minutes. What it cost her was the
running of the sum, over and over, on a two-lane road with a lake wind
pushing the box around, because deviating from a manifest is not one
decision, it is a decision that has to be re-made every eleven minutes
for the rest of the day.

*I took a different fuel stop.* That was the whole of it. She had taken a
different fuel stop on a job for a family that had, in fourteen years,
never once given her a written instruction and had this time given her
four typed pages, one of which said NO DEVIATION in ordinary lower-case
letters.

The fuel card would show it. That was the part she kept arriving back at.
She had paid cash at the independent, which meant the card would show a
gap of two hundred and eleven miles between fills, and a gap is louder
than a stop.

She could not make it not have happened. She could only decide, over and
over, whether she was going to do it again.

At about four in the afternoon she said, to the windscreen: "You get one."

Adam looked up.

"One what?"

"One of those." She did not take her eyes off the road. "You saw
something and you told me the thing you saw and not what you thought it
meant, and you did it without one word about your own situation in it,
and I want you to know that I noticed that and that it's the only reason
we're on this road instead of the other one.

"But you get one," Mira said. "If the next one's a feeling, or a bad
night, or a man looked at you funny, then you've spent it, and I'll go
back to driving the manifest, and I'll be right to. So don't spend it on
weather."

"Understood."

"Say it back."

Adam Marek sat in a passenger seat with his hands on his knees.

"Don't bring you anything I can't count," he said.

"That's it. That's the whole employment contract."

·

She thought about Ted Wisniewski for the rest of the afternoon, which
happened perhaps four times a year and always on days when a document had
turned out to be about something.

He had been seventy-one when she met him and had owned eleven trucks and
had smelled permanently of Swarfega. She had come to him in 2013 at
twenty years old with no licence, no papers, no English worth the name
and an ability to reverse a fifty-three-foot trailer into a gap that she
had acquired in nine months of being shouted at in a yard in Hegewisch,
and he had put her on the yard hostler for four dollars an hour cash and
had not asked her one question about herself in six years.

What he taught her, other than the truck, was the reading.

He did it on purpose. He sat her down in the shed with a clipboard on a
Tuesday in 2014 and said: *the driving isn't the business. Anybody can
drive. The business is that somebody in the building can read a rate
sheet and find the lie in it, and if that isn't you, then you work for
whoever it is.*

So they did rate sheets. Then bills of lading, then broker
confirmations, then insurance schedules — and he put deliberate errors
into them, a transposed digit, a tolerance in the wrong column, an
accessorial that did not exist, and made her find them. He was delighted,
in an entirely undemonstrative way, on the day in 2015 when she found one
he had not put there.

*There you are,* he said. *Now you can't be robbed.*

The sign over the gate was not his. That was the thing she had taken four
years to work out and had never once raised with him: WISNIEWSKI CARTAGE
went up in 1961, in white on green, in a typeface nobody had used since,
and it had been put there by Tadeusz Wisniewski, who came out of a camp
in 1949 with one truck and a nephew and had gone on to have four trucks
and then eleven, and who had died in 1979 with his son running the board.

She asked Ted about it once, in 2015, while the paperwork on the yard was
being drawn.

*Why not put your own name on it? It's been fifty years.*

*Because a sign that's been up since 1961 doesn't get read,* Ted said.
*That's the whole of it. A new sign is a question. An old sign is
furniture.*

And then, after a moment, in a different voice, because he was
seventy-three by then and had begun saying true things without being
asked:

*And because I like the idea that there'll still be a truck going down
Torrence with my father's name on it after I'm finished, and he never did
anybody any harm in his life, which is more than I'd swear to about
myself.*

He said the same thing to her about her own name the following week, and
twice more before he died, and she never did it.

Mira Dalca drove north through the flat green of western Michigan with a
man asleep in the passenger seat and thought about a sign, and about the
fact that she had run eleven years of somebody else's freight under a
dead man's name, and that she had told herself for the whole of it that
this was camouflage.

She had never once considered the other reading, which was that she had
been standing under it the way a person stands under a roof.

·

She waited until he was asleep.

It took until the second night — he did not sleep easily, and she had
noticed that he only went under when the road noise was constant, which
meant a long flat stretch, which meant she had to want it and wait for
it. She got it at half past eleven on a straight run of US-31 north of
Holland, and when his breathing changed she reached over and turned the
dash light down to nothing and pulled off into the dark apron of a closed
farm-equipment dealership.

She left the engine running so the sound would not change.

Before she got out the manifest she sat for a minute and looked at him.

She had not done that. Not once in three days — not properly, not with
her head turned. She had looked at the road and the mirror and the
paperwork, and had built an entire operational relationship with a human
being out of peripheral vision, which was the method, and the method was
the method for a reason.

He slept badly. He slept the way a man sleeps in a chair in a hospital
corridor: chin down, one hand closed, the whole body braced against the
possibility of being woken.

Forty-one years old, she thought, and did not know where she had got the
number, and then realized she had got it off a manifest that gave his
height and his approximate weight and no personal effects.

*You're not going to learn him,* she told herself. *That's the whole
apparatus. Eleven people are alive because of that apparatus.*

And then she thought, because it was two in the morning and there was
nobody to hear it: *he counted my mirror checks.*

Nobody had ever done that.

Fourteen years of yards and gates and scale houses, and a great many
people had learned things about Mira Dalca in that time — that she was
fast, that she did not drink, that she paid in cash and on the day, that
she could be relied upon and could not be moved. All of them things you
learn about an instrument.

He had counted the mirror checks and then he had *told her the number*,
flatly, without making it mean anything, and then had gone back to
looking out of the window.

She put the thought where she put things, and got out the manifest.

She had read it four times. She had read it the way you read a route
sheet — for the yards, the windows, the contact names, the crossing. She
had not read it the way she read everything else in her life, which was
as a document produced by a person with an intention, and now she sat
under the map light with a pen and a fuel receipt and did the thing she
had done since she was twenty-one and Ted Wisniewski had taught her, on
the theory that a business is not a business until somebody in it can
read.

Page four was the fuel and mileage allowance.

It was, she registered again, unusually detailed. Not a lump sum. A
schedule: a per-leg allowance, out and back, with tolerances, in the
style of a company that has been reimbursing drivers for forty years and
has stopped trusting any of them.

Out: Chicago to Portage to the shoreline route to the crossing to the
lay-over to the border yard. Nineteen days, with the waits built in.

Back: Port Huron to Chicago. Three hundred and forty-one miles.

She checked the outbound allowance against her own numbers, because she
had run every foot of that road and knew what the truck did on it. It
came out within four percent, which was fine, which was normal, which was
a real number written by a real person who knew trucks.

Then she checked the return.

And it was wrong.

Not wildly. Not by an amount anybody would ever query. She sat with the
pen in her hand and worked it a second time on the back of the receipt,
in the small square figures she had been making since a school in
Constanța, and got the same answer, and then worked it a third time
because the answer had to be wrong.

The return allowance was low.

Three hundred and forty-one miles at the rate they had used for the
outbound legs came to a number. The number on the page was less than
that, by an amount that a person who did not do this for a living would
never notice — call it four percent the other way, call it eleven
dollars.

Eleven dollars.

She sat in a dark truck on the apron of a farm-equipment dealership with
a fuel receipt on her knee and eleven dollars of arithmetic in front of
her, and understood that she was looking at the whole thing.

Because the outbound rate had been calculated for a loaded truck.

And the return had been calculated for an empty one.

That was the four percent. That was the eleven dollars. Somebody in an
office had sat down with a rate card and worked out, honestly and
carefully and with the diligence of a man who takes pride in his
paperwork, that on the way out the vehicle would be carrying a payload of
roughly one hundred and ninety pounds, and that on the way back it would
not be.

Mira Dalca looked at the number for a long time.

Then she looked at the passenger seat, where a forty-one-year-old man
who had lost eighteen pounds in six months slept badly with his head
against the window.

She did the arithmetic once more, because she was not a woman who
accepted a total she had run only three times.

It came out the same.

She got a lighter out of the door pocket and burned the fuel receipt in
the ashtray, which had never been used, and watched it go, and the smell
of it was strong and wrong in a cab that had never had a smoker in it.

Adam moved in his sleep and said something that was not a word.

"Sleep," Mira said, quietly, in Romanian, which she had not spoken aloud
to another human being in four years.

Then she put the truck in gear.

She drove north for two hours with both hands on the wheel and her jaw
set, past the exits she had planned to take, past the motel she had
budgeted for, and somewhere around one in the morning, north of Muskegon,
in the dark, she began — for the first time since a container ship in
2012 — to make a plan that had nobody's permission in it at all.


## Chapter Four — The Coffee Tin

She called Benny at six in the morning from a rest area on I-196, on a
burner she had bought in a gas station in Holland with cash, standing at
the far end of the lot with her back to the wind and the truck two hundred
feet away where she could see both its doors.

He picked up on the fourth ring, which meant he was under something.

"It's me."

"I know it's you." A clank, and the sound of a man sliding out from under
a truck. "It's six in the morning and it's a number I don't know, and
there's one person alive who does that."

"Anything?"

"Two men came to the yard yesterday." He said it in exactly the voice he
used for *twelve's got a noise*. "Half two in the afternoon. Very
pleasant. One did the talking. They had a clipboard, Mira, an actual
clipboard, I want you to picture that."

"What did they want?"

"Routes." A pause. "They said they were doing a survey for an insurance
consortium about lane utilisation on the Michigan corridor and they
wanted to know which roads we favour, our typical dwell times, whether we
run nights, whether we run the same lanes twice." Another pause. "They
asked it well. That's what I want you to hear. If I hadn't spent thirty
years in yards I'd have answered every question and made them a cup of
tea."

Mira watched a family unload three children and a dog from a minivan
seventy feet away.

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them we're a small operation and I'd need it in writing from
their principal, and I asked whether they'd be sending a certificate of
authority, and I asked twice, and by the second time the one who wasn't
talking had gone back to the car." Benny's voice did not change at all.
"They'll come again."

"They'll come again," she agreed.

"Mira."

"Yes."

"I have been minding this yard for you for eleven years and I have never
once asked you a question I did not want the answer to. I'm going to ask
one now and then I'm going to go back under twelve." The sound of him
setting something down. "Is what's in your truck the reason those men
came?"

"Yes."

"Right," Benny said. "Thank you."

And went back under twelve.

·

She stayed at the fence for a while after the call, in the wind, with the
phone in her hand.

Under the plank in the dispatch shed, in a coffee tin that had held
Maxwell House in 1988, there was a piece of paper folded twice.

She could see it. She could see the way the fold had gone soft where she
opened it, and the third column where the ink changed because she had run
out of the good pen in 2021, and the eleven lines.

*Kosovar woman + child, Aug 2019, Melvindale yard → Windsor via the
Coyle cut. Alive.*

*Two brothers, Bijeljina, Apr 2021, Toledo → Fort Erie. Alive.*

*Man, Ho Chi Minh City, name unknown to me, Oct 2022, the Portage yard →
Thunder Bay via the Soo. Cried four hours in the floor and said thank
you in a language I don't have. Alive.*

Eleven of them.

Number seven was the one that cost.

Number seven was a man of about thirty out of the Portage yard in
February 2023, moved north to the Soo in the false floor with a blanket
and a bottle and four hours of road, who had given her a name at the
start of the run that she had known immediately was not his, because
people who give you a false name give it a fraction of a second late.

She had not pressed. She never pressed. She had written the name he gave
her on the list because the list took what she was given.

Four months later she had been standing at a counter in a diner in
Hammond with a newspaper somebody had left, and there had been a
photograph of a man on page eleven under a story about a body found in a
drainage channel in Cicero, and the man in the photograph was not him. It
was his brother. She knew it was his brother from the jaw and from
nothing else, and the story gave the family name, and she had stood at a
counter with a coffee going cold and understood two things at once: that
she now knew what to write in the third column, and that the reason a man
gives you a false name is that there is somebody left behind who has the
true one.

She had gone back to the yard and taken up the plank and changed the
entry, in a different pen, because the good pen was finished.

That was the whole of number seven. He was alive. He was, as far as she
knew, alive right now, somewhere north, under a name that was not either
of the two she had for him.

It cost because it was the only entry on the list with a piece of
somebody else's arithmetic in it, and because she had never been able to
decide whether the brother in the drainage channel belonged in her column
or not, and because there was nobody on earth she could ask.

·

The way it worked was simple, which was the obscene part. The family
moved people; that was a business and had been a business since before
she was born. Most of the people the family moved arrived. They arrived
frightened and in debt and priced for the rest of their lives, and that
was the ordinary product, and Mira Dalca had been one unit of it in
2012.

But some of them did not arrive, and there was no mystery about which
ones. A person who could identify somebody. A person whose family had
failed to make the second payment and whose continued existence had
become an argument the family did not want circulating. A person who had
been quiet in the wrong yard at the wrong time and had seen something
that had a name.

And when the family had one of those, they did not use their own people.
They put it out to a carrier, because a carrier is deniable and a
carrier's yard is not the family's yard, and because the carrier is the
last name on the paperwork.

They had put eleven of them out to her.

And eleven times, Mira Dalca had made the delivery *somewhere else*.

It was not heroism and she had never once described it that way, even
privately. It was a series of extremely careful logistics problems, each
one solved with a false floor and a route nobody had sold her and a
window of about forty minutes, and each one had required her to lie to
the family afterward in a specific and sustainable fashion — *he ran at
the Toledo stop, I looked for two hours*, *she was gone when I opened the
box, there's a hole in that fence, ask Sammy* — and each lie had been
believed, because a carrier who loses one is a carrier who is bad at her
job, and being thought bad at her job was the cheapest thing Mira had
ever bought.

Eleven times. In eleven years.

And she had kept a list.

*Burn it,* Benny said, twice a year, and he was right, and she knew
precisely how right he was: that piece of paper was the only object in
North America that could take eleven people who now had jobs and
apartments and children in schools and put them back in a container.

She had a whole architecture of reasons why she kept it. That she needed
the routes recorded so she did not reuse one too soon. That if she was
ever taken it would be evidence of what the family did. That someone,
someday, might have to prove it.

None of them was true and she knew it.

She kept it because if nobody wrote it down, it did not happen.

Because a woman who has spent fourteen years being a unit of freight has
a specific and unslakable need for one document, somewhere, in her own
hand, that says: *this life produced eleven things, and here they are,
itemized.*

She had never been able to explain that to Benny, who was a good man and
had been safe his entire life and thought it was about sentiment.

She put the burner in her pocket and walked back to the truck.

He was awake. He had the door open and was sitting sideways in the seat
with his boots on the step, and he had — she saw this from forty feet
away and her stomach went — his hands in plain sight on his knees, palms
down, the way a man sits when he wants the person walking toward him to
be able to see that he has not moved.

"I didn't get out," he said.

"I know."

"I opened the door because it was ninety degrees in there and I wanted
air, and then I thought about it and I put my feet on the step and not on
the ground, and I want you to know I did that on purpose and I feel like
an idiot about it."

Mira Dalca stood in a rest area on I-196 at ten past six in the morning
and looked at a man being careful about a doorway.

"The rule's about fuel stops and yards," she said. "It's not about a rest
area at six in the morning with nobody in it."

"You said—"

"I know what I said. I say it at the gate to everybody and I don't put
conditions on it, because conditions are how people get themselves
killed." She got up into the cab. "But you're allowed to stand on the
ground next to my truck at a rest area, Marek. You're not a dog."

He got in and shut the door.

"That's the second time," he said.

"Second time what?"

"That you've told me something you didn't have to."

Mira started the engine and did not answer, because the honest answer was
that she had noticed it too, and had noticed the noticing, and had spent
the whole of the walk back from the fence arguing with herself about it,
and had lost.

·

The call came at eleven the next morning, when they were stopped in a
gravel turnout north of Ludington and she was checking a strap that did
not need checking.

Adam was asleep in the cab. She had left the burner on the seat and it
buzzed against the vinyl, and she came around and got it and looked at
the number and did not recognize it, and knew.

She walked forty feet from the truck before she answered.

"Yes."

"Mira." Traversa. There was a television on somewhere behind him, an
afternoon programme with an audience. "How's the road."

"It's a road."

"How's the weather up there?"

"Grey."

"It's beautiful here." A pause, and the audience laughed at something.
"You took a different fuel stop."

She looked at the gravel between her boots.

There it was. She had been waiting for it for thirty-six hours and it had
still, somehow, landed in her stomach like a step missed in the dark.

"I take different fuel stops," she said. "I've taken different fuel stops
for eleven years. Ask anybody who's ever ridden with me."

"That's what I said." Traversa sounded genuinely pleased. "That's exactly
what I said. I said, Mira Dalca doesn't fuel where the sheet says, she
fuels where the diesel's clean and the woman behind the counter doesn't
have a camera, and if you wanted a driver who does what a sheet says you
should have hired a company."

"So we're finished."

"We're not finished, we're chatting." The television went off. The change
in the sound of the line was enormous. "Let me say a thing and then I'll
let you go, because you're working.

"Your yard runs on Osei's licence."

The wind came across the turnout and moved the gravel dust.

"It's a good arrangement," Traversa went on, comfortably. "I've never
had a word to say against it. The man's careful, he's clean, he's had
that authority since 2015 without a single mark on it, and everything you
own sits on top of it. Every trailer. Every contract. Your whole eleven
acres."

"Gino—"

"And a licence is a piece of paper with a man's name on it," Traversa
said. "That's all it is, Mira. It's a piece of paper in a drawer in
Springfield with a man's name on it, and paper's fragile. Somebody makes
a complaint. Somebody notices a discrepancy in a filing from 2019 that
nobody was ever going to notice. There's a review. Reviews take about
eleven days and there isn't one thing anybody can do about them, not me,
not you, not a lawyer, because it's the *state*, and the state doesn't
take phone calls."

She said nothing.

"You're at the third day of nineteen," Traversa said. "The seventeenth is
the seventeenth. That's all I called to say."

"Gino."

"Yes, sweetheart."

"Say the rest of it."

"There isn't a rest of it." He sounded, genuinely, faintly surprised.
"That is the whole of it. I've told you about a licence and you've
understood me and neither of us has said one word that anybody could
write down. Do you know how long it took me to learn to do that? Twenty
years. My father couldn't do it. My father threatened people, and the
reason he did eleven years in Marion is that a threat is a *thing*, Mira,
it's an object, and objects go in evidence bags."

"So nothing's happened."

"Nothing's happened at all," Traversa agreed. "There's a hauler on a run
and a dispatcher with a clean licence and a lot of weather. And on the
seventeenth of May it'll all still be true and you'll come back and we'll
have a coffee and I'll tell you about my knees."

"You've never once had a coffee with me."

There was a pause on the line just long enough to be an answer.

"No," Gino Traversa said. "I don't come inside."

She stood in the turnout with the phone against her ear for a second
after the line went dead.

Then she did the thing she did, because it was the only thing she had.

She got back in the truck and started the engine and said, to a man who
had been awake for the whole conversation and had the courtesy not to
mention it:

"Two hundred and eleven miles to the Manistee cut-off. Four hours if the
wind stays where it is. There's a place at the top of it that does eggs
until two."

"Okay," Adam said.

"I'm going to drive it in three."

"Okay, Mira."

She pulled out onto the road, and after about a mile Adam Marek said,
conversationally, looking straight ahead:

"Nine."

"What?"

"Mirror checks. In the last mile." He had his hands on his knees. "It was
nine on the first day too. It's nine when you're steady. I thought you'd
want to know it's still nine."

Mira Dalca drove for a while with her jaw very tight, and did not thank
him, and could not have said, if anybody had asked her, why her eyes had
gone hot.

·

The fourth night they slept in the truck in a turnout above Pentwater
because there was nowhere else and because she would not go into a motel
two days running.

She had the driver's seat back and her coat over her and she did not
sleep at all, and at about half past one she gave up pretending and lay
in the dark with her eyes open, doing what she had been doing since a
turnout north of Ludington.

*Your yard runs on Osei's licence.*

The thing about Traversa's sentences, she had come to understand over
eleven years, was that they were never about the thing they were about.
He had not called to tell her that he could hurt Benny. She had known
that since 2015. He had called to tell her that he had *already done it*
— that the review was open, that the machinery had been started three
days ago and could not now be stopped by anybody including him, and that
therefore the only question left on the table was whether it ran the full
eleven days.

That was the elegance of it and she could not help admiring it, in the
way you admire a well-built false floor.

He had not threatened her. There was nothing to repeat to anybody. There
was a real complaint about a real discrepancy in a real 2019 filing, and
a real state officer would review it in a real proceeding, and every
single component of it was true and none of it would ever have happened
if a woman had not taken a different fuel stop on the third day.

*Everything they're doing is real,* Benny would say to her eleven days
later, in a hallway in Springfield. *There's just a set of true things
arranged into a shape.*

She lay in the dark and thought: *and I've been doing that too.*

Because the eleven were the same trick run backwards. Eleven separate
lies to the family, each one individually plausible, each one built out
of true things — a hole in a fence at Toledo that really was there, a
forty-minute wait at Portage that really did happen, a scale that really
was open. She had never once told the family an outright falsehood in
eleven years. She had told them a set of true things arranged into a
shape.

Mira Dalca lay in a truck in a turnout above Pentwater at half past one
in the morning with a man asleep four feet away, and had, for the first
time in her life, a thought she could not put anywhere:

*I learned that from him.*

·

He found the tail on the fifth day, outside Muskegon, and he found it
because finding it was the only work he had.

He had a system by then. He had built it out of nothing over four days
because a man with no phone, no map, no ability to leave a vehicle and no
say in his own direction has exactly one asset, which is that he is
facing backward.

The system was this: every vehicle that stayed with them for more than
eleven minutes got a fragment. Not a plate — plates were mostly
unreadable at distance in road spray — but a *fragment*: a bent trim
strip, a dealer frame, a roof rack with one crossbar missing, the way a
particular driver sat.

By the third day he had eleven fragments in his head. By the fourth,
eight of them had dropped off, which is what happens with traffic.

Three stayed.

"Grey sedan," he said. "Malibu, '19 or '20, right front hubcap's a
different grey than the other three. It's been behind us since Benton
Harbor."

Mira did not look in the mirror, which was correct, and which he
approved of so violently that it took him a second to keep going.

"That's two days."

"It's two days and it's not one driver. It's at least two. The first one
sits close and drifts — he's four seconds back and he wanders in the
lane. The one who's on it now sits at seven and doesn't move an inch.
They changed at the fuel stop at Whitehall."

"And the second."

"White box truck, twenty-six-footer, leasing company decal peeled off the
door badly enough that you can still read the shape of it. It's not
behind us. It gets *in front*." Adam kept his voice flat and
professional, which was, it turned out, the register in which he had
spent his entire adult life and the only one that had ever done him any
good. "It passed us at Grand Haven and it was in the lot at the Ludington
turnout when we came out. It's leapfrogging. That's a two-vehicle
box — one behind, one ahead — and you run it when you don't want to
follow, you want to *know*."

"And the third?"

"The third's nothing. It's a guy with a boat."

Mira drove for a mile.

"Say the plate fragments again," she said.

He said them. She repeated them back, once, and he understood from the
way she did it that she was putting them somewhere permanent.

"How do you know all this," she said.

"I ran their yards."

"That's not an answer. Any yard man knows what a tail looks like on a
television programme. You just gave me a spacing pattern and a shift
change."

Adam looked out the window at Michigan going by.

"Because for eleven years," he said, "my job was to make sure that when
one of our loads got followed, the driver knew before the tail did." A
beat. "I taught this. To about forty men. I had a *sheet*."

He heard himself and stopped.

"And now I'm going to apologize for it," he said, "which is the thing you
told me not to do, so I'm not going to. I'm just going to tell you what
the box looks like, and you can decide what to do about it, and I'll shut
up."

Mira Dalca reached over and turned the radio off, which had been on, low,
for four days, and which neither of them had ever mentioned.

"Don't shut up," she said. "Start counting."


## Chapter Five — The Unlisted Crossing

She took the cut on the sixth day, at four-twenty in the afternoon, in
rain.

She had been building toward it for a day and a half without telling him,
and the building had consisted almost entirely of doing nothing
interesting: fuelling where the sheet said, stopping where the sheet
said, running the speed limit through two counties, and letting a grey
Malibu sit at seven car lengths for two hundred miles while a white box
truck leapfrogged ahead of them and waited in lots.

"You're being boring on purpose," Adam said, somewhere north of Pentwater.

"I'm always boring."

"You're being *conspicuously* boring. You've fuelled at the sheet's
station twice. You did the mandatory stop at the mandatory place and you
sat in it for the mandatory forty minutes and you bought a coffee you
didn't drink." He was watching the mirror. "You're feeding them a pattern
so they'll believe the next thing that looks like the pattern."

Mira said nothing, which was an answer.

"How long have you had this planned?"

"Since Muskegon."

"And you didn't tell me because—"

"Because if you'd known, you'd have started riding differently, and the
man in the Malibu has been looking at the back of your head for two
hundred miles." She checked the mirror. "You'd have sat straighter.
Everybody does. It's not a criticism, it's a fact about necks."

Adam thought about that.

"That's genuinely good," he said. "I'd never have thought of that."

"You would."

"No, I wouldn't. I'd have thought of the route. I never thought about the
passenger's neck once in eleven years."

"Yes," Mira said. "I know."

The cut was a haul road.

It ran, gate to gate, three and a half miles through a sand and gravel
operation east of the shore highway, from a county road on the south side
to a service crossing on the north side that came out on the far bank of
a river the state had bridged in exactly two other places within forty
miles. It was private property from end to end. It had no county
designation. It appeared on no navigation product anywhere on earth
because the operator had, in 2004, successfully petitioned to have the
whole parcel classified as a single industrial site, and industrial sites
do not have roads, they have *internal circulation*.

Eleven minutes, gate to gate, at the speed the washboard allowed.

No cameras, because a gravel operation photographs its scales and its
office and nothing else. No county, because there was no county in it. No
record, because the gate code went into a logbook that a foreman named
Delgado had been forgetting to keep since the Bush administration.

She had found it in 2019.

She had found it with a Kosovar woman and a nine-year-old boy in the
false floor and thirty-five minutes on the clock and a car she could not
lose on the shore highway, and she had turned off onto a gravel entrance
because she had seen a haul truck come out of it that morning, and she
had gone through the whole thing on nerve with no idea where it came out,
and it had come out on the far side of a river with the shore highway
eleven miles behind her and nobody within four minutes of her in any
direction.

She had gone back three weeks later, in the daytime, with a legitimate
load of pipe for the operation's own yard, and had introduced herself to
Delgado, and had done a favour for Delgado in 2020 that Delgado had never
been asked to repay, and had been running the gate code ever since.

Nobody had sold it to her. That was the thing. Every other route in her
head had come from somebody — a driver, a dispatcher, a man in a bar who
wanted to be impressive. This one she had found herself, at speed, with a
child in the floor.

She had used it four times in seven years.

The favour, since nobody had ever asked and she had never told anybody:
in March 2020 a loader operator at the sand pit had put a hydraulic line
through his forearm at ten past four on a Friday afternoon, eleven miles
from a highway, in a week when the hospitals had begun turning away
anything that could wait, and Delgado had been standing in the rain with
a man bleeding into a hi-vis jacket and a company protocol that required
him to call a number in Grand Rapids.

Mira Dalca had been coming out of the north gate with an empty box.

She had put the operator across the passenger seat with Delgado holding
the arm and had done thirty-one miles in twenty-two minutes, and had
gone in through the ambulance bay because she did not know where else to
go, and had then stood in a parking lot for four hours in the rain
because Delgado had nobody to drive him home.

That was it. That was the entire transaction. She had never mentioned it
again and neither had he, and the gate code had changed twice since then
and both times a folded slip of paper had appeared in her door pocket at
the Portage yard inside a week.

She thought about that sometimes, on the nights when she thought about
the architecture of her life: that the four routes in her head that were
genuinely hers had all been built the same way, out of somebody's
worst afternoon, and that not one of them had been bought.

"Two hundred yards," she said. "When I turn, don't look behind us."

"I won't."

"You will. Everybody does. Look at the mirror on your side, because
that's a thing a passenger does anyway, and don't turn your head."

The rain had come in off the lake at three and had settled into the kind
of steady grey that kills visibility without ever becoming weather. The
Malibu was at seven lengths. The white box truck had gone by them
fourteen minutes ago and would be sitting in the lot at the junction
eleven miles ahead, waiting for a truck that was not going to arrive.

Mira did not signal.

She came off the shore highway at fifty-one miles an hour onto an
unmarked gravel entrance between a stand of jack pine and a berm, and was
through the first gate — which stood open, from six a.m. to six p.m., as
it had every working day since 2004 — before the Malibu had finished
going past the mouth of it.

Then she stopped.

Right there, forty feet in, behind the berm, with the engine running.

"What are you—"

"Wait."

Eleven seconds. Twelve.

Through the pines she saw the grey Malibu go by on the highway at speed,
committed, in the outside lane, with two hundred yards of empty road
ahead of it and a driver who had just lost a truck he had been watching
for two days and did not yet know it.

"That's the part that gets you," Mira said. "They don't lose you at the
turn. They lose you when you stop. A driver who's following expects the
truck to be *going somewhere.*"

Then she put it in gear and took the haul road.

It was, Adam thought, the single most impressive eleven minutes of
driving he had ever been a passenger for, and he had been a passenger for
a great deal of driving.

The road was washboard gravel with berms on both sides and no shoulder
and standing water in the ruts, and it ran between conveyor structures
and stockpiles forty feet high, grey and black and rust, in the rain. A
loader came the other way at the two-mile point, and Mira put two wheels
up onto the berm without slowing down and gave the operator a lift of two
fingers off the wheel, and the operator gave it back, and that was the
whole transaction.

At the north gate there was a chain and a keypad on a post.

She rolled down the window into the rain, put in a five-digit code
without looking at it, and the chain went slack, and she drove over it.

Then she stopped again, and got out, and walked back in the rain and lay
the chain flat the way she had found it, and got back in soaked to the
shoulders, and drove on.

"You put the chain back."

"You always put the chain back."

Adam looked at the mirror. There was a river behind them now and eleven
miles of highway on the other side of it and no vehicle in the world that
knew where they were.

"How many of these do you have?" he said.

"Enough."

"That's not a number, Mira."

She glanced over.

It was the first time he had used it. She registered that fact with the
same flat clarity with which she registered everything, and filed it, and
found that it would not stay filed.

"Four," she said. "Real ones. Ones that are mine and nobody sold me and
nobody's using. Four in the whole country." She turned the wipers up.
"There were five. I burned one in 2022."

"Burned how?"

"Used it twice in a year." She watched the road. "You use a route twice
inside a year and somebody notices. Then it's not a route, it's a habit,
and a habit gets you killed."

Adam sat back.

"You're the best I've ever seen at this," he said. "I want to say that
once and then I won't say it again, because I know what you think of
compliments. But I ran a board with fourteen trucks and I sat in on route
planning with men who'd been doing it for thirty years, and I have never
in my life seen anybody do what you just did."

Mira Dalca drove for about a mile.

"It's not a compliment," she said, at last. "It's an observation."

And Adam Marek laughed — genuinely, once, surprised out of himself in a
truck in the rain on the sixth day — and after a moment, looking straight
ahead at the road, so did she.

They ate at a table for the first time that evening.

It was not a decision either of them made. It was a consequence of
geography: the river road came out at a crossroads with a bar and grill
and a hardware store and nothing else for nineteen miles in any
direction, and it was seven in the evening, and she had been up since
half past three.

She parked at the back, out of the light, facing out, which was so
automatic that she did not register having done it until he said, "You
always park facing out," and she said, "So do you, you just don't have a
vehicle."

Inside there were eleven people and a television with a hockey game on
and a woman who brought two menus without being asked.

"Two rules," Mira said, sitting. "You take the seat facing the door. I
know that's backwards — everybody thinks the driver takes the door — but
you're the one who'd get recognized and I'd rather you saw it coming than
me."

"And the second?"

"You order for both of us, because I've got an accent and it's the first
thing anybody remembers about me."

Adam Marek ordered a walleye and a burger and two coffees in the flat
Chicago-Polish vowels of a man from Archer Heights, and did it without
looking at the woman's face, and the woman went away without having
looked at either of theirs.

Mira sat with her back to the room for the first time in fourteen years
and found it took her about eleven minutes to stop counting.

"You do it too," Adam said, eventually.

"Do what."

"Two exits." He had not moved his head. "Front, and the one through the
kitchen past the ice machine. You clocked them coming in. I watched you
do it and it took you about a second and a half."

"It's a habit."

"It's a *good* habit and I've got it too and I want to say the thing
about it that nobody ever says." He turned his coffee cup around.
"Everybody thinks it's fear. It isn't. I've been afraid, properly, four
times in my life and I couldn't have counted a door in any of them. This
isn't fear. It's the opposite. It's the thing you do so you can sit down."

Mira Dalca looked at a man across a table in a bar and grill in the
middle of Michigan.

"Say that again."

"It's the thing you do so you can sit down," Adam said. "You count the
exits and then the room's finished, and you can eat."

The food came. It was very good, in the specific way of food in places
that have one competitor and know it, and neither of them said anything
for eleven minutes, and afterward Mira Dalca would think of that meal as
the first hour of the whole nineteen days in which nothing at all
happened.

The stretch across the mitten took nine hours and neither of them slept.

They came off the river road onto US-10 east at eight in the evening and
ran through Baldwin and Reed City and Evart in the dark, ninety miles of
two-lane with the rain quitting somewhere around Chase, and by eleven at
night they were in the flat middle of the state where there is nothing at
all and the towns are eleven miles apart and the radio gets one station.

Nine hours in a cab is a room with no doors and a view that keeps
changing. There is nothing in it to do and nothing to look at that lasts,
and after the fourth hour the ordinary defences of a human personality
simply stop being available, because a defence requires maintenance and
maintenance requires something else to be happening.

They discovered the boring things first, which is what nine hours is for.

That he sang, very quietly, under road noise, and did not know he was
doing it, and stopped instantly and completely if she moved. That she
counted mile markers out loud in Romanian when she was tired, in
threes, and had been doing it since 2013 and had never once noticed.

That he was, by a distance, the more frightened of the two of them, and
also the calmer, and that these were not in conflict.

That she took her coffee with four sugars, which he found so at odds with
every other thing about her that he laughed out loud at a gas station in
Reed City and could not explain why, and that she took the fourth sugar
out of the next one and he had to spend eleven minutes talking her back
into it.


That he could not sleep sitting up, but could sleep with his head against
the window if the road was straight, which meant she had been choosing
straight roads for two days without admitting it. That she could not eat
and drive at the same time — physically could not, would put a sandwich
down on the console and forget it existed for ninety miles — and that
this was a thing he began working around by the fourth day without being
asked, handing her food in pieces at stops.

That they both hated the radio and neither had been willing to say so
first, and that it had been on, low, since Chicago, out of pure mutual
courtesy, for five and a half days.

"Turn it off," Adam said, at midnight.

"You turn it off."

"It's your truck."

"You're the guest."

"I'm the *cargo*," Adam said, and reached over and turned it off, and the
silence that came in was so enormous and so welcome that they both
started laughing at the same moment for no reason a third party would
have understood.

Somewhere past Clare, at one in the morning, he asked where she was from.

He asked it well. He had had five days to learn how she took questions,
and he asked it the way you ask a person about their work: as though the
answer were a fact about the world rather than a claim on her.

"Where'd you come up?"

She gave him a distance.

"Twelve hundred miles," she said. "Port to port."

"That's the whole answer?"

"It's the accurate one."

"It's a distance."

"Yes."

He let it go, which was the correct move, and which she noticed. And
because he let it go, and because it was hour seven, and because the
middle of Michigan at one in the morning is a place where nothing you say
seems likely to survive until daylight, she gave him the port.

"Constanța," she said.

Adam Marek did not move.

"It's on the Black Sea," she said. "It's the biggest port on it. It's got
a casino on the front that's been falling into the water since before I
was born and everybody photographs it." She watched the road. "I was
nineteen. There was work here — that was the thing you got told, there
was work here and there was a way to get to it and somebody would arrange
the way and you'd pay it back out of the work. Everybody knew somebody
who'd done it. My mother's cousin's girl did it in 2009 and sent
photographs of a car."

"How."

"Container to Toronto." Her voice had gone very level. "Nine days. Not
the whole nine in the box — four. There's a warehouse in the middle where
you're out. Then a truck down to Chicago, and a room over a shop on
Cermak with three other people in it, and a job wiping trailers for
somebody who paid the room instead of paying me."

She checked the mirror.

"And a number. Eleven thousand dollars, against future work. Somebody
said it to me in the first week, in English I didn't have, and I nodded,
and that's the whole contract. Nobody ever said it again." She lifted one
hand off the wheel and put it back. "That was 2012. It's 2026. I own
eleven acres and forty trailers and nine trucks and I have never, in
fourteen years, been out from under that number for one single day."

The road went under them. There were no other headlights anywhere in the
world.

"Say something," she said, after a while, "or I'm putting the radio back
on."

Adam Marek had his hands flat on his knees and was looking at the
windscreen.

"What was the month," he said.

"What?"

"You said 2012. What month did you come into Chicago?"

Mira frowned at the road.

"April," she said. "The end of April. I remember because there was still
ice in the alley behind the shop and I didn't understand it. It was
twenty-eight degrees in Constanța when I left."

Adam did not say anything for eleven miles.

She glanced over twice. His face, in the dash light, had nothing at all
on it, which she had come to understand in six days was not the absence
of an expression but the most extreme one he had.

"Marek."

"Yeah."

"You went quiet."

"I did," he said. "I'm sorry. It's a long night."

And she let it go, because that was the arrangement, because she had let
his silences alone for six days exactly as he had let hers, and because a
woman who has spent an hour telling a stranger the true shape of her life
does not then interrogate him about a pause.

She drove another forty miles before either of them said anything.

"Ask me something," she said, eventually, "because if you don't I'm going
to spend the rest of tonight deciding what the silence was."

"Alright." Adam sat up in the seat. "What's the yard's insurance renewal
month?"

Mira laughed — a real one, out of nowhere, the second in twelve hours.

"October," she said.

"And what's the deductible?"

"Ten thousand, which is criminal, and I'd have moved brokers in 2023
except the man who writes it is Kowalczyk's brother-in-law and it would
have taken about nine months off Kowalczyk's life."

"There it is," Adam said. "That's the answer to the other question."

"What other question?"

"Where you're from." He put his head back. "A woman who eats a
ten-thousand-dollar deductible for four years so that a sixty-one-year-old
who hates her doesn't have a bad Christmas with his sister isn't from a
port. That's not a place, that's a *practice*, and I've been in the truck
eleven hours today and I've now got the whole of it."

Mira Dalca drove through the middle of Michigan at one in the morning
with both hands on the wheel and did not say anything for eleven miles,
and then said, "You're going to have to stop doing that."

"Doing what?"

"That thing where you take something I told you about a rate and hand it
back as a thing about my character."

"No," Adam said, pleasantly, and went to sleep.

She would think about that pause for four days.

She would think about it, afterward, for years — about the fact that she
had been handed it, out loud, on a two-lane road in the middle of
Michigan at one in the morning, and had driven straight past it at
sixty-two miles an hour with both hands on the wheel.


## Chapter Six — What She Carries

The second yard was a shuttered marine hardware lot on the Saginaw Bay
side, four acres of cracked concrete behind a building with its windows
whited out, and the manifest said seventeen hundred hours on the seventh
day.

They came down the approach at half past four with the bay flat and
pewter on the left, and Mira saw it from a mile out and understood the
whole thing before she was within four hundred yards of the gate.

Three vehicles in the lot.

The manifest said one contact. One man, one vehicle, forty minutes, and
the same non-transaction as Portage: hand in a page, get looked at, get
phoned about.

There were three cars, and a man standing at the gate who was not
standing the way a yard man stands, and a second one visible at the
corner of the building with his hands in his jacket pockets in
sixty-degree weather.

She did not slow down.

She went past the exit at sixty-two miles an hour with her eyes forward
and her hands at ten and two, and she counted eleven seconds, and then
another eleven, and then she was around the curve and there was nothing
in the mirror at all.

Neither of them said anything for nine minutes.

She did not want to know what he was doing with his hands or his face and
did not look, and drove, and the nine minutes were the longest of the
whole first week.

What went through her head in them was a single sequence, repeated, the
way a machine repeats when the input does not change.

*One contact, forty minutes, hand in a page.* That was the manifest. That
was the shape of Portage and it was the shape of every yard on the sheet,
and it had been true at Portage twice, and the truth of it twice was the
reason she had come down that approach road at half past four with the
bay on her left and nothing in her chest at all.

Three cars.

The arithmetic was not difficult. Three cars is two more than one, and
two more than one is not a change of plan, it is a *plan*, because two
extra vehicles do not assemble themselves in a shuttered lot on the
Saginaw Bay side at four in the afternoon by accident.

She thought about the fact that she would have driven in.

That was the part she kept coming back to, at sixty-two miles an hour on
a shore road: that if she had come down that approach on the second day
instead of the seventh, before the walk and the water and the eleven
dollars and the man who counted her mirror checks, she would have taken
the gate at eight miles an hour and gone and stood at a scale house
window with her manifest page in her hand.

"That was the yard," Adam said, finally.

"That was the yard."

"How many?"

"Three cars. Two men outside." She let out a breath. "The sheet says one
man."

"So the sheet's for you and the yard's for something else."

"The sheet is a *receipt*," Mira said. "It's not for me. It's so that
afterward there's a piece of paper that says the Dalca woman came through
on time and everything was normal." Her jaw was tight. "I've been reading
it wrong for a week. It's not a route. It's a chain of custody."

Adam Marek looked out at the bay.

He had worked that out on the second day.

He said, "Yeah," and let her have it, because there is a way of already
knowing a thing that helps nobody, and because the only useful gift he
had left was letting her arrive at things in her own truck at her own
speed.

Benny called at eight that night, on the burner, and this time he called
her, which he had never done.

"Two things," he said. "You'll want to sit down for the second one."

"I'm sitting. I'm always sitting. I'm a driver."

"That's true." A pause. "First: they came back. Same two. Yesterday
afternoon, and this time they had a name. Yours. They said they were
following up on the lane survey with the *owner* this time, and could
they speak to Ms. Dalca, and I said she's out on a run, and they asked
which lane."

"What did you say?"

"I said I don't discuss client lanes with insurance consultants who won't
produce a certificate of authority, and then I asked the taller one what
his surname was, and he told me, Mira, he actually told me, and I wrote
it down and I put it in the safe. I don't know if it's real. But he told
me, which means he's twenty-eight years old and somebody sent him out
without telling him what he was doing."

Mira stood at the fence of a rest area on M-25 with her back to the wind.

"And the second thing."

"The state opened a review on the licence yesterday at four."

The wind came across the lot.

"That's not — Benny, the renewal packet's not due for ninety days—"

"It's not the renewal. It's a review. It's a different animal and it
comes off a complaint." His voice did not change at all, which was how
she knew. "It's a Chicago complaint about a 2019 filing discrepancy, and
I've had the file up on the screen for four hours, and Mira, there *is* a
discrepancy in a 2019 filing. It's a transposed digit in a trailer
registration. It's been there for seven years. Nobody on earth was ever
going to find it."

"How long does a review take?"

"Eleven days," Benny Osei said, "give or take. And they can suspend
pending, and if they suspend pending, every truck in that yard stops
rolling that day."

She closed her eyes.

Three cars in a lot on the bay. A twenty-eight-year-old with a clipboard
who did not know what he was for. A transposed digit from 2019 that had
sat in a database for seven years waiting to be an instrument.

"He's going to call me," she said.

"He's going to call you," Benny agreed.

"Benny. If it goes to suspension—"

"Then I'll be a mechanic with a very tidy licence file and no yard to
apply it to, and I'll be fifty-eight, and I'll be fine." He said it
absolutely evenly. "That is not the thing to be doing arithmetic about
tonight, and you're doing arithmetic about it, and I can hear you doing
it. Stop."

"Benny—"

"Twelve's got a noise," Benny said. "I'll call you Thursday," and hung
up.

·

She did not sleep after that call, and at about half past two in the
morning, sitting in the driver's seat of a stationary truck in a motel
lot in Standish with a man asleep four feet away, Mira Dalca did
something she had not done since 2019, which was to build a plan she had
no intention of using.

It was a habit Ted had given her and had called *the empty column*.

*You never run a job off one plan,* he had said, in about 2014, with a
rate sheet on the desk between them. *Not because the plan's bad.
Because a person with one plan stops being able to see. You build the
second one, properly, all the way to the end, and then you put it in a
drawer, and the whole point of it is that it's in the drawer.*

So she built it.

She built the version where she turned around.

It took about forty minutes and it was, she noted with the flat interest
of a professional, a good plan. Off the shore road at Standish, west on
US-23, the Coyle cut before eleven in the morning, a woman in Thunder Bay
by Thursday. It cost four thousand two hundred dollars and a route she
would never use again and it worked, and there was not one leg in it she
could not have driven.

And at the end of it there was Benny Osei in a hearing room in
Springfield, and eleven acres going quiet on a Tuesday, and eleven people
with jobs and apartments and children in schools whose names were on a
piece of paper under a plank in a shed that two men had already looked at
the floor of.

She put it in the drawer.

That is the thing she would try to explain, badly, to a woman in Beverly
on a Thursday evening four years later: that from about two-forty in the
morning on the eighth day of nineteen, she had known exactly how to save
him, and had known exactly what it cost, and had carried both numbers in
the same head for nine more days without once being able to make them
resolve.

·

Adam found the compartment at six the next morning, in a motel lot in
Standish, changing a tire.

It was not a dramatic discovery. The inside rear on the driver's side had
picked up something on the haul road and had been going down for two days
at a rate that only became a problem overnight, and he had said *I can do
that* at half past five in the way of a man who has been sitting in a
seat for seven days and would pay money to lift something heavy.

She had let him.

That was, in retrospect, the whole event: she had let him.

He got the truck up and the wheel off, and while he was in there he
noticed the plate.

The false floor was not amateur work. That was the first thing he
registered, professionally, with a kind of unwilling admiration: it was
built into the structure of the box behind the bulkhead, framed properly,
with the plate sitting flush in a rebate so that a hand run along the
floor would find nothing. It was not a smuggler's box, the kind he had
seen fifty of, cut in with a grinder and screwed back badly. Somebody had
built this with a plan and a square.

The catch was under the lip. He found it with two fingers because he had
spent eleven years finding things with two fingers, and the plate came up
on a piano hinge without a sound, and he looked in.

It was long enough for an adult to lie flat and about eleven inches deep.

There was a vent — not a hole; a proper louvered vent, ducted, so that
what came in was air and not exhaust.

There was a bottle holder, screwed to the side wall, of the kind that
goes in a boat.

And there was a light.

A little battery reading light, the kind you clip to a book, mounted to
the underside of the plate on a bracket somebody had made by hand out of
strap steel and painted with a brush.

Adam Marek knelt on the concrete of a motel parking lot in Standish,
Michigan, at four minutes past six in the morning, and looked at a
hand-made bracket holding a reading light in the roof of a hiding place,
and his legs stopped working properly.

Because the vent was survival. The bottle holder was survival. Any
competent criminal builds those, because a body that suffocates is a
problem and a body that dehydrates is a problem.

But nobody in the history of the trade had ever built a *reading light*.

Somebody had lain in that box, in the dark, with a woman driving, and had
gotten out at the other end, and the woman had gone home and thought
about the dark.

He knelt there for a while.

What he was thinking about, and what he would tell exactly one person in
his life, three years later, was a fuel stop north of Sarnia in November
2019 and a knock in three.

Because he had stood at the back of a trailer that night and run every
option a man can run, and not one of the options he ran had been *is
there air in there.* It had not occurred to him. It had genuinely never
crossed his mind, not that night and not once in the seven years since,
and he had spent four hours a week for six months describing that night
to a federal prosecutor and had never said the word air.

Somebody had built a duct.

Somebody had sat in a shed with a piece of strap steel and a hacksaw and
bent a bracket, and had painted it with a brush because a bare bracket
rusts, and had screwed a two-dollar clip-on reading light to the inside
of the roof of a hole in the floor of a truck.

He put the plate back down.

He heard it click into the rebate, and he ran his hand over the floor to
be sure it was flush, and then he finished the tire, and torqued the nuts
in the right sequence, and dropped the truck off the jack, and put the
tools away in the order they went in the box.

Mira came out of the motel office at twenty past six with two coffees.

## Chapter Seven — The One Who Drove

He did not tell her at the fuel stop.

He meant to. He had sixty-one miles to build it and he built it four

Then she came around the back of the box with a receipt in her hand and

They ran north through the dark and she did not wake him at Harrisville.

She had meant to. It was a straight enough stretch and she had told him
she would, and at about half past twelve she looked over and he had gone
under against the window with his mouth slightly open and his hands
finally, for the first time in nine days, off his knees, and she drove
past Harrisville at sixty-one miles an hour and let him sleep.

*It's about you. And I'm in it.*

She worked it the way she worked everything, which is to say
systematically, in a column, discarding as she went. It was not the
family, because he had no way of knowing anything about the family she
did not already know. It was not the yard. It was not the seventeenth,
because the seventeenth was hers.

be true, a very small set of things, and every one of them was in 2012,
and she had handed him 2012 herself on a two-lane road at one in the

Alpena, on the tenth day.

## Chapter Eight — The Imbalance

The tenth day was the first day in ten that had nothing in it.

That was its own kind of violence. They had come up an outside stair at
half past nine at night into a room with a slope in the ceiling, and had
woken to a harbour full of fog and a boat that was not coming, and the
entire apparatus that had been holding both of them upright — the route,
the clock, the mirror, the next fuel stop — had been switched off at the
wall.

Mira Dalca did not know what to do with a day.

She discovered this at about eight in the morning, standing in the middle
of a rented room in her coat with the keys in her hand, and it took her
until eleven to stop finding jobs.

She checked the truck. She checked the truck again. She walked the
harbour end of the town twice, once each way, timing it, and found four

they were parked, which is what vehicles do.

floors, yes."

She kept sorting.

"Take the bed, Marek."

"No."

She looked up.

"No?"

"No." He set the cup down on the windowsill. "And I'd like to say why,
properly, because if I don't say it properly it's going to sit in this
room for two days and get bigger, and this room is twelve by fifteen."

Mira stopped sorting.

"Alright," she said. "Say it properly."

·

Adam Marek turned around and put his back to the window.

He had been building this since Standish. He had built it while lying
under a truck and while riding four hundred miles with his hands on his
knees, and he had built it because he had understood, somewhere on the
Huron shore, that the thing between them had begun to have a shape and
that the shape was standing on ground that was not level, and that
somebody was going to have to say so out loud or it would go bad in a way
that could not be repaired afterward.

"I want to say a thing so it's said," he said. "Then I'll stop and we
never have to do it again."

"Go."

"You can open that door and I can't."

The room was very quiet. Down in the harbour something knocked
rhythmically against a piling.

"That's not a complaint," Adam said. "You've been straight with me for
ten days and you've never once used it, and I want that on the record
before I say the rest, because the rest is going to sound like an
accusation and it isn't one." He put his hands in his pockets. "But it's
true. It's the truest thing in this room. You've got the keys and the
money and the roads and the only opinion that matters about where this
truck goes, and I have got a jacket and a testimony date. If you decide
tomorrow morning to drive me to that yard on the seventeenth and hand me
over, there is nothing I can do about it. That's not a fear. That's an
arithmetic."

Mira said nothing.

"And I've been in a truck with you for ten days," he said, "and there's
something happening in it, and you know there is, and I'm not going to
sit here and pretend I don't. And I'm telling you that as long as that
first thing is true, the second thing can't be. Not a look. Not a joke
that's slightly not a joke. Nothing."

"Because you're afraid of me."

"Because I'm *not*," Adam said, "and that's the problem."

He said it hard enough that she looked up.

"If I were afraid of you I'd know what this was. I'd know that anything I
felt was a hostage feeling and I could put it in a box and be careful
with it." He shook his head. "But I'm not afraid of you, and I haven't
been since the second fuel stop, and that means every single thing I feel
in this room is one I can't verify. I can't tell whether I want to be
near you or whether I'm a man in a cell being kind to his guard, and
neither can you, and neither of us is going to be able to until I'm
standing somewhere with my own money and my own door."

He stopped.

"That's it," he said. "That's the whole speech and I've been writing it
since Standish."

·

Mira Dalca stood in a rented room above a chandlery with a bag half
sorted on the table and looked at a man she had been handed eleven days
ago in a fog on Torrence Avenue.

She had never in her life had anybody do this.

That was the thing that went through her, and it went through with an
almost physical force. In fourteen years — with the family, with the
men she had worked for and the two she had briefly not worked for, with
every single person who had ever wanted something from her — nobody had
ever stood in a room and named the imbalance out loud before using it.
That was not how it was done. The imbalance was the *point*. You
established it and then you were gracious about it, and the graciousness
was the instrument.

He had put it on the table like a fault in a load.

"Right," she said.

Her voice came out flat, which she was glad of.

"Then here's mine, and I'll say it as plain as you said yours, because
I'm not going to be worse at this than you are."

Adam waited.

"You're in my truck because you can't get out of it," Mira said.

"Yes."

"Nothing happens between us while that's true."

"That's what I—"

"I'm not finished." She put the bag down. "Nothing. Not a look I have to
wonder about afterward. Not a hand on a shoulder in a fuel stop. Nothing
either of us has to sit up at night deciding what it was. And I don't
mean out of politeness. I mean it as a rule, and I keep rules, and I have
never in eleven years broken one of my own."

"Agreed."

"I'm still not finished." She had to stop for a second. "Because there's
a second half of it and you're going to hate it.

"It doesn't come off when we get to the border. It doesn't come off when
you're standing in a courthouse. It comes off when you have got somewhere
you can walk to. An actual place, with a door you're holding, and money
in your pocket that isn't mine, and a real choice about whether you ever
see me again."

Adam Marek looked at her for a long moment.

"That could be months," he said.

"It could be never," Mira said. "That's the rule. Rules that only cost
you when it's cheap aren't rules."

The thing in the harbour knocked against its piling.

"Agreed," Adam said.

"Say it back."

"Nothing happens while I can't leave," he said, "and it doesn't come off
at the border or in a courtroom. It comes off when I'm standing
somewhere with my own door and my own money and I could walk in any
direction, and I choose one." He nodded once. "Agreed. Both ways. And
Mira — if I break it, you throw me out of the truck, and you don't
explain it to me, and I won't ask."

"And if I break it?"

"You won't."

"Say the sentence, Marek."

"If you break it," Adam said, "I'll say so out loud, in the room, at the
time. I won't be gracious about it. I've had ten days of you being
straight with me and I've developed a taste for it."

Mira Dalca picked the bag back up.

"Good," she said. "You're still not having the floor."

·

Afterward, when it had been said and agreed and nothing further was
required of either of them, they discovered that they did not know what
to do with a room.

Adam washed the two cups, badly, in a sink with one tap.

Mira finished sorting a bag that had not needed sorting and then put
everything back into it in the order it had come out, and knew she was
doing it, and did it anyway.

At about half past eleven he said, "Can I ask you something that isn't
about any of this."

"Ask it."

"How did you learn to drive?"

Mira Dalca stopped with a roll of tape in her hand.

"That's the question."

"That's the question. I've been carrying it since the haul road and I
haven't been able to ask it because everything in this truck has been
about the seventeenth." He dried his hands on his trousers. "You put two
wheels on a berm at thirty miles an hour in the rain without slowing
down. Nobody does that. Who taught you?"

"A yard in Hegewisch," she said. "2012, 2013. There was a man called
Sipos who ran the hostler and drank at lunch, and I was twenty and I
worked eleven hours a day for a room, and when Sipos was drunk in the
afternoon somebody had to shift the trailers." She put the tape in the
bag. "So I did. Nobody taught me anything. I learned by hitting things
slowly for about nine months, and by the end of it I could put a
fifty-three into a gap you'd swear was fifty-two, and that's the only
qualification I've ever had for anything.

"Then Ted found me, in 2013," she said. "And the first thing he did, the
actual first thing, before the licence and the truck and any of it, was
sit me in a shed with a rate sheet."

"Not a truck."

"Not a truck. He said anybody can drive." She half-smiled at the bag,
which he saw and did not comment on. "It took me two years to understand
that he was telling me how not to be robbed and I heard it as an insult
about my driving."

Adam Marek leaned against the sink.

"So the answer to *how did you learn to drive*," he said, "is that
nobody taught you and you did it by yourself in a yard for nine months
until it worked."

"Yes."

"Right," Adam said. "Then I'd like to formally retract every single time
I've told you in the last ten days that something you did was
impressive."

"Good."

"Because I've been complimenting you on a *technique*," he said, "and
there isn't one. There's a woman in a yard in Hegewisch in 2012 hitting
things slowly."

Mira Dalca stood in a rented room over a chandlery with a bag in her
hands and found, to her considerable irritation, that this was the
nicest thing anybody had ever said to her.

·

They slept six feet apart with the window open two inches and a lake wind
coming in over the harbour, Adam in the bed under a blanket that smelled
of somebody else's washing, Mira on the floor on the runner with her coat
over her and her boots by her hand.

Neither of them slept for a long time.

At about two in the morning Adam said, into the dark, "Are you awake."

"Yes."

"Is the floor bad?"

"The floor is a floor."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the accurate one." A pause. "It's bad."

"Take the bed."

"No."

"I'm not offering to *share* it, I'm offering to swap—"

"I know what you're offering," Mira said. "The answer's no, because if I
take the bed I'll lie in it doing arithmetic about whether I took it
because I wanted the bed. And then I'll be awake anyway, on a better
surface, being a liar."

Adam lay looking at the slope of the ceiling.

"That's the most Mira Dalca sentence I've ever heard," he said.

"There isn't a Mira Dalca sentence."

"There is, and it goes: *here is the true thing, here is why it costs, I
have already decided.*" He put an arm behind his head. "You've done about
forty of them since Chicago. Benny's probably got a list."

"Benny has got a list. He keeps it on the board with a magnet and he
thinks I don't know."

"What's on it?"

"*It's not a compliment, it's an observation.* *Don't thank a carrier
before delivery.* *That's not a plan, that's a hope with a map on it.*"
She shifted on the runner. "He wrote them down for four years and then he
put them up on the board in about 2021 and neither of us has ever
mentioned it out loud."

Adam Marek lay in a bed in a room above a chandlery and laughed at the
ceiling, quietly, for some time.

"What," Mira said.

"Nothing. It's just — " He stopped. "A man in a shed has been keeping a
list of the things you say."

"He's been taking the mickey."

"Mira," Adam said, "he has been keeping a *record*."

The fog moved against the window.

She did not say anything for a while, and when she did her voice had gone
somewhere else.

"Yes," she said. "I know."


"Go to sleep."

"Okay." A minute went by. "Mira."

"What."

"Thank you for the three days," he said. "I want to say it once more
because tomorrow I'm going to use one of them."

·

She woke at four, because she woke at four.

The room was grey. The lake wind had dropped and there was a fog on the
harbour thick enough that the breakwater lights were three soft spots in
it and the water was not visible at all.

She got up off the floor and put her boots on and her coat over the
clothes she had slept in and looked, for one second, at the man asleep in
the bed with one arm out from under the blanket.

Then she went down the outside stair.

The breakwater at Alpena runs out from the harbour mouth for about half a
mile, a spine of poured concrete and armour stone with a light on the
end, and on the north side of it the water is harbour water and on the
south side it is the shipping channel, and where they meet there is a
long grey seam that does not close.

She walked out along it in the fog with her hands in her pockets.

It took eleven minutes. The concrete was wet and the armour stone on the
south side was black and slick, and there was nobody on it at
five in the morning and nothing to hear but water moving in the gaps and,
somewhere a long way off in the grey, the low three-note call of a boat
telling another boat where it was.

Halfway out she stopped and did a thing she had done perhaps twice in
fourteen years, which was to stand still for no reason.

Ten days. Eleven acres and forty trailers and a man in Chicago with a
licence under review, and a review takes eleven days, and it had been
open since Wednesday.

Seven days left on the manifest.

She ran it and got the same answers she had been getting since the
farm-equipment apron north of Holland: that the eleven dollars was real,
that the yards were a chain of custody, that there was a man with a
photograph of Benny Osei in his jacket, and that the only variable in the
whole system she had any purchase on was the seventeenth of May at eight
o'clock in the evening.

And under it, unbudgeted and unwelcome, running the whole time like a
second engine: *and there is a man asleep in a bed above a chandlery who
put a bag of sugar on a table and thought about it for four minutes
first.*

She walked the rest of the way out.


At the end she stood and looked at the seam.

Somewhere out in it, standing off, waiting for the fog to lift and its
slot at the plant to open, a thousand-foot carrier had its deck lights
on. She could not see the hull at all. Just the lights, in a long low
line, suspended in grey, eleven of them, with nothing under them.

She heard him on the concrete behind her about ten minutes later. She did
not turn around.

"You always get up at four," Adam said.

"Yes."

"Since when?"

"Since 2012."

He came and stood beside her, not close, both of them facing the water,
which was — she thought — the only geometry in which either of them had
ever been able to say anything.

"I'm going to use one of my days," he said.

"I know. I came out here so you could."

"How did you—"

"Because you're a man who waits for a flat surface and a clear head,"
Mira said, "and there's no wheel in my hands and I've had four hours'
sleep on a rug, and it's the best you're going to get." She kept looking
at the lights. "And because I've got something to tell you first, and
mine's shorter, and I'd rather go first because I don't think I'll be
able to after."

"Go."

She took her hands out of her pockets.

"They're not paying me to deliver you," Mira said. "They're paying me to
be the last person who saw you."


## Chapter Nine — The Door

Adam Marek did not move for a while.

The fog stood on the water. The eleven lights hung in it in their long
low line, going nowhere.

"Say how you know," he said.

"The fuel allowance."

"Say all of it."

So she told him all of it, standing on a breakwater at ten past five in
the morning, in the only register she had ever been fluent in, and it
took about ninety seconds because that is how long the truth takes when
it is arithmetic.

"There's a per-leg schedule on page four. Rate card, out and back, with
tolerances. It's good work — somebody who's been reimbursing drivers for
thirty years wrote it, because the outbound legs are within four percent
of what my truck actually does on those roads, and that's not a number
you get from a spreadsheet, that's a number you get from knowing." She
kept her eyes on the water. "The return's three hundred and forty-one
miles, Port Huron to Chicago. And the return allowance is *low*. Four
percent under. Eleven dollars."

"Eleven dollars."

"The outbound rate is calculated for a truck carrying a hundred and
ninety pounds," Mira said. "The return rate is calculated for an empty
one."

The lake did not do anything at all.

"So somebody sat in an office," Adam said slowly, "with a rate card."

"And did the job properly. That's what I keep — " she stopped, and
started again. "It's not the murder. I've known this business since I was
nineteen, I know what it is. It's that whoever did the paperwork was
*careful.* He didn't round it. He could have rounded it and no human being
would ever have looked, and instead he took the eleven dollars off,
because the truck's going to be lighter, because that's how you do a rate
card properly."

Adam looked at the side of her face.

"When did you work it out?"

"The third day. Outside Holland. You were asleep."

"The *third day.*"

"I did it three times," Mira said. "Then I burned the receipt in the
ashtray and I drove for two hours."

Adam Marek let out a long breath into the fog.

"I've known since Portage," he said.

She turned her head.

"The first walk," he said. "Not the second one. The first. When he went
down the driver's side and stopped at the back of the box for six seconds
and looked at the slip." He put his hands in his coat pockets. "I've done
that walk, Mira. I taught it to about forty men. You do it in a yard when
there's no load, and what you're checking isn't the truck, it's the
distance from the fence line to the water and how many of you it's going
to take."

"You've known for eight days."

"Yes."

"And you didn't—"

"I couldn't," Adam said. "That's the part I need you to hear, and it's
the worst thing I'll say to you this morning. I couldn't tell you,
because I'm a man in your custody, and a man in custody who says *they're
going to kill me* sounds exactly like a man in custody who wants out of a
truck. There's no difference in the sentence. There's no way to say it
that isn't contaminated." He looked at the water. "So I had to wait until
you found it yourself. Eight days. And every one of those days I sat in
that seat knowing, and let you drive."

"That's why the fuel stop."

"That's why the fuel stop." He nodded once. "You went past exit 34 at
seventy miles an hour on day three and took an independent four miles up
a county road with a cash register and no camera, and I sat there and
didn't say one word about it, and it was the best moment of my life to
that point."

Mira laughed. It came out of her wrong, a single ugly sound in the fog,
and she put her hand over her mouth for a second and took it away.

"That's a low bar for a life, Marek."

"It was a low six months."

·

The fog moved. Out in the channel the eleven lights slid, very slightly,
and did not go anywhere.

"Say your second thing," Mira said.

"I'd rather do it sitting down."

"There's nowhere to sit. There's a breakwater and there's a room with one
bed in it and I'm not doing this in the room." She turned to face him
properly for the first time. "Say it here. Whatever it is, I've just told
you they're going to put you in the water and you took it like a
weather report, so I don't think either of us is going to fall over."

Adam Marek turned around and put his back to the channel.

"In 2012," he said, "I ran the Constanța line out of the Montreal yard."

Mira Dalca did not move.

"Four years, 2011 to 2015. That was my line. Containers into Montreal off
the Med boats, road to Toronto, and then Toronto down to Chicago, and I
did the Toronto–Chicago leg myself about one week in three because it was
the leg that paid."

The water went on doing what water does.

"April of 2012," he said. "The end of April. There was ice in the alleys
still. It was a nine-day box out of Constanța with a warehouse stop in
the middle, and there were four of them in Toronto and one of them was
going to Chicago, to a yard on Cermak, to a man called Ilie who ran a
shop and rooms above it and put people in the rooms and paid the rooms
instead of paying the people."

Mira had gone completely still.

"They were priced at eleven thousand dollars," Adam said, "against future
work."

The eleven lights hung in the fog.

"I routed you," he said. "I'm the one who drove."

·

Afterward she would not be able to reconstruct the next several minutes
in any order.

She knew she did not speak, because she remembered the silence going on
so long that a bird got up off the armour stone below them and went away
into the fog, and she remembered thinking, with a clarity that struck her
as obscene, *that's a herring gull.*

She knew she did not move toward him and did not move away.

And she knew, because the body keeps its own record, that at some point
her hands had come out of her pockets and she had put both of them flat
on the top rail of the breakwater ladder, palms down, the way she put
them on a desk when a number would not behave.

"You knew," she said. "On the road out of Clare. At one in the morning. I
told you Constanța, and I told you April of 2012, and you went quiet for
eleven miles and I asked you and you said *it's a long night.*"

"Yes."

"You've known for four days."

"I've known for four days that it was almost certainly you," Adam said.
"I've known for about ninety seconds that it *is*, because you just said
Ilie's rooms and I have never said that name to another living person
outside of a federal building."

"I didn't say Ilie."

"You said a room over a shop on Cermak with three other people in it and
a job wiping trailers for somebody who paid the room instead of paying
you." His voice was not steady. "That's the same sentence, Mira. That's
the same sentence I gave a prosecutor in February."

She looked at the water.

"Do you remember me."

"No," Adam said. "I want to lie to you and I'm not going to. I never saw
the people. That was the design — the driver never sees the load, so that
the driver can say he never saw the load. I did the Toronto–Chicago leg
about one week in three for four years and I have no idea how many
people I moved and I will not guess, and I have said that sentence in a
federal building three times and I'll say it in a courtroom on the
nineteenth." He swallowed. "So no. I don't remember you. I remember
the *route.*"

Mira Dalca started to laugh again and this time stopped it.

"That's the family disease," she said. "You know that? That's the exact
thing. You learn the route so you don't have to learn the person."

"I know."

"That's what I do."

"I know," Adam said. "That's what I've been sitting in the passenger seat
watching for eleven days."

·

"There's one more thing and then I've given you all of it," Adam said.

"Give it."

"I've been trying to work out for four days whether to tell you, and the
reason I couldn't decide is that it's not information, it's just true."
He had his hands in his coat pockets and his eyes on the water. "The
Constanța line was profitable. That's it. That's the sentence.

"It was the best line in the yard. Better than the electronics, better
than the cigarettes. And the reason it was better is that a container
costs the same whatever's in it, and the freight paid twice — once at the
front from whoever put them in the box, and once at the back for eleven
years afterward, out of the work." He shook his head slowly. "I ran that
board. I knew the second number was there. I did not know what it *was*,
and I want to be exact, I never saw a repayment schedule and I never
handled a dollar of it, but I knew there was a back end and I never once
asked what the back end was made of."

Mira stood with her hands on the rail.

"It was made of me," she said.

"It was made of you."

"Fourteen years of it."

"Yes."

She looked out at the eleven lights.

"I want to say a thing and I'd like you to take it exactly as it's meant,
which is not generously," she said. "You didn't do that to me. A company
did that to me, and you were a twenty-eight-year-old who ran a board for
it, and there is a real difference and I'm not going to pretend there
isn't because pretending would be doing you a favour and I'm not in the
mood.

"But you're the only one of them who has ever stood in front of me," Mira
said. "In fourteen years. The man who put me in the box, the man who
priced me, the man in Toronto, the man on Cermak, Ilie, the woman who
took the money off my mother in a kitchen in Constanța — six of them at
least, and not one has ever been in a room with me, and if I met any of
them tomorrow I'd walk past them in the street.

"So you're going to have to stand there," she said, "and be all of them
for a while, and that isn't fair either, and I don't care."

Adam Marek turned and faced the channel and put his hands on the rail
about a foot from hers.

"Alright," he said.

"That's it? *Alright*?"

"What else is there?" He did not look at her. "You're not asking me for
anything I've got a right to refuse. Stand there and be all of them.
Fine. I'll do that for as long as you need it and I won't put a clock on
it and I won't ask you how it's going, because a man who asks how it's
going is asking to be let off."

·

The fog began to go about six, in the way fog goes — not thinning so much
as deciding — and the breakwater came out of it in sections, and then the
harbour, and then the far shore, and out in the channel the eleven lights
turned out to be attached to a thousand-foot boat that had been three
quarters of a mile away the entire time.

They stood and watched it come clear.

"I want to say the thing I'm not allowed to say," Adam said.

"Say it."

"I'm sorry."

"I know."

"That's it," he said. "I'm not going to build anything on it. I've spent
seven years learning that the apology is the part that's for me." He put
his hands back in his pockets. "So: I'm sorry. It's the only true
sentence I've got and it's worth about eleven dollars."

Mira looked at the boat.

"You want to know the thing that's actually in my head," she said, "and
it isn't what you think, and I'm going to say it because if I don't say
it now I'll say it in nine days at the wrong time."

"Say it."

"It's not you." She said it slowly. "For fourteen years there has been a
man in my head who drove that truck. He's not a person, he's a — a shape.
He's a back of a head. And I have deliberately, on purpose, as a
*policy*, never once wondered about him, because a woman who starts
wondering about that has begun a job with no end in it."

The boat came the rest of the way clear.

"And now he's got a face," Mira said, "and he's been sitting in my
passenger seat for eleven days telling me not to apologize for things,
and I'm going to have to do something with that, and I don't know what it
is yet."

"Take the time," Adam said.

"I've got seven days."

"Then take seven days."

·

She did the arithmetic that afternoon, sitting at the table under the
window in the room over the chandlery, on a page of the road atlas, with
the boat now in and unloading at the plant across the harbour and a mate
who owed her from 2021 not answering his phone.

Adam sat on the end of the bed and watched her do it and said nothing for
forty minutes, because he had learned by now what it looked like when she
was working and had come to regard interrupting it as a species of
vandalism.

At the end of the forty minutes she said, "Right," and turned the atlas
around.

"That's four thousand two hundred dollars," she said.

Adam looked at the numbers on the page.

"Mira—"

"Four thousand two hundred is what I've got that's actually mine and
actually liquid and doesn't move through any account with my name on it.
It's in a coffee can in the truck, which is a stupid place, and I've been
adding to it since 2016 for a day I never specified." She tapped the
page. "Second thing. A name. Not a fake name — a fake name is how people
get caught, a fake name is amateur. A *real* one, belonging to a real
woman who died in Sarnia in 2019 and whose sister owes me and would do
it, which will hold for about three years if you're careful and don't try
to leave the country on it."

"Mira."

"Third thing." She turned the atlas ninety degrees. "The Coyle cut. It's
here. It's mine, nobody sold it to me, I found it in 2019 and I've used
it twice and I will never use it again after this, and it puts you across
at a place where there's no camera on either side and forty minutes of
nothing." She looked up. "And a woman in Thunder Bay who owes me nothing
at all and will take you in anyway, because I moved her sister-in-law in
2021 and she's been trying to give me money ever since and I've never
taken it."

Adam Marek looked at the road atlas.

"Twelve hours from now," Mira said, "you're a man nobody is looking for.
No strings. No invoice. I'd drive you to the cut tonight and put you
across before three in the morning and I would never see you again, and
that's not a threat, that's the offer — you'd be gone, properly gone, and
nobody on earth would have a claim on you including me."

She sat back.

"That's everything I have," she said. "That's fourteen years. That's the
whole tin."

·

The room was very quiet.

Down in the harbour a truck was backing up to the plant with its alarm
going.

Adam Marek sat on the end of a bed in a rented room and looked at a woman
who had just put the entire liquid contents of her life on a table for a
man she had known for eleven days, and he understood, with a completeness
that he would spend the next four days trying to describe to himself, that
this was the moment.

Not the breakwater. This.

Because on the breakwater she had given him a fact, and facts are cheap
between people who are frightened.

Here she had given him a *door*. She had built it, funded it, staffed it
at the other end, and set it in the wall of the room, and stepped back
from it.

He looked at it for a long time.

"If I go through that," he said, "the case dies."

"There'll be other cases."

"There won't." He said it without heat. "You know there won't. Raman's
had this severed calendar since January and the whole transport theory
runs on one witness who can say what the routes were for. The paper's
nothing without somebody to read it. I *am* the reading." He turned his
hands over. "If I disappear, then Gino Traversa runs freight on the south
side for another twenty years, and the road stays open, and next April
there's a nineteen-year-old in a box out of Constanța with a number
against future work."

"You'd be alive."

"I'd be a man in Thunder Bay with a dead woman's name," Adam said, "who
let a road stay open."

He pushed the atlas back across the table.

"I'm going to the nineteenth," he said.

Mira did not pick the atlas up.

"You understand what you're refusing."

"Yes."

"Say it, so I know you do."

"I'm refusing four thousand two hundred dollars, a real name, a route
nobody's sold you, and a woman in Thunder Bay," Adam said, "which is
everything you own, offered with no strings by the only person in this
country who has been straight with me, and I'm refusing it in order to
get into a truck and be driven toward a yard where two men have already
measured the distance to the water." He nodded once. "Yes. I understand
it. It's the first decision I've made since 2019 that I'd say out loud in
a room."

·

Mira Dalca sat for a while with her hands flat on the table.

Then she said, "Alright," and folded the road atlas closed, and something
in the room changed so completely that both of them felt it go.

Because the door was open.

That was the whole of it, and both of them knew it, and neither of them
said it for another eleven seconds.

He could leave. Not theoretically — actually, tonight, funded, with a
route and a name and a person at the other end. The floor of the room had
stopped being level in one direction and had come level, and everything
that had been standing on the slope had to be looked at again.

"I'm going to reopen last night," Mira said.

"Alright."

"The rule was: nothing happens while you can't leave." She kept her hands
flat. "You can leave. You've got a route, you've got money, you've got a
name and a person at the other end. That's not a theory. If you stood up
right now I'd drive you there tonight and I'd hold to every part of it,
and you know I would, and that's the only reason it counts."

"I know you would."

"So the rule's finished." She made herself say the next part looking at
him, which took more than the breakwater had. "Which means I'm asking.
Not for anything tonight. I'm asking for permission to want it out loud,
because I have been keeping it in a box for about six days and the box is
getting expensive."

Adam Marek sat very still on the end of the bed.

"You have it," he said.

"Say it properly."

"You have my permission to want it out loud," he said, "and you have it
in every room, and you can say it at a fuel stop or at four in the
morning on a breakwater or in a truck at eighty kilometres an hour, and
nothing about it obligates either of us to a single thing." He took a
breath. "And I'd like yours."

"You have it."

"Say it properly, Dalca."

"You have my permission," Mira said, "to want it out loud."

Neither of them moved.

It was, Adam thought afterward, the strangest and best evening of his
adult life: two people in a twelve-by-fifteen room over a ship's store
with a boat unloading across the harbour, having just granted each other
the right to say a thing, and then saying absolutely nothing, and going
to bed six feet apart at eleven o'clock, and both sleeping better than
they had in a week.

·

She lay awake for a long time that night with six feet of floorboards
between the bed and the runner, and did an inventory.

Not of the money, which was gone from the column now, and not of the
route, which he had refused. Of herself.

*Item.* A man had told her, on a breakwater, that he had driven the truck
that brought her into this country, and she had not felt one single thing
about it that she could name. Not rage. Not even the cold clean satisfied
click of a total coming out. Nothing.

She had turned that over for six hours and had eventually got at what it
was, and it was this: that the man in her head who drove that truck had
never had a face because she had never permitted him one, and a shape
with no face cannot be forgiven and cannot be blamed, and the moment it
acquired a face it stopped being the thing she had been not-thinking
about for fourteen years and became a forty-one-year-old man from
Archer Heights who laid a table.

She was not required to do anything about that tonight.

*Item.* She had offered him everything she owned and he had pushed it
back across a table, and the four thousand two hundred dollars was still
in a coffee can under the passenger seat, and it had gone strange in her
mind. It had been *for* something for ten years, unspecified, a
capacity. And it had turned out that when the day came she had spent it
in four minutes on a man she had known for eleven days, and had felt
nothing about the money at all.

Which meant either that it had never been for a rainy day, or that this
was the rainy day, and she could not make either reading behave.

*Item.* She had stood in a room and asked a man's permission to want
something out loud, and he had asked for hers, and nothing whatsoever had
happened afterward, and she had gone to sleep in her coat on a runner
feeling — she made herself find the word, in the dark, because a woman
who will not name a state cannot manage it — *safe.*

She had not had that word available since April 2012. It had not been in
the inventory. And it had arrived on the eleventh day of a delivery whose
return allowance was written for an empty truck.

The next day was a day of ordinary things.

The boat was in and would not go out for two days. The mate who owed her
from 2021 did not answer his phone, which she noted and did not yet
panic about. They did laundry in a machine behind the chandlery that took
quarters and ate two of them. Adam went out with Ruth's grandson and came
back with a belt for the truck, which he installed himself, badly, and
which Mira redid, and the argument about whether it had been the belt or

into the permanent record.

At four in the afternoon it rained.

At six she came up the outside stair with two boxes of food from a place
on the corner and found him standing at the window with his hands in his
pockets, watching the harbour.

She put the boxes on the table.

"You gave me permission to want it out loud," she said.

Adam turned around.

"I did."

"So: I want to kiss you."

"Then that's two of us," Adam Marek said.

She crossed the room.

She kissed him standing up, with the rain going on the roof slope over
their heads and the harbour doing its work below and two boxes of food
going cold on a table — her hand on his jaw, because that was where her
hand went, and his hands not coming up for a full second because he was a
man who had just been given something and was determined to receive it
properly.

Then they did come up, one at the back of her neck and one flat between
her shoulder blades, and she made a sound against his mouth that she had
not authorized, and eleven days of a truck cab went out of the room
entirely.

When they came apart she kept her hand where it was.

"For the record," Adam said, not very steadily, "that was you. Your
motion, your room, your terms, and a door standing open behind me that
you built and paid for. I'd like that minuted."

"Noted," Mira said.

"By whom?"

"By me," she said. "I'm the one who keeps the record," and kissed him
again.


## Chapter Ten — Waiting on Water

She sat with it.

The boat had been the plan since Chicago. Not a plan she had told him
about until the city limits, but the plan: the one leg of the whole
nineteen days that was hers, that came off her own list, that nobody in
the family had sold her or knew about. Four days on the water and off at
the Soo, and from the Soo everything opened.

And it was gone because a mate had taken a transfer to be nearer his
mother, which was the most ordinary sentence in the world.

That was how it always went. She had known that since she was
twenty-one. The routes never closed dramatically. They closed because
somebody's mother was ill, or a gate got a camera, or a foreman retired
and the new one kept the log properly.

"Right," she said, at eleven minutes. "Atlas."

·

They put the road atlas on the bed, because the table was too small, and
worked on it for four hours.

And it was, Mira thought later, the strangest four hours of the whole
nineteen days, because what happened in them was that she did her job in
front of another person.

She had never done that. Not once. Benny knew the yard and Benny knew
the trucks and Benny had known for eleven years that there were routes he

of love in both directions.

So the planning had always happened in her own head, at four in the
morning, alone, and had come out of her mouth afterward as an
instruction.

Now there was a man kneeling on the floor beside a bed with a road atlas
open on it, and he had run fourteen trucks for eleven years, and every
forty minutes he said something that made her stop and re-do a leg.

"You're routing around Bay City."

"There's a scale."

"There's a scale that's been closed since the state budget in 2023,"
Adam said. "I know, because we routed *toward* it for two years

it. That's forty minutes back in your pocket."

She looked at him.

"Say that again."

"It's closed. Or it was in November. I'd want to see it before I bet
anything on it."

"We'll see it."

arrives at the last minute has no options and a driver who arrives early

between this man and every other man she had ever worked with.

"Do it anyway."

window.

Forty minutes back in her pocket, on the word of a man who had run
freight for the people who were trying to kill him.

Leg nine was the second night, and they had the only real argument of the
four hours about it, which was that she wanted to be at Port Sanilac and
he wanted to be forty miles short of it.

hours in three days."

"I've done worse."

the atlas flat on the bed with his finger on Sanilac County. "Take the

"That's better," she said.

"I know it's better."

"Don't."

"I'm going to have it," Adam said. "I've been in this truck eleven days
and I've been right about a scale house and a belt and a tail, and this
is the first one that was about *you*, and I'm going to have it."

in the afternoon out of a sense that they should, and which neither of
them expected to use, and which was the one they used.

At the end of the four hours they had seventeen legs, five days, four
alternates, and a shape.

"There it is," she said.

"There it is." Adam sat back on the floor with his back against the bed
frame. "Mira. That's a good plan."

"It's an ordinary plan."

"It's a *good* plan, and I'd like you to let me say so, because I've
watched forty men build worse ones with more resources." He tipped his

"What's the best part of it."

"That it doesn't have a rescue in it," Adam said. "Every plan I've ever
seen a frightened man build has a moment in it where somebody arrives.
Yours doesn't. Yours is seventeen legs and a fuel schedule and a place to
put the truck at the other end."

Mira looked at the atlas.

"There's no rescue coming," she said.

"I know. That's why it's a good plan."

"You don't have to."

"Okay."

"Four hours if the bridge is clear. Four and a half if it isn't."

"Okay, Mira."

## Chapter Eleven — Berth

out at the turn. It's kept eleven people alive. It's also the reason
you've never had to sit through anybody's opinion about their own life.
And I've been on the receiving end of a man deciding what I could handle
— I was that man, for eleven years, for about forty drivers — and I know
exactly what it does."

"So the term is—"

"If you're going to decide something about my life," Adam said, "you say
it to my face first. Before. Not after, not on the way, not once it's
already turning. Before."

Mira Dalca sat at a table in a cabin outside Port Sanilac and looked at
that term for a long moment.

She knew, at the time, that it was the one she should argue about.

She could feel the shape of the objection: *there are situations where
there are eleven seconds and no conversation in them.* It was true. She
had been in four of them. It was the single most operationally correct
thing she could have said and she had it fully formed in her mouth.

And she did not say it, because saying it would have meant explaining
what those four situations had been, and because she wanted, more than
she wanted to be right, to be a person who could agree to a term like
that.

"Agreed," she said.

"Say it back."

"If I'm going to decide something about your life, I say it to your face
first."

"Thank you."

"Six," Mira said, "is that we're not doing anything tonight."

Adam blinked.

"That's — alright."

"Not because I don't want to." She said it evenly, looking straight at
him, and had the satisfaction of watching a forty-one-year-old man have
to look at the table. "Because I've just spent nine minutes agreeing to
terms and I'd like about four hours in which the agreement is the only
thing that's happened. I want to sleep in a room where the only thing
between us is a contract nobody's tested yet."

"That," Adam said, "is the most alarming and correct thing anybody has
ever said to me."

"Ratified?"

"Ratified," he said.

And they went to bed in two beds four feet apart in a cabin under maples
with the window open, and slept, both of them, for nine hours, which
neither had done in a year.

The next night was the fifteenth.

They spent the day badly — a scale that was closed exactly as he had said
it would be, a fuel stop, a hundred and eleven miles of nothing, and
underneath all of it a clock that had gone from being a date to being a
countable number of hours.

And at nine in the evening, in cabin six, she stood in the middle of the
room and said, "Still yes?"

"Still yes," Adam said. "You?"

"Still yes."

"Say it in the plain language."

"Everything I granted last night," Mira said, "on my schedule, and
tonight is on the schedule, and one word stops it at any point and I'd
rather you used it than spared me."

"Ratified," he said, and she crossed the room.

·

He was slower than she expected and more deliberate, and she understood
about eleven minutes in that this was not technique. It was that Adam
Marek had spent seven years believing he was a man who had driven a
truck for six hours with a person in the back, and had arrived at a
policy about how to touch another human being, and the policy was: *ask,
and then wait for the answer, and do not move until it arrives.*

So he asked. Out loud, in words, in a room in a cabin court in Michigan,
and waited.

And Mira Dalca, who had spent fourteen years being freight, discovered
that she had no idea how to answer, because nobody had ever waited long
enough for the question to require one.

The first time she went still he stopped.

Stopped completely — propped over her, breathing hard, absolutely
motionless — and said, "Where'd you go?"

Not *what's wrong*. Not *sorry*. *Where'd you go*, in the tone of a man
asking a colleague about a load.

"I'm here."

"You went somewhere."

"I went — " She had her hand flat on his chest and could feel his heart
going like something running. "I went to the ceiling. I do that."

"Come back or say stop. Both are fine and I mean that exactly as much as
it sounds."

"Come back," she said. "Give me eleven seconds."

He gave her eleven seconds. He gave her, in fact, about forty, with his
forehead against her temple and neither of them moving at all, and it was
in those forty seconds — not in anything that came afterward, though what
came afterward was considerable — that something in Mira Dalca that had
been welded shut since a container ship in 2012 came loose with a
sensation like a strap letting go.

"Here," she said. "I'm here. More."

"Say what."

"*More*, Marek, God—"

And he laughed against her throat, and she said it again, out loud, in
her own voice, in a room, stating a thing she wanted at a volume another
person could hear — and then said the next thing, and the next, until she
had said more true sentences about her own body in twenty minutes than
she had in fourteen years.

Afterward she lay with her ear on his sternum and listened to his heart
come down and thought, with the flat clarity that arrives at such
moments: *that is the whole thing that has been wrong with my life, and
it took a man asking a question and then shutting up.*

·

They traded scars, because there was nothing else to do and both of them
were awake.

Her hands: cargo straps, mostly, a decade of them, the little white
crescent between thumb and forefinger from a ratchet that let go in 2017
and had needed nine stitches from a man in Hammond who did not ask
questions and charged three hundred dollars.

The one on her shin, four inches, from a container door in Toronto in
2012, which she gave him flatly and without decoration and watched him
receive without a single word of apology, which she appreciated more
than she could have explained.

His hands, which were a driver's hands and had never once been in a
fight.

His arms, which stopped at the wrist — a driver's tan, permanent, eleven
years of a left elbow in a window, the line so sharp you could have ruled
against it.

"That's the stupidest thing I've ever seen," Mira said, in the dark,
running her thumb across it.

"It's professional."

"It's a *tan line*, Marek."

"It's eleven years," Adam said. "It's the only thing I've got on my body
that's from the job, and for about four years I've hated it, and I'm
lying here at eleven o'clock at night deciding I don't have to."

She was quiet.

"Say the rest of that."

"The rest of it is that I've spent seven years thinking the only thing I
ever made was freight," he said. "That's the sentence. That's the one
that's been in my head since 2019 — that I got to thirty-four years old
and the sum total of what I'd produced was other people's cargo going
from one place to another place, and one of it was a person, and that's
it, that's the whole ledger." He put his hand over hers on his wrist.
"And then a woman told me a thing on a breakwater and I've been doing
arithmetic ever since."

"What arithmetic?"

"That I routed you," Adam said. "In 2012. And that you got out, and built
eleven acres, and moved eleven people the family meant to put in the
water."

Mira went very still against him.

"That's not—"

"I know it's not," he said. "I know exactly what it isn't, and I'm not
claiming one inch of it. It isn't mine and it doesn't lower my number by
a dollar." His voice was steady. "But it's *true*, Mira, and it's been
true for fourteen years, and nobody on earth knows it but the two of us,
and I've been lying here trying to decide what to do with a fact that's
true and doesn't help."

She thought about it.

"You don't do anything with it," she said. "You carry it."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the accurate one," Mira said, and felt him laugh under her ear.

His shoulder, which had a white seam across the top of it about two
inches long.

"Loading dock?"

"Wall," Adam said. "1991. I was six and my brother was nine and he was
teaching me to slide on a wet floor."

"You've got a brother."

"I had a brother." He said it without any weight on it at all, which was
how she knew. "Tomasz. He went in 2011. It's not a story with anything in
it — he came off a roof in Berwyn doing a job for a man who didn't have
insurance, and he was twenty-nine, and that's the whole of it."

Mira lay with her hand flat on the seam.

"2011," she said.

"Yeah."

"And you went to work for them in 2008."

"I went to work for them in 2008," Adam said, "because my mother had a
mortgage and no husband and one son left, and my uncle had a friend who
ran a yard in Melrose Park, and I want to be extremely clear that I have
never once believed that's an excuse. It's a *sequence*. That's all it
is. It's a set of true things arranged into a shape and I've had seven
years to learn the difference."

"Say the difference."

"An excuse is a thing you offer somebody else," he said. "A sequence is a
thing you look at on your own, at four in the morning, so you can work
out where in it you could have turned. I know where I could have turned.
It was November 2019 and it was a knock in three at the back of a
trailer, and I did not turn, and I turned eight days later, and the eight
days are mine."

The maples did what maples do.

"That's the best thing anybody's ever said in front of me about a life,"
Mira said, eventually.

"It took me seven years and a woman in a truck telling me not to
offload."

"Don't do that either."

"No," Adam agreed. "That's fair."

·

She woke at four, because she woke at four, and this time she was not
alone in it.

He was awake. She could tell without looking: his breathing was the awake
kind, and he had one arm behind his head.

"How long have you been up?"

"About an hour."

"Why didn't you—"

"Because you were asleep," Adam said, "and it's the only time I've seen
you do it that wasn't in a driver's seat."

Mira sat up in the dark and put her back against the headboard and pulled
the blanket up, and sat for a while.

"I'm going to tell you the thing," she said.

"Which thing?"

"The one in the coffee tin."

He did not move or say anything, which was correct.

"There's a plank in the floor of my dispatch shed," Mira said. "North-east
corner. It's been soft since about 2014 and I've never fixed it, and
under it there's a coffee tin that used to have Maxwell House in it, and
in the tin there's a piece of paper folded twice with eleven names on
it."

The room was very dark. Outside, the maples did what maples do.

"Eleven people," she said. "Since 2019. The family moves people — that's
the business, that's been the business since before I was born, and most
of them arrive. They arrive frightened and priced and they spend fourteen
years paying it off, and that's the ordinary product, and I was one unit
of it."

"And the ones who don't arrive."

"There's no mystery about which ones. Somebody who can identify a person.
Somebody whose family stopped paying and whose being alive has become an
argument. Somebody who was quiet in the wrong yard." She kept her voice
absolutely flat, which was the only way the sentences would come out at
all. "And when they've got one of those, they don't use their own people.
They put it out to a carrier, because a carrier's deniable and a
carrier's yard isn't the family's yard and the carrier's name is the last
one on the paper."

"They put eleven out to you."

"They put eleven out to me," Mira said. "And eleven times I made the
delivery somewhere else."

Adam Marek did not move at all.

"A woman and a nine-year-old boy in 2019," she said. "Kosovar. That's the
first one, and I want you to understand that I didn't decide anything —
there was no decision, I want to be accurate, I had a Kosovar woman and a
boy in the box and thirty-five minutes and a car I couldn't lose and I
turned off onto a gravel entrance because I'd seen a haul truck come out
of it that morning." Her hands were flat on the blanket. "That's the
Coyle cut. That's where it came from. I found it that day, at speed, with
a child in the floor and no idea where it came out."

"Jesus."

"Two brothers from Bijeljina in 2021. Toledo to Fort Erie." She said it
in order, because that was how the list was written. "A man from Ho Chi
Minh City in 2022, and I never got his name and I've never forgiven
myself for it, and he was in the floor for six hours and cried for four
of them and got out at the other end in Thunder Bay and said thank you in
a language I don't have. Eleven of them. Last October was the eleventh."

Adam sat up.

"The false floor," he said. "The vent and the bottle holder and the—"

"Yes."

"The reading light."

"Yes," Mira said.

And in the dark of a cabin in Port Sanilac, Adam Marek put his hands over
his face for a second, which she felt rather than saw.

"I found it in Standish," he said, from behind his hands. "Eight days ago.
I put the plate back and I never said a word."

"I know you did. I knew inside ninety seconds."

"Why didn't—"

"Because you put it back properly," Mira said, "and because you closed
the tool box in the right order, and because a man who has just found a
hiding place with a reading light in it and says nothing about it for
eight days is a man who has understood something, and I did not want to
find out I was wrong."

·

"Say the last part," Adam said, later. "There's a last part."

"There's a last part."

She had got up and was standing at the window in his shirt with her arms
folded, looking out at nothing, which was where she had ended up without
deciding to.

"If Gino ever finds that tin," Mira said, "then eleven people who have
jobs and apartments and kids in schools get found in the same week. Every
one of them. Because it's not a list of names, it's a list of names and
dates and *routes*, and a route tells you where somebody went, and eleven
of those routes end at a person who took them in."

"Then why in God's name do you—"

"Because if nobody writes it down it didn't happen," Mira said.

The room was quiet.

"That's the whole reason," she said. "I've had six years of Benny saying
burn it, and I've had every good argument, and there isn't one that
touches it. I have spent fourteen years being a thing that got moved from
one place to another place, and there is one piece of paper in the world,
in my own handwriting, that says this life produced eleven things and
here they are, itemized." She did not turn around. "That's not sentiment.
It's the opposite of sentiment. It's a document."

She heard him get up.

He did not come and put his arms around her, which she had braced for.

He came and stood beside her at the window, facing the same direction,
about a foot away, with his hands in his pockets — the geometry they had
found on a breakwater and had used ever since for everything that
mattered.

"That's what's actually in the truck," Adam said.

"Yes."

"Not me."

"You're a hundred and ninety pounds and four percent of a fuel
allowance," Mira said. "That paper is eleven people. So no. Not you."

"Okay," Adam said.

And that was all he said, for a long time, standing at a window in the
dark, which was the correct thing, and which was why she was able, four
minutes later, to say the last part out loud without her voice going.

"And I'm going to have to spend it," she said. "Aren't I."

"I don't know."

"Yes you do."

"Yes," Adam said. "I think you are. I think that's what the seventeenth
is."

·

There was an hour on the morning of the sixteenth, between about half past
six and half past seven, that Mira Dalca would spend the rest of her life
returning to, and which contained nothing whatsoever.

They had breakfast at a table in cabin six with the door open onto the
maples. He made eggs badly. She made coffee and put four sugars in hers
and he said nothing at all about it, which she registered as a
concession and a small defeat on his part and enjoyed enormously.

They talked about a dog.

That was the whole of it. There was a dog at the cabin court, a
disreputable brown animal of no discernible breed belonging to the woman
in the office, and it had come and sat in their doorway on the fifteenth
and had come back on the sixteenth, and they had a long and detailed
conversation about what it was and where it had come from and whether it
was being fed by everyone in all eight cabins simultaneously, which it
was.

Adam thought it was some part collie. Mira thought this was sentimental
nonsense and said so at length.

They did not discuss the seventeenth. They did not discuss the tin or the
licence or the yard or the four men who would be standing in gravel in
thirty-eight hours. Adam Marek had put a term on the table two nights
earlier — *the job stays outside the door after dark* — and had then
quietly extended it, without asking and without mentioning it, to
breakfast.

At about seven he said, "You've got a face on."

"I'm thinking."

"About what?"

"About the fact that I've had eleven days of the most frightening
fortnight of my life," Mira said, "and I've just spent forty minutes
arguing about a dog, and I can't remember the last time I did that with
anybody."

"When was the last time?"

She thought about it, properly, the way she did everything.

"2016," she said. "Ted. In the shop. About whether a man in Hegewisch had
overcharged us for a windscreen."

"That's ten years."

"That's ten years," Mira agreed, and drank her coffee, and out in the
doorway the dog turned around twice and lay down.

Traversa called at eleven the next morning, from a turnout north of
Lexington, with the lake flat and blue on their left and eighty-one miles
left to the border yard.

She saw the number and put it on speaker before she answered, and set the
phone on the dash, and looked at Adam once, and Adam nodded.

"Mira."

"Gino."

"Where are you?"

"North of Lexington. I'll be in position tomorrow with a day in hand."

"That's good." He sounded like a man in a kitchen. Somewhere behind him a

early, she's always early, it's the thing about her."

There was a pause with a shape to it, and Mira Dalca, who had spent
eleven years learning the dialect of this family the way other people
learn scales, knew exactly what was coming and had about one second to
decide whether she wanted a witness to it.

She let it come.

"So here's the seventeenth," Traversa said. "You bring him into the yard
at eight in the evening. The gate'll be open, you drive in, you don't get
out of the truck until somebody comes to the window. They'll take him off

on the eighteenth and the passage is finished."

She heard herself say it and could not have said afterward whether she
had decided to.

refrigerator cycle in whatever kitchen he was standing in, eight hundred
miles away.

Two words. In fourteen years he had never once given her two words.

"I want to hear it," she said.

"No, you don't."

"I do."

"You don't, and here's why," Traversa said, and his voice was not
unkind — that was the part she would think about for the rest of her
life, that it was not unkind. "Because right now you're a driver who took
a load to a yard. That's what you are. That's what you'll be when it's
finished and that's what you'll be able to think about yourself at four

everybody knows that about you.

"And if I say the sentence out loud, then you're something else, and
you'll be that at four in the morning instead. So I'm not going to say

Mira Dalca sat in a truck in a turnout on the Lake Huron shore with both

The line went dead.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

Down on the shore a man was walking a dog along the water, throwing
something for it, and the dog was going into the lake after it in the way
dogs do, wholly, with no reservations at all.

Mira turned her head.

"That's it? *Okay*?"

"You heard it and I heard it," he said. "There's nothing for me to break

seven years I've been in a room where somebody said the true thing out
loud with me in it." He was very pale. "So: okay. Now we both know, and
we both know the other one knows, and that's — Mira, that's not nothing.
That's most of what I've wanted since 2019."

"No."

professional driving, and she knew it at the time, and she did it anyway.


## Chapter Twelve — The Twelfth Name

She called Benny from a payphone outside a hardware store in Lexington on
the sixteenth, because the burner had started making a sound she did not
like and because there are moments when a woman wants a telephone that is
bolted to a wall.

It rang eleven times.

She was about to hang up when the line opened and there was a room behind
it — not the shed, not the shop. A room with other people's conversations
in it and a woman's voice somewhere saying a number.

"Benny."

"Ah," he said. "Good."

"Where are you?"

"I'm in the hallway outside a hearing room in Springfield," Benny Osei
said, "which is not where I'm meant to be until the nineteenth, but they
moved the pre-hearing conference up to today, which I found out at nine
o'clock last night."

"Today's Saturday."

"Today is Saturday," Benny agreed, "and there is a hearing officer in
that room on a Saturday morning in May, and I would very much like you to
sit with that for a moment, because I have been sitting with it since
nine o'clock last night."

Mira put her free hand flat on the cold metal of the phone housing.

"They moved it up."

"They moved it up." A pause. "I've got a fellow here from a firm on
Wacker who I could not possibly afford and who told me at eight this
morning that his retainer's been taken care of, and when I asked by whom
he did the thing lawyers do with their faces."

"Benny—"

"I know. I know exactly what it is." He said it evenly. "Somebody's put a
very good lawyer in my corner so that I understand precisely how much
better my day could go if the right people are pleased with the road
situation. It's rather elegant, actually. Nobody's threatened me with
anything at all. They've just made my problem *solvable* and left it
lying there."

The hardware store's sign creaked in the wind off the lake.

"They took the file," Benny said.

"What?"

"The licence file. The physical one, from the yard. Wednesday. Two men
with a state letterhead that I checked and which was real, because that's
the other thing, Mira, everything they're doing is real — it's a real
review off a real discrepancy and it'll be a real hearing with a real
officer." A short breath. "That's what I keep — there's nothing to fight.
There's no lie in it anywhere. There's just a set of true things arranged
into a shape."

"The man in the shed," Mira said. "Wednesday. Has he been back?"

"He has not been back," Benny said, "and I am going to tell you the rest
of that and then I want you to say nothing for a moment, because you'll
want to say something and it won't help."

"Tell me."

"He sat in your chair," Benny said. "Not mine — yours, the one by the
board, and he sat in it the way a man sits in a chair he's decided
about. And he asked me about the trucks, and he asked me about the lanes,
and he asked how long I'd had the authority. All perfectly ordinary. And
then on the way out he stopped at the door and looked back at the floor
and said, 'What have you got under there, Mr. Osei?'"

Mira closed her eyes.

"The plank," she said.

"The plank."

"He knows."

"He doesn't know," Benny said. "If he knew he'd have taken it, and I'd
have been in a room with the state and the family both, and my licence
would be the least of anybody's difficulty. He was *fishing*. He fished
badly, because he's about thirty and he wanted me to see how clever he
was." A dry sound. "But he'll fish again with somebody older, Mira, and
that man won't ask, he'll just come at four in the morning with a
crowbar."

She stood at a payphone outside a hardware store and did the arithmetic,
which was very short.

"Take it out and burn it," she said. "Tonight. In the bin behind the
shop where we do the rags. Every page. Then go to your sister's in
Evanston and don't sleep at the house."

"No," Benny Osei said.

Mira opened her eyes.

"What?"

"No," he said again. "Not the second part — I'll go to Adaeze's, I'm not
a fool and there's a car at the top of my street. The first part. No."

In eleven years he had disagreed with her four hundred times, always
while already doing the thing. He had never said the word.

"Benny, if that tin—"

"I know what's in the tin," he said.

The line hummed.

"I've known since about 2020," Benny said, "and you have never once asked
me whether I knew, and I want you to notice that, because I've been
noticing it for six years."

"Benny—"

"I know that there are eleven names in it and I know approximately what
they are, because I am not a stupid man and I have been running a yard
for you for eleven years and I have watched you come back from four runs
in seven years looking like a woman who had done something." His voice
did not change at all. "And every year twice I have told you to burn it,
and I want to tell you why I'm not telling you today."

"Because it's evidence—"

"It is not evidence and you will never spend it," Benny said, "and if you
tried I'd knock you down myself, and we both know that isn't the question.

"I'm not telling you to burn it because one of these days somebody is
going to ask you what your life has been for."

The wind came along the storefronts.

"And you are going to want that list in your hand when you answer,"
Benny said, "because I have known you for eleven years and you have never
once, not one single time, been able to say a true thing about yourself
out loud without a document in front of you."

Mira Dalca stood at a payphone in Lexington, Michigan, and could not
speak.

"There," Benny said. "That's the whole of it and I've been wanting to say
it since about 2021." A door opened somewhere behind him and a voice said
a name that was not his. "I'm going in. It'll be twenty minutes and it'll
be nothing — it's a scheduling conference, they'll set the date they've
already set."

"Benny."

"Twelve's got a noise," he said. "Call me Thursday," and was gone.

·

She did not go back to the truck for eleven minutes.

She stood in the wind at the end of a block in a lake town of nine
hundred people with her hands in her pockets and looked at nothing, and
what went through her head was not Benny and was not the hearing.

It was a sentence.

*Somebody is going to ask you what your life has been for.*

·

They ran south down the shore that afternoon with the lake on the left
and neither of them said very much.

At about two she said, "Ask me about the tin," and Adam said, "No," and
she drove for four miles and then said, "Why not," and he said:

"Because you've spent eleven days deciding what to hand me and you've
never once got it wrong, and I'm not going to start taking things off you
now."

"That's not an answer, that's a policy."

Because the arithmetic would not close.

She had been staring at it for perhaps ninety seconds before it arrived,

*2012. Constanța. Nineteen years old. A container to Toronto and a truck

*12. M.D., 19, Constanța. Apr 2012. Toronto–Chicago. Alive.*

## Chapter Thirteen — Delivered

They saw the yard for the first time at eleven in the morning, from the
service road on the bluff above the highway, with eleven hours to go.

It sat on made ground between the interstate approach to the bridge and
the river: eleven acres of gravel and container stacks behind an
eight-foot fence, a scale house, a long steel shed, and a slip on the
north side where a barge had been tied up so long that there were weeds
growing out of the cleats.

Beyond the fence, the water.

Not lake water. This was the river now, the whole outflow of three
inland seas coming down through a gap eight hundred yards wide with a
current that ran at three knots on a quiet day and pulled a
thousand-foot carrier down it every forty minutes.

And past the slip, the seam.

Mira saw it and stopped the truck on the shoulder without meaning to,
because it was the same seam. The long grey line where the sheltered
water met the channel and did not mix with it — the thing she had stood
and looked at on a breakwater in Alpena six days ago while a man told her
he had driven her out of a container in 2012.

Eleven acres. A fence. And the whole of the Great Lakes going past the
end of it at three knots.

"Say it," she said.

Adam had the window down and was looking at the yard the way a man looks
at a page of accounts.

"One gate," he said.

"That's what I get."

"That's what there is." He pointed with two fingers, not raising his
hand above the sill. "Fence on the highway side, fence on the north
side, and both of those are just fence — you could put a truck through
either one if you didn't mind what it did to the truck. The east side is
the slip. The south side is the river."

"So."

"So one in," Adam said. "Everything else is the river."

They sat on the shoulder for a while with the engine ticking.

"How many will there be?"

"Tonight? Four." He said it without hesitating, which told her he had
been working on it for days. "Two at the gate, because the gate's the
only thing that matters, and one in the scale house because somebody's
got to sign the receipt, and one more you won't see until you're inside,
who'll be at the corner of the shed where he can see both the gate and
the truck."

"And him?"

"Gino Traversa will be in the scale house," Adam said, "because it's the
only building with a chair in it, and because a road boss doesn't stand
in gravel at night at fifty-five with two bullets in him."

They spent two hours in a diner across the highway and neither of them
ate.

It was a good diner. That was one of the small cruelties of the day: a
1962 diner with a counter and eleven stools and a woman named Deb who
called them both honey and brought coffee without being asked, and a
window with the whole yard in it across four lanes of traffic.

Mira drew it on a napkin.

She drew the fence line and the gate and the scale house and the shed and
the stacks, and where the light poles were and which ones had lamps on
them and which had lamps that were out, and she did it from a distance of
four hundred yards and from memory of forty other yards, and Adam
watched her produce, in about eleven minutes, a site plan he would have
put in front of a federal grand jury.

"There," she said.

"That's the yard."

"That's the yard."

He turned the napkin around.

"Where's the truck at eight o'clock?"

She put an X.

"Inside the gate, four lengths in, nose toward the shed," she said. "It's
where they'll want it, because it's out of the sightline from the
highway, and it's where I'd want it too because it's the only place where
I can see both the gate and the scale-house door."

"And where's the water?"

Mira put her finger on the napkin at the eastern edge.

"Sixty yards from the X," she said.

Adam Marek sat back in a vinyl booth in a diner in Port Huron, Michigan,
and looked at a napkin.

"Say the thing you're not saying," he said.

Mira Dalca picked up her cold coffee and put it down again.

"There's nothing I'm not saying."

"Mira."

"I'm working," she said. "That's what this is. I've got eleven hours and
I'm working."

Adam looked at her for a moment longer.

He let it go.

That was the decision — his, not hers, made at eleven minutes past one in
the afternoon in a diner booth — and he would think about it for the rest
of his life, because he had a term, and the term was *you don't route
me*, and he could feel her doing it across the table and he did not
force it.

He told himself she would get there. She got everywhere four days before
he did.

He told himself he had already used his one hard sentence on a
breakwater, and that a man does not get two.

Both of those were true. Neither of them was the reason.

The reason was that he was frightened, and that in eleven days he had
learned to feel safer when Mira Dalca was working than when she was
talking to him, and he wanted her to work.

·

At two o'clock she went out to the truck and did the pre-trip.

She did the full one, the DOT one, the one she had not done properly in
four years because nobody does it properly, and it took her forty
minutes: lights, brakes, air, hoses, the fifth wheel she did not have,
the tires all round with a bar, the fluid levels, the wipers, the
reflective triangles she had never once deployed in fourteen years.

Adam came out and stood at the end of the truck and watched her do about
half of it before he understood what he was watching, and then he went
back into the diner and sat in the booth and put both hands flat on the
table.

She was not checking the truck. There was nothing wrong with the truck.

She was saying goodbye to it.

He sat in a vinyl booth in Port Huron, Michigan, with a woman named Deb
refilling a coffee he had not touched, and thought about the fact that in
seventeen days he had learned to read Mira Dalca so well that he could
tell what she was doing from four hundred feet away through a plate-glass
window, and that he had not once, in seventeen days, used it.

He could go out there right now. He could stand at the front bumper and
say *you are doing the thing where you get everything straight because
you have decided something*, and she would look at him for four seconds
and then either tell him or not.

That was all it would take. One sentence, four hundred feet, eleven
seconds' walk.

He drank the coffee instead.

The phones started at four.

Three calls in forty minutes, and afterward she would remember them as
one continuous piece of weather.

"Scheduling conference went as expected. Date holds. Nineteenth, ten
a.m." A pause with a room behind it. "There are two men in the yard."

"In the yard? *Our* yard?"

with the door open." Benny's voice was steady in a way that told her
exactly how frightened he was. "Not doing anything. Standing. I've told
Ricky and Kowalczyk to finish their runs and go home and not come back
until I call."

"I'm leaving in eleven minutes," Benny said. "I want you to know that I
haven't touched the plank and I'm not going to, and if they take it up
they take it up, and that's my decision as well as yours because I'm the
one standing here."

"Twelve's got a noise," he said, and the line went.

She called him back four minutes later.

It rang out.

She called again at half past four and it rang out, and at ten to five,
and it rang out, and at five it went to a voicemail she had heard eleven
thousand times — *this is Osei, leave it after the tone and I'll get to
it* — and Mira Dalca stood in the parking lot of a diner in Port Huron
and understood, in an entirely physical way, that a part of her life had
been detached and was floating.

The second call came at twenty past five, from a number she had never
seen.

"Speaking."

"Gino wanted me to confirm the gate opens at eight." A young man's voice.
Careful. Reading. "He says to tell you he appreciates punctuality and
he's always appreciated it about you."

"Tell him I'll be there at eight."

"He also says—" a pause, and a rustle, and she realized with a small
cold shock that the boy was reading from something written down, "—that
after eight o'clock tonight nobody is going to bother Mr. Osei about
anything, and that these things have a way of resolving themselves when
everybody's been reasonable."

"Say that again."

"I'm—" He lost his place. "That's what it says."

"What's your name?"

The line went dead.

The third call came at ten to six, and she did not recognize the number,
and she did not answer it.

It was not the federal duty line. She had that in her coat, folded in
half, over her ribs, and she had looked at it eleven times in twelve
hours and she knew the digits the way she knew her own gate code.

It rang eleven times and stopped.

It did not ring again.

She stood in the lot beside her truck with the phone in her hand and the
yard four hundred yards away across the highway, and did the arithmetic.

She did it the way she did everything, in a column, out loud, under her
breath, in Romanian, which was the language arithmetic still happened in.

*If I run:*

Benny loses the licence on the nineteenth, certainly. The yard stops
rolling that day: eleven acres, forty-one trailers, nine trucks and
fourteen people's jobs, and Kowalczyk who is sixty-one and hates her and
would have died for her.

And then they go looking for what she has, because a woman who runs is a
woman with a reason, and the first place they go is a shed with a soft
plank in it, and eleven people who have apartments and jobs and children
in schools get found inside a week.

And Adam Marek is a man with no papers and no phone and no route in a
country where the only people who know his face have already measured a
distance to the water. Four days, maybe. A week.

*If I go in:*

He dies at eight o'clock.

That was the column. She ran it three times, standing in a parking lot,
and it came out the same three times, which is what a true total does.

There was no third line.

She had spent eleven years being the person who found the third line. It
was the entire content of her professional life — the sand road, the
Coyle cut, the four unlisted crossings, forty minutes and a false floor
and a way through where there was no way through — and she stood in a
parking lot in Port Huron on the seventeenth of May and could not find
one.

*Say it to his face first,* the term said.

And Mira Dalca thought, with total clarity: *say what? There is nothing
to say. There is no plan to disclose. There is a column with two lines
in it and both of them are bad, and telling him about it will not add a
line, it will only mean that he spends his last two hours knowing.*

She had never once in fourteen years told a passenger a thing that did
not improve their odds.

She got in the truck.

·

There is a thing she would say, once, four years later, in a room, to a
woman in Beverly on a Thursday evening, and would never say again.

She would say: *I want to be clear that I did not go into that yard as a
sacrifice. I've heard four people describe it that way and every one of
them has been wrong and every one of them has meant it kindly.*

*I went in because the column said go in.*

*That's the part nobody wants. There was a total and it said drive and I
looked at the total and I said no, and then I made a new column with a
different value in one line, and the new column said go in, and I went in
because it said so. That is what I am. I don't do things because they are
right. I do arithmetic and then I do what it says, and the only thing
that changed on the seventeenth of May was that for the first time in
fourteen years I let myself put a real number in the line that had him
in it.*

*And the reason that's worth saying out loud in this room is that
everybody has been telling me for four years that I found my courage in a
parking lot in Port Huron, and I didn't. I found an error in my own rate
card.*

·

She did four things in the eleven minutes before she got in the truck,
and she did them in a fixed order, and she was aware while doing them
that she was making a will.

One. She took the four thousand two hundred dollars out of the coffee can
under the passenger seat and put it in the glovebox in an envelope with
BENNY OSEI written on the front, because a glovebox is the first place
anybody looks and she wanted it found.

Two. She wrote the gate code for the sand road on the back of a fuel
receipt and burned it, which she understood at the time to be pure
sentiment, because there was nobody left to give it to.

Three. She moved her mirrors.

Not far. About a quarter of an inch, both of them, down and out — to the
positions a man six foot two would want, which she had watched him set on
a straight bit of US-23 north of Tawas and which he had put back while
driving at seventy-two miles an hour in the dark so that she would not
have to reach up when she woke.

She did not have a coherent reason. She sat in the driver's seat afterward
looking at a mirror she could not properly see out of and did not put it
back, and it stayed like that for four days, through the yard and the
gravel and a federal impound lot, and when she got the truck back on the
twenty-first of May it was the first thing she noticed and the only time
in the whole business that she had to stop and sit down.

Four. She went back inside.

Deb was closing up and let her have the end of the counter anyway, and
refilled a coffee twice without being asked, and Mira Dalca sat on a stool
in a 1962 diner in Port Huron with four paper placemats and a borrowed
pen and wrote out, in her own small columnar hand, four pages of
arithmetic about a debt from 2012.

It took eleven minutes. She did not know at the time why she was doing it,
and she said so afterward, every time anybody asked, and nobody ever
believed her.

They did not talk on the way over.

It was four hundred yards and one traffic light and it took ninety
seconds, and Adam Marek sat in the passenger seat where he had sat for
seventeen days with his hands on his knees.

At the light he said, "Mira."

"Yes."

"Whatever happens in there," he said, "you got me nineteen days. I was
supposed to have about four."

"Don't."

"I'm going to. It's the last flat surface." He was looking straight
ahead. "I've spent seven years believing the only thing I ever made was
freight. And then a woman in a truck told me to stop apologizing and gave
me a distance to a fuel stop, and I have been more myself in seventeen
days in that seat than I was in eleven years of running a board."

The light changed.

"I love you," Adam said. "You don't have to do a single thing about
that. It's not a claim. I just wanted it said in a place where it was
true and not useful."

Mira Dalca turned left across four lanes and took the approach road down
toward the gate.

She did not answer him.

She would think about that — the not answering — every day for a long
time.

exactly, and the gate rolled shut behind the truck.

She took it four lengths in and stopped, nose toward the shed, exactly
where the X was on a napkin in her pocket.

There were four men.

Two at the gate. One at the corner of the shed where he could see both
the gate and the truck. And one at the scale-house door.

The one at the scale-house door came over and stood at her window and
said, pleasantly, "Ms. Dalca. He's inside."

"I stay in the truck. That's what I was told."

"That was before." He stepped back. "He'd like you inside. It's the
paperwork."

The scale house was eleven feet by fourteen with a counter, two windows,
a space heater running on a cord, and a chair.

Gino Traversa was sitting in the chair.

It was the first time in eleven years that Mira Dalca had been in a room
with him.

That was the fact that arrived first, ahead of everything else, and it
arrived with a completeness that made the floor feel further away than it
was: eleven years of a lowered car window on Torrence Avenue, of standing
in the cold at the height of a seated man, of *he never comes inside* —
and here he was, in a building, with the door shut behind her.

She knew what it meant. She had known what it meant before the door
finished closing.

He never came inside, because inside is where you have to be seen and
remembered. He was inside now because after tonight it was not going to
matter who remembered.

"Mira," Traversa said. "Sit down."

"I'll stand."

"Suit yourself." He had a manifest on the counter and a pen. "How was the
road?"

"It was a road."

"It's a good road, that stretch. I did it myself in — Christ, '04?" He
turned the manifest around and squared it to the counter's edge. "Sign
at the bottom. That's your receipt for delivery. You get the yellow, I
keep the white."

Through the window she could see the truck.

They had opened the passenger door. Two of them, one on each side, and
Adam Marek got down out of the cab on his own feet without being pulled,
which she understood immediately was a decision he had made in advance,
and stood in the gravel.

He did not look at the men.

He looked at the scale-house window.

Mira picked up the pen.

"Your father used to say a thing," Traversa said conversationally,
watching her. "Not your father. Ted Wisniewski. He used to say the whole
business is that somebody carries and somebody gets carried and there's
no third category."

"He never said that."

"Well." Traversa smiled. "Somebody said it."

She signed.

She signed at the bottom of the manifest in her own hand, on the
seventeenth of May, at eleven minutes past eight in the evening, in a
scale house at a border yard, and she did not look up while she did it.

Outside, in the gravel, Adam Marek watched her not look up.

"You were carried," Traversa said, taking the pen back. "Now you've
carried. That's the whole business, Mira. Nobody gets to be the only one
who was ever helped."

He tore off the yellow copy and handed it to her.

"Drive safe," he said. "It's dark out."

·

She walked back to the truck across sixty feet of gravel.

Adam was eleven feet away, between two men, and as she came past he said
her name — once, not loudly, in an ordinary voice.

"Mira."

She did not stop.

She got up into the cab and started the engine and put it in gear, and
the gate rolled open, and she drove out onto the approach road with a
yellow copy of a delivery receipt on the passenger seat where a man had
been sitting for seventeen days.

In the mirror the gate rolled shut.

And Mira Dalca drove north up the approach road at forty miles an hour
with both hands on the wheel and her face doing nothing at all, and the
thing in her chest was not grief and was not fear.

It was the flat, familiar, professional feeling of a delivery completed.

That was the part that would take her the longest to survive.


## Chapter Fourteen — Two Miles

She got two miles.

Two miles north up the approach road with the yellow copy of the delivery
receipt on the passenger seat, and then the bridge came up ahead of her —
the whole blue span of it lit from underneath, going over into another
country — and Mira Dalca put on her indicator, which she noticed herself
doing, on an empty road, at twenty past eight at night, and pulled onto
the shoulder under the approach and stopped.

She did not turn the engine off.

That was the first thing she noticed about herself, sitting there: that
her hands had done all of it correctly. Indicator, mirror, shoulder,
handbrake, flashers. Fourteen years of doing the correct thing with her
hands while the rest of her was somewhere else, and here it was again,
working perfectly, at the worst hour of her life.

The diesel idled. The bridge traffic went over the top of her, four
lanes of it, a sound like water.

She sat with both hands on the wheel and did the arithmetic.

She did it because it was the only thing she had ever been able to do
when a thing was too large to look at directly. You put it in a column.
You put what it costs on the left and what it buys on the right, and you
add it, and whatever the total says, that is the answer, and you drive.

*Cost of going back in:* one truck, one woman, four men, sixty yards of
gravel, and a road boss with a manifest signed at eleven minutes past
eight.

*What it buys:* nothing. It does not buy one thing. She had run it three
times in a diner lot and it had come out at nothing three times, and a
total that comes out the same three times is a true total.

*Cost of driving on:* eleven hours to Chicago. A licence hearing on the
nineteenth. A shed with a soft plank in it and a coffee tin under the
plank. Eleven people with apartments and jobs and children in schools.

*What it buys:* all of them. Every one. Alive and unlooked-for and never
knowing there had been a night when somebody added them up.

The column was not close. It was not even interesting. It was the
clearest arithmetic she had done in fourteen years and it said, in the
plainest possible terms, *drive*.

Mira Dalca sat on the shoulder of the bridge approach with the engine
running and looked at the total and said, out loud, in an empty cab:

"No."

·

The word surprised her.

She heard it in the cab the way you hear something a stranger says in the
next booth, and she sat absolutely still with her hands on the wheel and
waited to find out what she had meant by it.

What came was not an argument. It was a sentence she had been saying her
whole adult life, arriving with its meaning showing.

*I don't lose freight.*

She had said it to Benny in 2015 when the Portage yard got a camera. She
had said it to a mate on a boat in 2021 and to a frightened woman in a
container in Melvindale in 2019, and she had said it four days ago in a
truck stop outside Bay City to a man with no papers, and every single
time she had meant it as a promise, and she heard now, at twenty-four
minutes past eight on the seventeenth of May, what it actually was.

It was the whole architecture, in five words.

*Freight.* Because freight is a thing you are responsible for and not a
thing you know. Freight does not ask you how you are. Freight does not
sit in your passenger seat for seventeen days and put your mirrors back
exactly where they were. Freight goes in the back, and you route it, and
you deliver it, and then it is gone and you are still the one driving,
which is the entire point, which is the only way she had found in
fourteen years to be near a human being and survive it.

She had been calling people freight since she was nineteen.

She had been doing it because she had been freight. Because a person who
has been priced and boxed and moved has exactly two positions available
to them afterward, and only one of them has a wheel in it, and she had
taken that one with both hands at twenty-one and never once let go.

And it worked. That was the thing nobody ever said about it. It had
worked for fourteen years. Eleven people were alive because of it.

Mira sat with the engine running and thought, with total clarity: *and
tonight the method delivered a man to a yard to be killed, on time, in
good order, with a signature on the receipt.*

Because that is what the method is for. The method does not know the
difference between a border and a grave. It knows the road and the clock
and the terms, and it had done all three of them beautifully.

The bridge traffic went over.

She looked at the passenger seat, where there was a yellow carbon copy of
a delivery receipt and nothing else, and where for seventeen days there
had been a man sitting with his hands on his knees who could not stop
counting exits.

"You're not freight," she said.

·

She took the folded paper out of the inside pocket of her coat, over her
ribs, where it had been for a day and a half.

Ten digits in a driver's blocky capitals, and under them a name.

She looked at it for a long time and understood that she was not
hesitating about whether to call. She had stopped hesitating about that
somewhere in the last ninety seconds, in a way that had happened below
the level she could watch.

What she was doing was something else. She was standing at the edge of
the actual thing, the one she had spent fourteen years building a yard
and a business and a personality on top of so that she would never have
to stand at the edge of it, and she was working out how to make her
mouth do it.

Because a call to that number is not a call about Adam Marek.

You cannot report a homicide in progress at a border yard and stop there.
The first question is *who is speaking*. The second question is *how do
you know*. And the true answer to the second question is fourteen years
long and has eleven names in it and starts in a container off Constanța
in April 2012 with a nineteen-year-old girl in it who was priced at
eleven thousand dollars against future work.

Every time she had imagined telling anybody any part of it — and she had
imagined it, at four in the morning, more often than she would ever
admit — she had run into the same wall, and the wall was this: *if I say
the first sentence, I have to say all of them.*

She sat on a shoulder under a bridge with a phone in her hand.

And what she thought was not *I have to*. What she thought, in her own
voice, in Romanian, which was the language that the true things had
always happened in:

*I have spent fourteen years making sure that no one would ever be able
to say what I am. And they were never going to. And it did not save one
single person tonight.*

She looked at her watch. Eight-thirty.

The gate had shut at eight. He had got down out of the cab at about ten
past. Traversa had handed her the yellow copy at eleven minutes past and
told her it was dark out.

Twenty minutes. Nineteen minutes.

She had absolutely no idea how long a thing like that took, and she
understood with a lurch that this was itself a piece of information about
her life — that she had spent eleven years in the freight arm of an
organization that killed people and had arranged her entire existence so
as to never once have to know the schedule.

Mira Dalca dialed the ten digits at 8:31 p.m.

It rang twice.

"Duty line, Northern District of Illinois."

A woman's voice. Awake. Not young.

Mira found that her mouth was open and nothing was coming out of it, and
that her hand on the wheel had gone white, and she did the only thing she
had ever been able to do in that condition, which was to start with a
number.

"There is a man being killed right now at a container yard on the water
south of the Blue Water Bridge in Port Huron, Michigan," she said. "It's
eleven acres, one gate, off the approach road on the east side. He has
been there for thirty-one minutes."

A change in the quality of the line. The particular silence of somebody
picking up a pen.

"Ma'am, I need—"

"His name is Adam Marek," Mira said. "He is a federal witness with a
testimony date on the nineteenth in front of an AUSA named Priya
Raman, and he was taken out of a protective house in Berwyn on the
first of May, and the reason nobody has found him in seventeen
days is that he has been in the passenger seat of my truck."

The pen stopped.

"Who am I speaking to?"

And there it was, the whole of it, one question wide.

Mira Dalca sat on the shoulder of the bridge approach with the flashers
going and the diesel idling and a yellow carbon copy on the seat beside
her.

"My name is Mira Dalca," she said. "I own a yard on Torrence Avenue in
Chicago. I want to report a homicide that is happening right now, and I
want to tell you who I am, and I am going to start in 2012."

·

It took twenty-two minutes.

She would not remember most of them in order. What she would remember,
for the rest of her life, was the flashers ticking, and the underside of
the bridge, and the extraordinary discovery that once you begin at the
beginning, the rest is only work.

The duty officer put her through inside ninety seconds. The second voice
was a man who said his name was Weiss and asked her to say the location
again, twice, and read it back to her both times. The third voice was a
woman.

"This is Priya Raman."

Mira had been braced for a hurry. There was no hurry in it at all. There
was a woman on the end of a telephone at twenty to nine on a Sunday night
who sounded exactly like a person who has been sitting beside a phone for
eleven months waiting for one specific kind of call and has already
decided not to frighten it.

"Ms. Dalca. Weiss has the yard. There are people moving. Keep talking to
me."

"How long."

"Not yet. Keep talking to me."

So she talked.

She gave the yard first, because the yard was the only part that was
urgent: the fence lines and the gate and the scale house and the shed and
where the light poles were and which lamps were out, all of it off a
napkin she had drawn four hundred yards away in a diner, and she heard
Raman relaying it in pieces to somebody in the room with her, and heard
that somebody say, distinctly, *she's got the light poles*.

She gave the four men and where they stood.

She gave the truck, four lengths in, nose to the shed, and the slip on
the north side, and the sixty yards from the X to the water, and said,
"That's why it's that yard. That's the only reason it's that yard," and
Raman said, "Yes. We know," and did not say anything else about it, which
Mira was grateful for in a way she could not have explained.

And then the yard was given, and there was nothing left that was urgent,
and Priya Raman said:

"Now tell me the rest."

·

Here is the part Mira Dalca would think about for a long time afterward.

She had assumed — for fourteen years, without ever once examining it —
that the government wanted the eleven.

It was the load-bearing assumption of her whole life. It was why the tin
was under a plank. It was why she had never gone to a lawyer, never gone
to a priest, never in fourteen years said one true sentence to anybody
who could write it down. Because the eleven were the only thing she had
that was worth anything, and the moment she opened her mouth they would
be taken off her and spent.

And it turned out to be true. Raman asked for them at minute nine.

"You said eleven people."

"I did."

"I want to be straightforward with you, because you've been straightforward
with me." No pressure in it whatsoever, which was worse. "The eleven
passages are the corroboration. Your word about Traversa is one woman's
word. Eleven documented movements, with dates and yards and routes, in
your hand, is a pattern, and a pattern is a case. I'd want their names."

Mira looked at the bridge.

"No," she said.

"Ms. Dalca—"

"I want to say this properly, because I've only got one go at it." Her
voice was doing something strange. It was completely steady. "Those
eleven people did not agree to be moved. They agreed to *live*, and I
gave them a route, and that's not the same thing, and I have known it
isn't the same thing since about the third one. Every single one of them
got in the back of a vehicle because there was no other door. That's not
consent. That's arithmetic."

"I understand—"

"You don't yet, but you will in a second." She put her palm flat on the
wheel. "They didn't agree to be moved. And they have not agreed to be
evidence. And I have already done the first one to those people without
asking. I'm not doing the second one."

Silence on the line.

"Then what are you giving me?"

And Mira Dalca, on a shoulder, under a bridge, at eleven minutes to nine
in the evening, gave the only answer she had.

"The roads," she said. "Every route the transport arm has run out of
Chicago since 2015 — the lanes, the yards, the crossings, the four
unlisted ones, and who keeps which gate. And eleven years of Gino
Traversa's manifests, which are all in my yard, in my hand, because I've
been reconciling them for him since the year Moretti went to trial, and
there is four hundred and six thousand dollars of freight on them that
never went on the family's books.

"And me," she said.

"Meaning what, specifically."

"Meaning I'm the twelfth," Mira said. "April 2012, Constanța to Toronto
in a container and Toronto to Chicago in a truck, nineteen years old,
priced at eleven thousand dollars against future work by a man I never
met, and every single day since then I have paid it in road. That's not a
witness statement, that's a confession, and I'll sign it. I know exactly
what it does to my licence and my yard and I've done the column.

"I'm not giving you the people," she said. "I'm giving you the roads and
I'm giving you me. If that's not enough, hang up and I'll go back in
alone and you can read about it."

The flashers ticked.

"Ms. Dalca," Priya Raman said, "it's enough."

·

It was enough. It was also forty minutes from enough, which is a
different problem entirely.

"Talk to me about your team," Mira said, and heard her own voice go
completely flat and professional, which was a relief so enormous it was
almost physical.

"Port Huron is not Chicago." Raman did not soften it. "We have two
agents in Detroit who are moving now and a resident agency contact and
the state police, who are good, and who are also being asked at ten to
nine on a Sunday night to go through a locked commercial gate on the
word of a woman on a telephone. There is a process for that. The process
is not slow, but it is not nothing."

"Give me the number."

"Best case, a vehicle at that gate at nine thirty-six."

Mira Dalca looked at the dashboard clock and did the only arithmetic she
had ever been genuinely gifted at.

Eight fifty-two.

Forty-four minutes.

And a man who had got down out of a cab on his own feet at ten past
eight, thirty-eight minutes ago, in a yard with sixty yards between the
gravel and the water.

"That's too long," she said.

"I know it's too long."

"He doesn't have forty-four minutes. He didn't have twenty."

"Ms. Dalca." And here at last there was something in Priya Raman's voice
that was not calm. "I am not going to lie to you about this. If it has
already happened, it has already happened, and nothing you or I do in the
next forty minutes changes it. If it has not — and there is a real reason
it may not have, which is that a yard like that is a place where things
are done at a particular hour for particular reasons and eight o'clock is
early — then the only thing on this earth that matters is whether
somebody is still standing in that gravel at nine thirty-six."

"Forty minutes," Mira said.

"Forty minutes. Can you give us forty minutes?"

And Mira Dalca — who had spent fourteen years in the business of
producing exactly one commodity, which was time, in hostile yards, on
other people's clocks, for people who had nothing else — sat up straight
in her seat and felt something arrive in her that she recognized, with a
kind of horror, as *pleasure*.

"Yes," she said.

"How?"

"By going back in and arguing about a bill."

"Ms. Dalca, I am obliged to tell you—"

"Tell me while I'm driving," Mira said, and put it in gear.

·

·

Priya Raman talked to her for the whole two miles, which Mira understood
perfectly well was a technique and did not mind in the least.

"I'm obliged to tell you that we are not asking you to re-enter that
property and that we are advising you against it, and I'd like that on
the recording."

"It's on the recording."

"And now I'd like to say the other thing." A change in the line — a door,
a room with more people in it. "Ms. Dalca, do you have any training of
any kind?"

"I've got fourteen years of yards."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the only answer I've got and it's a better one than you think,"
Mira said. She had the phone against her shoulder and both hands on the
wheel. "I have stood at scale houses in four states and produced time out
of nothing in front of men whose entire job was not giving me any, and I
have never once been wrong about what a yard was going to do next, and
tonight I have four pages of true arithmetic in my coat about a man who
has never in forty years walked away from a wrong number."

"Give me the shape of it."

"He'll let me in because I'll give him a reason that flatters him, and
he'll listen because I'm going to be right about a figure he can check.
That's it. That's the whole plan, and it's got about forty minutes in
it, which is exactly what you asked for."

"And when the forty minutes are up?"

"Then I'll be standing in a scale house with a man who's worked out he's
been held," Mira said, "and I'd very much like your people to be at that
gate."

There was a pause with a room behind it.

"What do you need from us?"

"Two things. One, nobody comes down that approach road with a light bar
going until they're inside four hundred yards, because that yard's got
one gate and a slip on the east side and sixty yards of gravel in
between, and a man who sees a light bar at a mile has time to make a
decision.

"Two." She checked the mirror out of pure habit. "There's a container
office on the north side with the light on. That's where he is. The
first thing through that gate goes to the office and not to the scale
house, because I'm the one in the scale house and I'll keep. He won't."

Another pause.

"Ms. Dalca," Raman said, "how long have you been doing this?"

"Doing what?"

"Producing time for other people."

Mira Dalca drove down an approach road toward a gate.

"Since I was twenty-three," she said.

·

She did not go in fast.

That was the whole thing. Everything she knew, all fourteen years of it,
came down to the difference between arriving and *arriving*, and she took
the two miles back down the approach road at thirty-one miles an hour
with the radio off, because a truck that comes down a yard road at speed
is an emergency and an emergency gets a decision made about it at the
gate.

She thought about Adam at the light on the way over, saying *it's the
last flat surface*.

She thought about the fact that she had not answered him.

She put it away — not gently, not with any sort of peace about it, just
put it in the place where she put things she would have to carry later —
because the gate was coming up and there were four men on the other side
of it and she needed all of herself.

Two hundred yards out she turned the headlights on full.

At a hundred she put the window all the way down, and the cold came in
off the river, and she could smell the water and the diesel and the iron
smell that container yards have everywhere in the world.

At fifty she put both hands up on top of the wheel where they could be
seen from anywhere in the yard, and drove the last of it that way, with
her wrists in the light and her face lit up by her own dash.

That is how you enter a yard you are not welcome in. Slow, lit, hands
showing, window down. She had learned it at twenty-three from a man in
Melvindale who had learned it in Beirut, and she had used it four times
in her life and had never once expected to use it in a place where she
had signed a receipt fifty minutes earlier.

The gate was shut.

A man came out from the scale-house side, and it was the same one, the
pleasant one, and he had his hand somewhere she chose not to look at, and
she stopped the truck eleven feet short of the fence line and let the
engine idle and did not open the door.

"Ms. Dalca," he said. "You've made a wrong turn."

"Tell Gino the manifest is wrong."

She said it in the voice she used at scale houses, the one that has no
performance in it at all, the one that says *I am tired and you are tired
and there is a form to fix*.

"He's signed his receipt," she said. "He's got the white copy and I've
got the yellow and I'm telling you that piece of paper does not discharge
anything, because the price on it was set in 2012 against future work,
and nobody in eleven years has ever run the interest on it. I've run it.
It's in my coat. It's four pages.

"Now, I can turn around and drive to Chicago," Mira said, "and this
becomes a thing that gets discussed by people above him about why a
delivery he took receipt of on a Sunday night came apart over an
accounting error. Or you can walk over there and tell him, in these
words: *she says the arithmetic's wrong and she's got it itemized*."

The man stood in the road with his hand where it was.

"He'll want to know why you didn't say it inside."

"Because I hadn't finished adding it up," said Mira Dalca, "and I don't
bring a number to that man until it's right."

Something happened in the man's face that she catalogued and would think
about later, which was that he believed her instantly, and that the
reason he believed her instantly was that it was the single most
Traversa-shaped sentence available in the English language, and he had
worked for Gino Traversa for a long time.

He walked away toward the scale house with a phone at his ear.

Mira sat with the engine running and the window down and her hands on top
of the wheel and looked, past the fence, past the shed, at the long low
container office on the far side of the yard where there was a light on.

*Be alive,* she thought. *Be alive for forty minutes.*

The gate rolled open at eight fifty-six.

She drove in with her lights on and her hands showing, and the gate
rolled shut behind the truck for the second time that night, and this
time she had counted the doors on the way in.


## Chapter Fifteen — The Handover

The scale house had not changed in forty-eight minutes and that was the
first useful thing.

Same eleven by fourteen. Same counter, same two windows, same space
heater running off a cord that went out under the door, same chair.

Same man in it.

But there was a difference and she found it in the first two seconds,
because she had been trained by fourteen years of yards to walk into a
room and read what had moved: the manifest was still on the counter, and
the white copy was still under it, and Gino Traversa had not put either
of them away.

Which meant he had not gone anywhere. Which meant nothing had been
finished.

Which meant, and she made herself hold it flat and not let it into her
chest where it would be no use to anybody, that there was a real chance
Adam Marek was alive in a container office sixty yards away with a light
on.

"Four pages," Traversa said.

"Four pages."

"You know what nobody's ever done." He did not get up. He was
sixty-three years old and he sat the way men of that age sit in cold
rooms, forward, with his forearms on his knees. "Nobody's ever come back
through that gate. In this whole business, in forty years. They come out.
They don't come back."

"I'm not here about him."

"No?"

"I'm here about a bill," Mira Dalca said, "and I want to do it standing
up, because it's long."

She took the four pages out of her coat.

They were diner placemats. That was the truth of it, and she had decided
in the truck that she was not going to hide it, because hiding it would
have been a performance and Gino Traversa had spent forty years watching
men perform: four paper placemats from a 1962 diner across the highway,
covered edge to edge in her own small columnar handwriting, done in
eleven minutes at a counter with a woman named Deb refilling her coffee.

She put them on the counter and turned them to face him and did not let
go of the top one.

"Eleven thousand dollars," she said. "April 2012. That's the principal.
Do you want to argue about the principal?"

Traversa looked at the placemats.

"No," he said.

"Good. Then here's the first thing, and it's not a complaint, it's a
figure." She put one finger on the first column. "Nobody ever set a rate.
In fourteen years, nobody has ever once written down what the interest
was. Which means it was never a loan. A loan has a rate. What it was, was
an *account*, and an account gets reconciled, and nobody has ever
reconciled it, and I have been the person who reconciles everything in
this organization since 2016."

"Mira—"

"Page one is what I've moved," she said. "By year."

·

Here is what she had, and this was the thing she had understood in a
diner booth at one in the afternoon while a man asked her to say the
thing she was not saying:

She had eleven years of Gino Traversa's paperwork in her head.

Not in a file. Not in a tin. In her head, where she had put it because
she had never trusted a piece of paper in her life, and because a woman
who reconciles a road boss's freight for eleven years without ever once
being permitted to ask a question develops, out of pure defensive habit,
a total recall for numbers she is not supposed to have.

She had never done anything with it. She had never intended to. It had
sat in her the way the tin sat under the plank, and the reason she had
never spent it was the reason she had never spent the eleven, which was
that spending things was what *they* did.

And in a diner booth she had realized that she was not going to spend it
either. She was going to *say* it. Out loud. Slowly. In a small room. For
as long as it took.

Because Gino Traversa had one weakness and it was not greed and it was
not vanity. It was that he had spent forty years being the man who
understood the freight better than anybody in the room, and there was no
force on this earth that could make him get up and walk out of a
conversation in which somebody was reading his own numbers back to him
and getting them right.

Not while there was a chance the next line was wrong.

She had forty minutes to sell. That was the whole job. It was the exact
job she had been doing since she was twenty-three, at scale houses and
gates and crossings, for eleven people whose names were in a coffee tin —
manufacture time, out of nothing, in front of a man with the power — and
the only difference tonight was that she was doing it for one man, and
that she was going to have to spend herself to do it, and that she had
gone past caring about that somewhere on the shoulder of a bridge.

·

"2015," she said. "Four hundred and eleven loads out of the Torrence

## Chapter Sixteen — Day Nineteen

On the nineteenth of May, at twenty minutes to ten in the morning, Adam
Marek walked into a federal courtroom on the twenty-third floor of the
Dirksen building on his own feet.

Nobody was holding his arm.

That was the whole of it and it took him the length of the corridor to
absorb. He had been imagining this walk for six months and in every
version of it there had been a hand somewhere on him — a marshal at the
elbow, the flat guiding pressure of a man in a corridor — because that
was how he had entered every room since November and he had stopped
noticing it the way you stop noticing a coat.

There were two deputy marshals. They walked ahead of him and behind him
and neither of them touched him at any point, and one of them held the
door.

He had a suit. Somebody had produced it out of the machinery of the
government at eight o'clock that morning and it did not fit him at the
shoulders, and he had thought, doing the tie in front of a mirror in a
room on the eleventh floor: *I have not put on a piece of clothing I
chose since the second week of November.*

He was forty-one years old. He had run fourteen trucks. He had lost
eighteen pounds in a rented house in Berwyn and put four of them back on
in a passenger seat between Chicago and the border, mostly in the form of
gas-station sandwiches handed to him without comment by a woman who never
once asked whether he was hungry, because asking is a question and she
did not do questions, she did deliveries.

He got to the door of the courtroom and stopped for a second, which the
marshal took for nerves and which was not nerves.

It was that he had counted the exits, out of habit, and there were three.

Three. Not two. A courtroom is a room with three doors — the public
doors, the judge's door, the jury's — and he had spent nineteen days and
six months and eleven years counting rooms that had two, and he stood in
the doorway of the Northern District of Illinois and understood that he
had arrived somewhere with a door to spare.

·

She was in the second row.

He had asked for that. It was, in fact, the only thing he had asked for
in three days of being handled by extremely competent people who kept
telling him he did not have to do this today — the second row, on the
left, behind the government's table, where he could see her from the
witness box without turning his head.

Raman had said, "That's not usual," and Adam had said, "I know," and
Raman had looked at him for a moment over her glasses and made a note and
it had been arranged.

Mira Dalca had been in a federal building since Sunday night in one
capacity or another. She had slept, by her own account, four hours in
three days. She was wearing a navy jacket that was not hers and had come
from a duty officer's locker, and her hands were flat on her knees, and
she looked exactly like what she was, which was a woman who had run a
truck yard for eleven years and had been up since four.

She did not smile at him. She had never once smiled at him and he would
not have known what to do with it.

She looked at him and gave him, across a courtroom, the small
economical nod that a driver gives another driver at a gate: *you're
clear, go*.

Adam Marek took the stand.

"State your name for the record."

And this was the thing.

This was the part he had run four hundred times in a room in Berwyn with
a cribbage board on the table, the whole six months collapsed down into
five words, and he opened his mouth and nothing came out of it.

Not fear. He would be exact about this afterward, to Raman, to a
psychologist the government made him see in July, to Mira in a yard
office in October: it was not fear.

It was that a name is a claim.

For eleven years he had been a man who moved other people's property and
had been very careful to have as little of his own as possible, and the
smallest thing you can own is your name, and he had spent seven years
after 2019 giving it as little as possible — to marshals, to a landlord in
Berwyn who knew him as Anderson, to two men at a door who had used it as
a courtesy before putting him in a truck.

The court reporter waited. The judge, who had seen this before, did not
say anything at all.

"Adam Marek," he said.

It came out wrong. It came out at about half the volume of a sentence and
it went nowhere, and the reporter looked up.

"Sir, could you repeat that for the microphone."

Adam Marek put both hands flat on the rail of the witness box.

He thought, in a way that was not a decision and arrived whole: *nothing
I carry gets left on the road.*

He thought: *you arrive on the day I said, on your own feet, with your own
name on you.*

She had said it in a scale-house yard in Portage, Indiana, under the

worked out how to say a load-bearing one.

He had carried it for seventeen days in a passenger seat.

He had carried it into a container office and put his shoulder against a
steel wall with it.

"Adam Marek," he said, and it went into the room, and it went into the
record, and it stayed there.

Across the courtroom, in the second row, a woman put her hand over her

in the hundreds and everybody in this room would write it down.

"I'm not going to do it," he said, "because the number isn't mine to make
up. Those are people. Some of them are alive. If I sit here and estimate
them, then I'm doing to them in a courtroom the exact thing I did to them
on a road, which is handling them without ever once finding out who they
were.

"I'll give you the runs and the dates and the plates," Adam Marek said,
"and you can count them, and whatever you get, that's the number. But
I'm not putting a guess on human beings in a record. Not again."

The room was quiet.

"Thank you," Priya Raman said. "Let's go to the dates."

·

In the second row, Mira Dalca sat with her hands flat on her knees and
listened to a man describe, in the ordinary bored competent voice of the
trade, the route that had brought her to Chicago in April 2012.

The Montreal yard. The container legs. The truck down.

She had thought she would not be able to survive it. She had said so, on
Sunday night, at about two in the morning, to a woman in a windbreaker,
in the only moment of the entire week in which she had come apart.

What she found, sitting in a wooden pew on the twenty-third floor of a
federal building on a Tuesday in May, was that it was extraordinarily
boring, and that the boringness was the mercy.

It was a road. He described it as a road. Mileage and gates and hours and
where the fuel was, and at no point did the man in the box perform one
single ounce of feeling about it, and she understood at about the third
hour that this was deliberate, and that he was doing it for her, and that
it was the second-largest gift she had ever been given.

The largest was a coat held closed on a breakwater.

·

They took her statement on the same day, in a beige room on the eleventh
floor with a table and four chairs and a window that did not open, and it
took nine hours, and it was not boring at all.

Anthony Okafor arrived at half past eight in the morning and had been
retained, he said, by an anonymous party. Mira said she had no anonymous
party, and Okafor said that in his experience anonymous parties usually
turned out to be a man who ran a truck yard and had a sister in Evanston,
and did she wish to proceed.

She proceeded.

The immunity took three hours to argue and it was narrow and it was hard
and Okafor earned every dollar of a fee that Benny Osei never once
admitted to paying. It covered her passage in 2012. It covered eleven
years of reconciling Gino Traversa's freight. It covered a great many
things she had done in the ordinary course of running a yard that she had
never previously understood to be crimes, which was its own education.

It did not cover the eleven.

Nothing covered the eleven, because the eleven were never in it.

Raman raised them once, at four in the afternoon, in the same flat voice
she used for everything.

"Ms. Dalca. I want to ask you once more and then I'll leave it. Eleven
passages, documented, in your hand, corroborating a pattern of movement
across the same lanes Mr. Marek described this morning. That is the
strongest single piece of evidence available to this office and you are
the only person who can produce it."

"Yes," Mira said.

"Why not the other eleven?"

Mira Dalca sat in a beige room with her hands flat on a table.

"Because they didn't agree to be moved," she said.

Raman waited.

"That's the whole answer, but I'll say the rest so it's on the tape."
Mira looked at the recorder. "Every one of those people got into the back
of a vehicle because there was no other door. That's not a choice, that's
arithmetic, and I did it to them, and I'd do it again, and I have never
once pretended it was consent.

"And now you're asking me to put their names in a document that goes into
a courthouse and gets read by lawyers and clerks and eventually
journalists," she said, "and they haven't agreed to that either. Nobody's
going to ask them. You'd be doing the same thing to them that I did, in a
different building, with better stationery.

"I've done that to people once," Mira Dalca said. "I'm not doing it
twice."

Priya Raman looked at her for a while.

"Understood," she said, and closed the folder, and it was never raised
again, and in the four years of litigation that followed, in eleven
thousand pages of filings and exhibits and transcript, the eleven were
never named in any document by anybody.

·

At the end of the ninth hour Raman put her pen down and did the thing
that Mira had not been braced for, because there had been nothing in her
life to brace with.

"There's a finding I want to put to you," Raman said. "It's not a
question. It's the office's position and I'd like you to hear it said out
loud before you go home, and then we're finished for today."

"Alright."

"In April 2012 you were nineteen years old. You were transported from
Constanța to Toronto in a shipping container and from Toronto to Chicago
in a truck. You did not arrange it, you did not price it, and you were
not asked."

"No."

"A fee of eleven thousand dollars was assigned to you afterward, against
future labour, by a person you never met."

"Yes."

"Ms. Dalca, that is not a debt."

The room hummed the way rooms with no windows do.

"It is not an underpaid debt, it is not an outstanding debt, and it was
not discharged at a border yard on Sunday night," Priya Raman said. "It
is not a debt. It has never at any point since 2012 been a debt. There is
nothing in the law of this country or any other under which a person
incurs an obligation by being trafficked, and I want to be precise with
you because I don't think anyone ever has been: there has been nothing to
discharge. Not since the day you arrived. Not for one single hour of
fourteen years."

Mira Dalca sat in a chair, in a building, with her hands flat on a table.

She had already been told this. Adam had said the same sentence on a
picnic table above Lake Huron at five in the morning on the seventeenth
and she had worked it out herself, in her own handwriting, on the twelfth
line of a list, half an hour before that.

It turned out that knowing a thing and having it said to you in a beige
room by a stranger with a folder are entirely different operations.

"Say it again," she said.

Raman said it again.

Mira looked at her hands, which were her mother's hands, which had been
on a wheel for fourteen years.

"Fourteen years," she said.

"Yes."

"I got up at four in the morning."

"I know."

"Every day. Fourteen years. I built a yard on it." Her voice did not do
anything at all. "There are eleven people alive because of a debt that
didn't exist."

Priya Raman, who had built the Moretti prosecution off a bookkeeper's
ledger and had not been visibly moved by anything in eleven years, took
her glasses off.

"Yes," she said. "There are."

·

There was one more thing, and Raman did it standing up, with her coat
already over her arm, in the doorway, which Mira came to understand later
was deliberate.

"I want to give you a name and I want you to write it down."

"I don't write things down."

"You'll write this one." Raman put a card on the table. "Elena Fusco. She
runs a legal clinic on Ashland and she has spent nineteen years doing
nothing but immigration relief for trafficking survivors, and she is
extremely boring and extremely good and she does not lose."

Mira looked at the card and did not pick it up.

"I've got Okafor."

"Okafor is a criminal defence lawyer and he has done a superb job today
and he cannot do this," Raman said. "This office is going to want you in
this country and testifying in the spring, and you have had lawful status
since 2020 and you have never once moved past it, and I would like to
know why."

"Because it holds," Mira said. "It holds as long as nobody looks at
April 2012."

"That." Raman did not move from the doorway. "That, exactly. Say the next
part."

"There isn't a next part. That's the whole of it. I've got a piece of
paper that works and there is a fact underneath it that would take it
away if anybody ever went and got it, and so for six years I have not
filed one single form of any kind, and I have not left the country, and I
have not been in a room with a lawyer, and I have run a yard under
another man's licence." She heard herself say it out loud for the first
time in her life. "You keep the paper and you don't disturb it. That's
the arrangement."

"Ms. Dalca, that entry is not a defect in your file."

"It's an illegal entry."

"It is a *documented instance of trafficking*," Priya Raman said, "and it
has been treated as one in federal law since the year 2000, and there is
relief that runs to permanent residence and past it, and Elena Fusco has
obtained it for about two hundred people, and every single one of them
had to write the true thing in the box first.

"You have spent six years believing that the fact under your paper is a
mine," she said. "It isn't. It's the application."

The room went very quiet.

"Since 2000," Mira said.

"Since 2000."

"Nobody told me."

"No."

"There was nobody to tell me." She heard her own voice do something and
did not try to stop it. "That's the design as well, isn't it. That's not
an accident either."

"No," Raman said. "It isn't. A person who thinks the truth about herself
is the thing that would sink her doesn't go and ask anybody, and that is
worth a great deal of money to a great many people, and it has been for a
very long time."

She put her coat on.

"Take the card."

Mira Dalca sat in a beige room and looked at a business card on a table.

Fourteen years of a coffee tin under a plank. Fourteen years of a licence
in another man's name. Fourteen years of being extremely careful never to
put a true sentence anywhere a person could pick it up and read it, on
the load-bearing assumption — never once examined, never once tested,
never once said out loud to a single human being who would have known —
that there was no door.

Mira Dalca took the card.

·

Benny Osei's licence suspension was vacated at twenty past eleven that
morning in Springfield by a hearing officer named Delacroix who had read
the Chicago papers over breakfast and had, according to Okafor's
associate, spent about four minutes on it.

Two rooms, two hundred miles apart, on the same morning: a man in a
courtroom on the twenty-third floor giving his name for the record, and a
man in a hearing room off a corridor in Springfield being handed back a
piece of paper by a woman who did not look up.

Mira found out about it at nine o'clock at night, in the yard, on
Torrence Avenue, when she came through the gate for the first time in
nineteen days and saw the light on in the shed.

·

Benny had turned the chair round the wrong way and was sitting on it
backwards with his arms along the back of it, which he did when he was
too tired to sit properly and too pleased to go home.

"There she is," he said.

Mira stood in the doorway of a dispatch shed and counted the exits,
because she was never in her life going to stop doing that, and there
were two, and she came in anyway.

"You paid Okafor."

"I have no idea what you're talking about."

"Benny."

"I remortgaged nothing, I sold nothing, and my sister is not involved,"
Benny Osei said, "and if you press me on it I shall become extremely
Ghanaian about the whole subject and we'll be here till Thursday."

She sat down on the edge of the desk.

The board was up. Eleven trucks, four of them out, the magnets in Benny's
handwriting. The kettle. The smell of diesel and cold metal and the
paraffin heater. Nineteen days.

"Two men sat in that chair," Benny said.

Mira looked at the chair.

"I know."

"I want to tell you two things about it and then we'll have tea." He said
it without any drama whatsoever. "The first is that I gave them nothing.
Not one thing. Not a route, not a plate, not a name, not the thing under
the floor, and I want that on the record between us because you'll never
ask.

"The second," Benny Osei said, "is that I would have."

The heater ticked.

"If they'd come back with the older one and a crowbar at four in the
morning, I'd have told them where the plank was inside eleven seconds.
I'm fifty-eight and I've got a bad hip and I've been afraid since
Tuesday the fifth." He looked at her steadily. "I'd like you to know that
about me, because you've spent eleven years believing I'm a better man
than I am, and I'd rather be known."

Mira Dalca sat on the edge of a desk in a shed she had built.

"Benny," she said, "that's what a debt's *for*."

"Sorry?"

"A real one. Not the other kind." She was working it out as she said it,
which was new. "You've had eleven years of me being unable to say one
true thing to you, and you carried it, and you never once put a price on
it, and you'd have given them the plank because you're a human being with
a hip. That's the whole of it. Nobody's owed. You're just — " she moved
her hand, "— on the list."

"What list?"

"The one that's replacing the other one."

·

They had tea and Benny told her about the yard, because that was the only
way either of them had ever been able to have a conversation about
anything that mattered.

Nineteen days. Kowalczyk had run every day of it and had not once asked
where she was. Ricky had put a mirror through a gatepost at the Melrose
Park lot and had been extremely upset about it and Benny had told him it
was a fifty-dollar mirror and a free lesson. Two customers had gone
elsewhere. One — a broker in Bedford Park who had been trying to get a
rate out of her since February — had called four times and left messages
of increasing warmth and had, this afternoon, sent an email offering a
lane.

"He's read the papers," Mira said.

"He has certainly read the papers."

"So he wants to be able to say he stayed."

"He wants to be able to say he stayed," Benny agreed, "and I want you to
take the lane anyway, because his money is the same colour as anybody's
and we are going to need it, and because in about four years you will
have entirely stopped caring why people do things and started caring only
whether they do them, and I'd like you to begin practising."

Mira turned her mug around on the desk.

"Say the thing you're not saying."

Benny Osei put his tea down.

"They'll come back," he said. "Not those ones. Those ones are finished
and they'll go away for twenty years and the papers will get bored by
August. But there'll be others, in about two years, when the yard's
running again and there's a new man on the south side who needs a carrier
who can be relied upon, and somebody will remember that there used to be
a woman on Torrence Avenue who was very good at this."

"I know."

"And when they come," Benny said, "you're going to want to be a woman
with her own licence and her own lawyer and her own name on a federal
docket, because the only thing on this earth that has ever protected
anybody from those people is being *too visible to move quietly*."

She looked at him for a while.

"You've had that one ready."

"I have had that one ready since about half past four this afternoon on
I-55," Benny said, "and I would like it noted that I got through the
whole of it without stopping."

·

They took the plank up at half past ten at night.

It came up the way it always had, with a flat bar, four screws that had
been countersunk in 2015 and had never once been painted over because
paint gets noticed. Underneath: eleven inches of joist space, a plastic
bag against the damp, and a coffee tin.

Mira put it on the desk.

"Do you want me out?" Benny said.

"No."

She took the lid off.

Eleven entries on a folded page in her own handwriting, going back to
2019, name and date and place and route and the word at the end. And a
twelfth line, added at a picnic table above Lake Huron four days ago in a
different pen.

She read them aloud.

All twelve. Out loud, once, in an empty shed on Torrence Avenue, to the
only other person on earth who had ever known there was a list — and it
took nine minutes, and Benny Osei sat on a chair the wrong way round with
his arms along the back of it and did not say one word.

Number seven took the longest.

"Alive," Mira said, at the end, on the twelfth. "That's the last one."

"That's the last one," Benny agreed.

They burned it in the bin behind the shop where they burned the oil rags,
in the yard, under the sodium lights, in the fog coming off the Calumet.
The paper went first and then the folded page and then the tin, which did
not burn, and which Benny fished out with a bar and dropped into a skip.

Nobody said anything ceremonial. They stood in a yard at eleven at night
and watched a fire in a bin, which is a thing that happens in truck yards
every night of the year.

"Right," Benny said. "Tea."

·

The form was on the desk when they came back in, because he had put it
there before she arrived, which meant he had printed it that afternoon,
which meant he had done it between a hearing in Springfield and a drive
back up I-55, which she noticed and did not mention.

*ILLINOIS SECRETARY OF STATE — APPLICATION FOR MOTOR CARRIER OPERATING
AUTHORITY.*

Mira Dalca stood and looked at it.

"I'm not licensable," she said.

"You have been licensable since 2020," said Benny Osei.

"Benny—"

"Six years." He filled the kettle. "You got the status in 2020 and you
have never once filed, and every year I put the form on the desk and
every year you look at it the way you're looking at it now and find a
reason, and the reason is always excellent and always about the *yard*."
He put the kettle down. "It was never about the yard. It was that you did
not believe you were allowed to have your name on a piece of paper in
this country, because a man told you in 2012 that you were carrying
something, and you have been paying for a bunk on a boat ever since."

"There was never a debt," Mira said.

"I know. I heard it on the radio at six o'clock," Benny said, "from a
government lawyer, and I had to pull over on I-55, and I am not going to
discuss that further."

She sat down and pulled the form toward her.

It took forty minutes, because she filled it out the way she filled out
everything, in block capitals, checking each line twice. Name. Date of
birth. Place of birth: Constanța, Romania. Date of entry: April 2012.
There was a box for that and she looked at it for a long time and then
wrote the truth in it, because there was now nothing else available.

At the bottom there was a line for a sponsor.

She turned it round and pushed it across the desk and Benny Osei signed
it without reading it, in about two seconds, and pushed it back and
started making the tea, and did not make a thing of it at all.

"Right," he said, from the kettle. "Twelve's still got a noise."


## Chapter Seventeen — Safe Passage

He came to the gate on foot at a quarter to eleven at night, from a bus.

That was the argument. He had spent most of the day constructing better
ones and had thrown all of them away on the Halsted platform at about
nine o'clock, because they were all speeches, and because he had realized
somewhere between the Loop and Eighty-seventh Street that the only
statement available to him that could not be misread was arriving on his
own legs at a gate nobody had driven him to.

Nineteen days in a passenger seat. Six months in a rented house. Eleven
years before that in the cab of somebody's truck or the office of
somebody's yard, and every single mile of it in a vehicle that belonged
to an organization.

So: a bus, then the Red Line, then another bus, then eleven blocks along
Torrence Avenue in the dark past the tank farms and the rail spur, with
the fog coming up off the Calumet and the sodium lights making their
orange holes in it, and the sound of the interstate a mile off like
water.

It took him two hours and forty minutes. He had checked the distance
before he set out — twenty-one miles — and had done it in his head the
whole way as a fuel calculation out of pure habit, and had laughed once,
out loud, alone, on a bus on Ninety-fifth Street.

The gate was a chain-link slider on a track with a padlock and a sign
that said WISNIEWSKI CARTAGE and under it, in smaller letters, NO
ADMITTANCE WITHOUT AUTHORIZATION.

Adam Marek stood outside it and did not go in.

There was a light on in the office, four hundred feet away across the
gravel. He could see her through the window: sitting at the desk with the
board behind her, doing paperwork at eleven o'clock at night, because of
course she was.

He put his hands on the fence.

Then he took them off, because a man's hands on a fence at eleven at
night is a shape he did not want to be, and he stood back from it and
called her name once, in an ordinary voice, across four hundred feet of
gravel.

"Mira."

·

She came out fast. He saw the door go and heard the gravel and she was
across a third of the yard before she had properly registered that
nothing was on fire, and he stood at the gate and let her come, and did
not say anything to slow her down, because he had spent nineteen days
learning that this woman moved first and processed afterward and that the
moving was the tell.

She stopped on the other side of the fence.

"How did you get here?"

"Bus," Adam said.

"From the—"

"From Dirksen to the Red Line to the ninety-five bus to the eleven blocks
up Torrence." He put his hands in his pockets. "Two hours forty. I
brought nothing. Nobody drove me and nobody knows I'm here and I have got
a receipt for a bus fare in my pocket, and I would like you to understand
that all of that is on purpose and is, in fact, the entire content of
what I came to say."

Mira Dalca stood on her own side of her own gate.

"Say it anyway."

"I'm going to. I want to do it out here first."

"Adam, it's eleven o'clock, come inside—"

"No," Adam said, "and this is the last time I'll be difficult about a
door, I promise you. I want to say it from out here because out here I
haven't been let in yet, and the whole point is that I'm asking."

She stood very still.

"Alright," she said. "Ask."

·

"On the eighteenth, on the breakwater, I withdrew it," Adam said. "All of
it. Everything we agreed at that table in the cabin court on the
fourteenth.

"I want to say first that I meant it and I'd do it again and I'm not
apologizing for it. I was a man who had been in somebody's custody for
nineteen days — yours, and before that the government's, and before that
theirs — and a man in custody can't agree to anything. He can only be
relieved. And you have built your entire life out of not being able to
tell those two things apart, and I was not going to be one more person
who blurred it for you at six in the morning while you were still cold."

"I know," Mira said. "I got that at the time. I got it about four hours
later."

"Good."

"It was still the worst thing anybody's ever done to me."

"I know that too," Adam said. "I've had two days of knowing it."

The fog moved along the fence line.

"I've been a free man for about eleven hours," he said. "That's the
count. I gave my name to a court at ten in the morning on the nineteenth
and I walked out of that building at four in the afternoon and stood on
Dearborn for a while and nobody in the world had any claim on me at all.
No agreement, no protection order, no debt, no route, no seat. Nothing.
Eleven hours."

"And?"

"And I want to say it again from here," Adam said. "From this side of
your fence, at night, on my own legs, with nothing on me. That's the
only place it counts. Everything I said to you in that truck I said
sitting in a seat you were driving, and I'm not letting one word of it
stand."

Mira Dalca put both hands on the chain-link.

"Say it, then."

"I'd like the terms back," Adam said. "All of them. And I'd like to widen
one."

·

He had worked out the wording on a bus.

"On the fourteenth we agreed to a set of conditions and every one of them
had a fence round it," he said. "In the cab. Not in front of anybody.
Nothing on the road. Nothing at a yard. And that was right — that was
exactly right for two people in the middle of a delivery, and I'd sign it
again for those nineteen days.

"I don't want it now," Adam said. "I want it anywhere. In front of
anybody. In a yard, in a diner, in a corridor at the Dirksen building
with four lawyers watching, at Benny Osei's kitchen table, at the gate,
in daylight.

"I've spent my whole life being moved quietly," he said. "Eleven years of
driving loads that weren't on any manifest, seven years of being a man
with a different name in a rented house, nineteen days in a passenger
seat with the window up. I've never once had a thing about me that
happened where people could see it. And I'd like this one to be that,
and I'd like it to be that from the start, and I'd like it to be that
even if the answer's no, because you should get to say no in daylight
too."

The fog came along the fence.

"That's the whole ask," Adam said. "Scope: anywhere. Everything else the
same as the fourteenth. You can take a week."

Mira Dalca stood with her fingers through the chain-link.

"No," she said.

Adam went absolutely still.

"Not *no* — Christ." She closed her eyes for a second. "That was — I've
been doing that my whole life, I lead with the word and then I put the
sentence after it. No, I'm not taking a week."

"Ah."

"Give me a minute. I have to do this the unfluent way and I've been
practicing since Sunday and I'm still bad at it."

"Take the minute," Adam said.

·

She took about forty seconds.

"Here are my terms," Mira said. "Yes to all of yours and I'll say that
first so it isn't hanging over the rest, and yes to anywhere, and I want
you to know that the anywhere is the part I'd never have asked for and
the part I want most.

"Now mine. Three."

"Go."

"One." She held up a finger against the fence. "I don't want to route
you. I want to *know* you. And I have to tell you that I do not know how
to do that, and I'm not being modest, I mean it as a technical statement:
I have never in my adult life known a single person and let them know me
at the same time. I've got Benny, and Benny got there by standing next to
me for eleven years and not asking. That's my entire body of experience.

"So I'm going to be bad at it," she said. "For about a year. I've done
the arithmetic and a year is what I'd budget. I'm going to give you
distances when you ask me how I am. I'm going to decide something in a
diner and not tell you. I'm going to do it *again*, Adam, that's not a
theory, that's a forecast — and when I do, I need you to say the word and
not carry it for four days being decent about it. The clause holds. But
you have to enforce it, because I won't see it."

"Agreed," Adam said. "That's fair."

"Two." Second finger. "I'm going to be in court for four years. Traversa's
in the spring, and then the transport counts, and then whatever's after
that, and I'll be doing it under my own name with my whole history read
out loud by strangers in rooms with no windows. Some of it's going to be
in the papers. All of it is going to be true and none of it is going to
be flattering and I am not going to be a good person to be near in
February."

"I've been the witness," Adam said. "I know exactly what February is
like. Next."

"Three." She stopped.

"Three," Adam said.

"Three is a job," Mira Dalca said, "and I want you to hear that it's not
the third thing, it's the first thing, and I put it last because I'm a
coward about the order of a list."

·

"I filed for authority in my own name last night," she said. "Benny
signed as sponsor. It'll take about ninety days and then this yard runs
on my licence, which it has never done, in eleven years.

"When it does I'm short a dispatcher, because Benny's fifty-eight with a
bad hip and I've been working him six days a week since 2015 and I would
like him to be alive in 2040." She had her hand flat on the fence now.
"So there's a board with eleven trucks on it, and it's going to be
fourteen by the autumn, and every one of them legal and boring and
carrying steel and produce and nothing else, and I need somebody who can
run it who understands lanes.

"I can't pay you what you're worth for about two years. The hours are
bad. It's four in the morning and the heater doesn't work and the man in
the shed will disagree with you about everything for the first six months
on principle."

She stopped.

"That's the offer," she said. "Six a.m. Wednesday. Bring a coat you can
sleep in."

Adam Marek stood on the other side of a chain-link gate in the fog on
Torrence Avenue and put his head back and laughed — properly, the way he
had not done in seven years, so that it carried across the gravel and
came back off the shed.

"What," Mira said.

"You did it as a delivery term."

"I—"

"Six a.m. Wednesday, bring a coat you can sleep in." He wiped his eyes
with the back of his wrist. "That's the same grammar as *nothing I carry
gets left on the road*. That's the same woman in the same yard in
Portage, Indiana, on the second of May."

"It's a job offer."

"Mira." Adam put both hands on the fence. "Is it the job or the other
thing?"

And Mira Dalca — who had spent fourteen years fluent in gates and fuel
and distances and mute in every other language available to a human being
— stood in her own yard, under her own lights, and made herself say it in
the plain way.

"It's both," she said. "It's both, and I did it in a delivery term
because that's the only register I've got, and I told you I'd be bad at
this and here we are eleven seconds in.

"The job is real and I need a dispatcher. And I want you to come at six
in the morning on Wednesday and I want you to be here on Thursday and I
want you to still be here in about forty years, and I do not have a
sentence for that, Adam, I've been trying to build one since Sunday and
every one I make comes out as freight."

"That one was fine," Adam said.

"That one was terrible."

"That one was the best sentence anybody's ever said to me," Adam Marek
said, "and I'd like you to open the gate."

·

She opened the gate.

It ran on its track the way it had run since 1961, badly, with the noise
it made in cold weather that Benny had been going to fix since 2019, and
it took her both hands and a shove at the end, and Adam Marek walked into
Wisniewski Cartage on the nineteenth of May at eleven minutes past
eleven at night, on foot, carrying nothing, with nobody's manifest on
him.

He stopped just inside.

"Ask me," Mira said.

"I've got a standing grant. You said anywhere."

"Ask me anyway. I'd like to hear one."

"May I," Adam said.

"Yes."

·

Afterward, neither of them was able to give a coherent account of the
next few minutes, and both of them tried, separately, years later, and
the accounts did not match on a single detail except two.

The first was that it happened in the middle of the yard, in the open, in
the gravel, forty feet inside the gate, under the light pole with the
working lamp, where anybody coming along Torrence Avenue could see it,
and that this was deliberate on both sides and neither of them ever said
so out loud.

The second was that at some point she started shaking and could not stop
and was extremely angry about it, and that he said, into her hair, "It's
just the adrenaline coming off, it took me eleven days in 2019," and she
said, "Shut up, Marek," and did not let go.

·

There was a third thing, which neither of them put in either account,
because it did not occur to either of them that it was unusual.

At some point Adam Marek said, "How far is it?"

And Mira Dalca, with her face against his collarbone in the gravel of her
own yard at eleven o'clock at night, said, "Twenty-one miles," without
having to think about it, and then, after a second, "Two hours forty on a
Tuesday. Nearer three if you miss the ninety-five bus."

"I did miss the ninety-five bus."

"Then it was three hours."

"It was three hours and eleven minutes," Adam said, "and I'd like it
minuted that I did every single one of them on purpose," and she said,
"Noted," into his shirt, and neither of them moved for a while longer.

·

They sat in the office afterward with the door open and the fog coming in
and drank tea out of two mugs that said things about trucking.

The board was up. Eleven trucks. Four out.

"That one's got a noise," Adam said, looking at it.

"Twelve," Mira said. "Yes. Everybody knows. It's had a noise since 2021
and Benny brings it up eleven times a week and I have personally driven
it four hundred miles listening for the noise and there is no noise."

"There's a noise."

"There is no noise."

"Mira," Adam Marek said, "I have run fourteen trucks for eleven years and
I am telling you that when a man who has run a yard for you since 2015
says a truck has got a noise, the truck has got a noise, and on Wednesday
morning at six o'clock I am going to find it."

Mira Dalca sat behind a desk in a shed on Torrence Avenue at midnight and
looked at a man who had arrived on a bus.

"Wednesday," she said.

"Wednesday."

·

At about half past midnight he asked her about the tin, because it was
the only thing left in the room that had not been said.

"It's gone."

"Gone how?"

"Bin behind the shop, with the oil rags, at half past ten." She had her
hands round a mug. "Benny stood there and I read all twelve out loud
first and it took nine minutes and then we burned it, and he fished the
tin out with a bar because tin doesn't burn, and put it in the skip."

Adam Marek sat with that for a while.

"Twelve."

"Twelve."

"You read yourself out."

"I read myself out," Mira said. "Out loud. To a man in a shed. And I want
to tell you what it was like, because you'll ask me in about a year
anyway and I'd rather do it now while I'm still able to."

"Tell me."

"It was boring." She turned the mug around. "That's the whole report. I'd
built it up for fourteen years into a thing that would take the roof off,
and it was eleven names and a date and a place and a word, and it took
nine minutes, and Benny didn't say anything, and at the end of it I said
*alive* and then I said *that's the last one* and he said *that's the
last one* and we went outside and set fire to a piece of paper in a bin."

She looked up.

"And then I went in and filled out a form."

"That's the part that got you."

"That's the part that got me," Mira said. "There's a box on it that says
DATE OF ENTRY INTO THE UNITED STATES and I sat and looked at it for about
four minutes, and there was nothing to put in it except April 2012, and
so I wrote April 2012, and it's going to be read by a clerk in
Springfield who won't look at it twice."

Adam put his mug down.

"Say the thing about it."

"There's nothing to—"

"Mira."

She was quiet for a second.

"I have spent fourteen years being a person whose life could not survive
being written down," she said. "That's the whole architecture. Everything
in a tin, everything in my head, nothing in my name, nothing on any piece
of paper anywhere that a person could pick up and read.

"And it turns out you can just write it in the box," she said. "It's a
box. It's a quarter of an inch high. It's got a *line* under it."

·

At some point after one in the morning she took him out into the yard and
showed him the sign.

It was the original. Painted in 1961 on two eight-foot sheets bolted to
the frame above the gate, and repainted twice, the second time in 2016 by
a man in Hegewisch who had matched the lettering off a photograph.

WISNIEWSKI CARTAGE.

"Everybody's asked me for eleven years why I never changed it," Mira
said. "I always said it costs money and it attracts reading."

"Both true."

"Both true and neither of them was it." She had her hands in her coat
pockets, looking up at it. "Tadeusz Wisniewski put that sign up in 1961
and died in 1979 owing nobody anything. One truck, then four, then
eleven, and he paid tax on every one of them.

"His son ran it for thirty-two years and sold it to me in 2015 for eighty
percent of what it was worth and a handshake, and told me twice to put my
own name on the board, and died the spring after." She kept looking up.
"And I have never once in fourteen years been able to work out how a
person gets to be that, and I thought if I kept the name over the gate I
might learn it by standing under it."

Adam looked up at the sign.

"And?"

"And it turns out that's not how it works," Mira said. "You don't learn
it off a sign. You get it in a beige room off a woman with a folder, in
about eleven seconds, and then it takes a year."

She took her hand out of her pocket and pointed at the bottom right
corner of the second sheet, where there was a rectangle of clean primer
about the size of a road atlas, still tacky, that had not been there in
the morning.

"Benny did that this afternoon," she said. "Before he printed the form.
He didn't ask me and he didn't tell me and when I came in at nine he was
sitting in a chair backwards pretending he hadn't."

"What goes in it?"

"The licence number," Mira Dalca said. "When it comes. And my name under
it, small, in the corner, the way it goes on a carrier's board.

"The sign stays," she said. "I've grown fond of a man who didn't owe
anybody anything. But there's going to be a line under it, and it's going
to be mine, and it's going to be in a place where anybody driving past on
Torrence Avenue can read it."

They stood in the fog under the sodium lights and looked up at a rectangle
of primer.

"That's the whole book," Adam said.

"What is?"

"Safe passage." He had his hands in his pockets. "I've thought about
those two words every day for nineteen days and I had them wrong the
entire time. I thought it meant getting out. A route, a gate, a border,
somebody delivering you to the far side of something."

"It's not that."

"It's not that," Adam Marek said. "It's arriving somewhere and being
allowed to stay."

The fog came off the Calumet. A mile away the interstate ran, and beyond
it the tank farms, and beyond those the lake, which was doing what it had
done for eleven thousand years.

"Six a.m. Wednesday," Mira said.

"I'll be here."

"Bring a coat you can sleep in."

"I've got a coat."

"You've got somebody else's coat," said Mira Dalca. "We'll get you one."


## Epilogue — Open Water

The fog comes earlier in October.

By the first week it is on the yard at five in the morning instead of six,
coming up off the Calumet and standing between the container stacks in
slabs, and the sodium lights make their orange holes in it exactly as
they have done every autumn since 1961, and Mira Dalca walks the fence
line at four-forty with a torch she does not switch on.

She still counts the doors. She has stopped expecting that to change.

What has changed is the arithmetic at the end of the walk, which used to
come out as a list of things that could be taken and now comes out, most
mornings, as an inventory: eleven trucks on the board and three more on
order, fourteen drivers, two dispatchers, four hundred and six thousand
miles run since June, and not one single load on any of it that could not
be read aloud in a courtroom.

The yard is boring now. She had not anticipated how much she would like
that.

·

The authority came through on the eleventh of August.

It arrived in an envelope with a window in it and she opened it standing
in the gravel by the mailbox at seven in the morning and read it four
times, and then went and sat in the cab of a truck that was not going
anywhere for about eleven minutes, which is where Benny Osei found her
and from which he tactfully withdrew.

The number went up on the sign in September. A man came out from Hegewisch
with the same lettering off the same photograph and did it in an
afternoon, and it says, in the bottom right corner of the second sheet,
under WISNIEWSKI CARTAGE, in letters four inches high:

*M. DALCA — MC 1147822*

Adam took a photograph of it on his phone from across Torrence Avenue,
from the far side of four lanes, because he said the point was that you
could read it from there.

She has looked at that photograph more times than she is prepared to
discuss.

·

The trial is in the spring.

Gino Traversa has four counts and the transport arm has eleven defendants
between them, and Okafor says the spring is optimistic and that she
should plan for a year of continuances, and Raman says nothing at all
about the schedule, which Mira has learned to read as agreement.

She will testify under her own name. Everything will be read out: the
container, the price, the fourteen years. She has stopped rehearsing it,
which took until about the end of August, and which happened not through
any decision but on a Tuesday afternoon in the shed when she noticed she
had not run the sentences in her head for three days.

She sees a woman in Beverly on Thursday evenings. This was Adam's
suggestion, made once, in September, in the form of an observation about
his own July, and never mentioned again by either of them.

The eleven were never named. They are not in any document, in any
filing, in any exhibit list, and they will not be, and four separate
lawyers have now explained to Mira Dalca that this was a significant
strategic cost, and she has agreed with all four of them and not moved.

·

Adam Marek dispatches Tuesday through Saturday and is, to the documented
irritation of every driver on the board, extremely good at it.

He found the noise on twelve at ten past nine on his first Wednesday. It
was a heat shield. It had been a heat shield since 2021.

He has an apartment on Avenue O with a lease in his own name that he
signed with a hand that was not entirely steady, and he stays there
perhaps three nights a week, and neither of them has said anything about
the arrangement out loud because neither of them is in any hurry
whatsoever. He owns a coat. He owns a bicycle, a cast-iron pan, four
books, and a cribbage board that a retired deputy marshal named Halvorsen
mailed to him in July with a note that said *111–1, and I want a rematch*.

He is bad at Saturdays. Saturdays are the day the fear comes, and it
comes at about four in the afternoon for no reason at all, and Mira has
learned to be in the yard on Saturdays with a job that needs two people.

She is bad at the middle of the week. She decided something in June
without telling him — a route change, four trailers, nothing dangerous —
and he said the sentence, and she was furious for a day and a half and
then apologized in the ugly halting unfluent way she has, standing up, in
the shed, with her hands doing nothing.

It has been five months. It is going about as well as she budgeted for,
which is to say badly, in the right direction.

·

The envelope came on the fourteenth of October.

No return address. A Canadian stamp and a Thunder Bay postmark, and her
name and the yard's address in a careful hand that had been taught
letters in a different alphabet first.

Inside there was a photograph and nothing else.

A girl of about twenty-one in a graduation gown, on a lawn, squinting
into the sun, holding a rolled paper. There is a woman's arm across her
shoulders, cropped at the elbow, and you cannot see the woman's face.

No note. No names. Nothing on the back.

Mira Dalca stood in a yard on Torrence Avenue in the fog at seven in the
morning and looked at a photograph of a girl she had last seen at
eleven o'clock at night in November 2023, aged eighteen, in the false
floor of a trailer between Portage and Fort Erie, with her mother's hand
over her mouth.

Number nine on a list that no longer exists.

She has no way to reply. That is the design and it is her own design, and
she taught it to the mother herself, in a yard, in the dark, in 2023:
*nothing that can be traced back, ever, in either direction.*

So there will never be a name, and there will never be a note, and there
will never be another one.

It is the only receipt any of the eleven will ever send.

It is the only one she will ever keep.

It is in a frame in the dispatch shed above the board, where a fire
extinguisher inspection certificate used to hang, and every driver who
has asked about it has been told it is a cousin.

·

Gino Traversa is in a federal facility in Milan, Michigan, awaiting
trial, and has not once, in five months, said one word to anybody about
Mira Dalca.

That is not loyalty and she has never once mistaken it for loyalty. It is
the same thing it has always been: a man who learned at forty that a
threat is an object and objects go in evidence bags, and who is not going
to produce one now.

He wrote to her in September.

The letter came through his lawyer, on the lawyer's paper, four lines
long, and Okafor read it first and then handed it over with a face that
said *I have advised you and you are going to do this anyway.*

It said:

*Mira. They tell me you did eleven years of my reconciliation off the top
of your head. I want you to know that I never once checked it. That is
the only compliment I have got left to give anybody. G.T.*

She read it in the shed with the door open and the fog coming in, and
then she did the only thing that seemed to her to be accurate, which was
to put it in the bin behind the shop with the oil rags and not burn it,
because burning things is ceremony and she had finished with ceremony in
May.

·

Benny Osei works four days a week now and has been to Ghana twice.

The first time he went he left a list on the board of everything that
could go wrong, in his own handwriting, in order of probability, eleven
items long, and Adam Marek took a photograph of it and had it laminated,
and it is still up there, and item four happened in the second week
exactly as forecast.

He calls her from Accra at absurd hours to ask about trucks.

"Twelve," he said, in August, at four in the morning her time.

"Twelve is fine. Twelve has been fine since May. Your dispatcher found a
heat shield."

"My dispatcher," Benny said, with enormous satisfaction, down eleven
thousand kilometres of telephone line, "is the second-best hire either of
us has ever made," and hung up before she could ask him what the first
one was, which she thought about for the rest of the day and has never
raised since.

·

She does the morning manifest at ten to five, at the desk, in her own
hand, because she has tried the software four times and it makes her
nervous.

The nineteenth of October. Fog. Two drivers: Kowalczyk, who is
sixty-two now and still hates her, and a new woman called Reyes who has
been on the board for eleven days and is going to be excellent. One
load: forty thousand pounds of coil steel, Burns Harbor to Melrose Park,
which is a run they now do four times a week and which pays badly and
which she took because it is legal and dull and because a yard that runs
coil steel four times a week is a yard that exists in five years.

Sheet number. Date. Carrier.

In the corner of the carrier box, where it has gone on every manifest
since September, in block capitals in her own handwriting:

*WISNIEWSKI CARTAGE — M. DALCA, MC 1147822.*

Nothing owed. Not to anybody, by anybody, in either direction.

She hears the gate at five, and the gravel, and the office door.

"Two hundred and eleven miles," Adam says, hanging his coat on the nail
that is his now, "if you go the way you're going to go, and a hundred and
ninety-four if you'd let me route it, and I've brought the coffee."

Mira Dalca signs the manifest and puts the pen down and gets up.

Outside, the fog is going off the Calumet the way it does when the sun
gets under it, and the lake is out there past the tank farms doing what
it does, and eleven trucks are on a board with nothing hidden in any of
them, and the whole of the road is only a road.

"Let's go to work," she says.

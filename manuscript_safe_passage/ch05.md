## Chapter Five — The Unlisted Crossing

She took the cut on the sixth day, at four-twenty in the afternoon, in
rain.

She had been building toward it for a day and a half without telling him,
and the building had consisted almost entirely of doing nothing
interesting: fuelling where the sheet said, stopping where the sheet
said, running the speed limit through two counties, and letting a grey
Malibu sit at seven car lengths for two hundred miles while a white box
truck leapfrogged ahead of them and waited in lots.

"You're being boring on purpose," Adam said, somewhere north of Pentwater.

"I'm always boring."

"You're being *conspicuously* boring. You've fuelled at the sheet's
station twice. You did the mandatory stop at the mandatory place and you
sat in it for the mandatory forty minutes and you bought a coffee you
didn't drink." He was watching the mirror. "You're feeding them a pattern
so they'll believe the next thing that looks like the pattern."

Mira said nothing, which was an answer.

"How long have you had this planned?"

"Since Muskegon."

"And you didn't tell me because—"

"Because if you'd known, you'd have started riding differently, and the
man in the Malibu has been looking at the back of your head for two
hundred miles." She checked the mirror. "You'd have sat straighter.
Everybody does. It's not a criticism, it's a fact about necks."

Adam thought about that.

"That's genuinely good," he said. "I'd never have thought of that."

"You would."

"No, I wouldn't. I'd have thought of the route. I never thought about the
passenger's neck once in eleven years."

"Yes," Mira said. "I know."

·

The cut was a haul road.

It ran, gate to gate, three and a half miles through a sand and gravel
operation east of the shore highway, from a county road on the south side
to a service crossing on the north side that came out on the far bank of
a river the state had bridged in exactly two other places within forty
miles. It was private property from end to end. It had no county
designation. It appeared on no navigation product anywhere on earth
because the operator had, in 2004, successfully petitioned to have the
whole parcel classified as a single industrial site, and industrial sites
do not have roads, they have *internal circulation*.

Eleven minutes, gate to gate, at the speed the washboard allowed.

No cameras, because a gravel operation photographs its scales and its
office and nothing else. No county, because there was no county in it. No
record, because the gate code went into a logbook that a foreman named
Delgado had been forgetting to keep since the Bush administration.

She had found it in 2019.

She had found it with a Kosovar woman and a nine-year-old boy in the
false floor and thirty-five minutes on the clock and a car she could not
lose on the shore highway, and she had turned off onto a gravel entrance
because she had seen a haul truck come out of it that morning, and she
had gone through the whole thing on nerve with no idea where it came out,
and it had come out on the far side of a river with the shore highway
eleven miles behind her and nobody within four minutes of her in any
direction.

She had gone back three weeks later, in the daytime, with a legitimate
load of pipe for the operation's own yard, and had introduced herself to
Delgado, and had done a favour for Delgado in 2020 that Delgado had never
been asked to repay, and had been running the gate code ever since.

Nobody had sold it to her. That was the thing. Every other route in her
head had come from somebody — a driver, a dispatcher, a man in a bar who
wanted to be impressive. This one she had found herself, at speed, with a
child in the floor.

She had used it four times in seven years.

·

"Two hundred yards," she said. "When I turn, don't look behind us."

"I won't."

"You will. Everybody does. Look at the mirror on your side, because
that's a thing a passenger does anyway, and don't turn your head."

The rain had come in off the lake at three and had settled into the kind
of steady grey that kills visibility without ever becoming weather. The
Malibu was at seven lengths. The white box truck had gone by them
fourteen minutes ago and would be sitting in the lot at the junction
eleven miles ahead, waiting for a truck that was not going to arrive.

Mira did not signal.

She came off the shore highway at fifty-one miles an hour onto an
unmarked gravel entrance between a stand of jack pine and a berm, and was
through the first gate — which stood open, from six a.m. to six p.m., as
it had every working day since 2004 — before the Malibu had finished
going past the mouth of it.

Then she stopped.

Right there, forty feet in, behind the berm, with the engine running.

"What are you—"

"Wait."

Eleven seconds. Twelve.

Through the pines she saw the grey Malibu go by on the highway at speed,
committed, in the outside lane, with two hundred yards of empty road
ahead of it and a driver who had just lost a truck he had been watching
for two days and did not yet know it.

"That's the part that gets you," Mira said. "They don't lose you at the
turn. They lose you when you stop. A driver who's following expects the
truck to be *going somewhere.*"

Then she put it in gear and took the haul road.

·

It was, Adam thought, the single most impressive eleven minutes of
driving he had ever been a passenger for, and he had been a passenger for
a great deal of driving.

The road was washboard gravel with berms on both sides and no shoulder
and standing water in the ruts, and it ran between conveyor structures
and stockpiles forty feet high, grey and black and rust, in the rain. A
loader came the other way at the two-mile point, and Mira put two wheels
up onto the berm without slowing down and gave the operator a lift of two
fingers off the wheel, and the operator gave it back, and that was the
whole transaction.

At the north gate there was a chain and a keypad on a post.

She rolled down the window into the rain, put in a five-digit code
without looking at it, and the chain went slack, and she drove over it.

Then she stopped again, and got out, and walked back in the rain and lay
the chain flat the way she had found it, and got back in soaked to the
shoulders, and drove on.

"You put the chain back."

"You always put the chain back."

Adam looked at the mirror. There was a river behind them now and eleven
miles of highway on the other side of it and no vehicle in the world that
knew where they were.

"How many of these do you have?" he said.

"Enough."

"That's not a number, Mira."

She glanced over.

It was the first time he had used it. She registered that fact with the
same flat clarity with which she registered everything, and filed it, and
found that it would not stay filed.

"Four," she said. "Real ones. Ones that are mine and nobody sold me and
nobody's using. Four in the whole country." She turned the wipers up.
"There were five. I burned one in 2022."

"Burned how?"

"Used it twice in a year." She watched the road. "You use a route twice
inside a year and somebody notices. Then it's not a route, it's a habit,
and a habit gets you killed."

Adam sat back.

"You're the best I've ever seen at this," he said. "I want to say that
once and then I won't say it again, because I know what you think of
compliments. But I ran a board with fourteen trucks and I sat in on route
planning with men who'd been doing it for thirty years, and I have never
in my life seen anybody do what you just did."

Mira Dalca drove for about a mile.

"It's not a compliment," she said, at last. "It's an observation."

And Adam Marek laughed — genuinely, once, surprised out of himself in a
truck in the rain on the sixth day — and after a moment, looking straight
ahead at the road, so did she.

·

The stretch across the mitten took nine hours and neither of them slept.

They came off the river road onto US-10 east at eight in the evening and
ran through Baldwin and Reed City and Evart in the dark, ninety miles of
two-lane with the rain quitting somewhere around Chase, and by eleven at
night they were in the flat middle of the state where there is nothing at
all and the towns are eleven miles apart and the radio gets one station.

Nine hours in a cab is a room with no doors and a view that keeps
changing. There is nothing in it to do and nothing to look at that lasts,
and after the fourth hour the ordinary defences of a human personality
simply stop being available, because a defence requires maintenance and
maintenance requires something else to be happening.

They discovered the boring things first.

That he could not sleep sitting up, but could sleep with his head against
the window if the road was straight, which meant she had been choosing
straight roads for two days without admitting it. That she could not eat
and drive at the same time — physically could not, would put a sandwich
down on the console and forget it existed for ninety miles — and that
this was a thing he began working around by the fourth day without being
asked, handing her food in pieces at stops.

That they both hated the radio and neither had been willing to say so
first, and that it had been on, low, since Chicago, out of pure mutual
courtesy, for five and a half days.

"Turn it off," Adam said, at midnight.

"You turn it off."

"It's your truck."

"You're the guest."

"I'm the *cargo*," Adam said, and reached over and turned it off, and the
silence that came in was so enormous and so welcome that they both
started laughing at the same moment for no reason a third party would
have understood.

·

Somewhere past Clare, at one in the morning, he asked where she was from.

He asked it well. He had had five days to learn how she took questions,
and he asked it the way you ask a person about their work: as though the
answer were a fact about the world rather than a claim on her.

"Where'd you come up?"

She gave him a distance.

"Twelve hundred miles," she said. "Port to port."

"That's the whole answer?"

"It's the accurate one."

"It's a distance."

"Yes."

He let it go, which was the correct move, and which she noticed. And
because he let it go, and because it was hour seven, and because the
middle of Michigan at one in the morning is a place where nothing you say
seems likely to survive until daylight, she gave him the port.

"Constanța," she said.

Adam Marek did not move.

"It's on the Black Sea," she said. "It's the biggest port on it. It's got
a casino on the front that's been falling into the water since before I
was born and everybody photographs it." She watched the road. "I was
nineteen. There was work here — that was the thing you got told, there
was work here and there was a way to get to it and somebody would arrange
the way and you'd pay it back out of the work. Everybody knew somebody
who'd done it. My mother's cousin's girl did it in 2009 and sent
photographs of a car."

"How."

"Container to Toronto." Her voice had gone very level. "Nine days. Not
the whole nine in the box — four. There's a warehouse in the middle where
you're out. Then a truck down to Chicago, and a room over a shop on
Cermak with three other people in it, and a job wiping trailers for
somebody who paid the room instead of paying me."

She checked the mirror.

"And a number. Eleven thousand dollars, against future work. Somebody
said it to me in the first week, in English I didn't have, and I nodded,
and that's the whole contract. Nobody ever said it again." She lifted one
hand off the wheel and put it back. "That was 2012. It's 2026. I own
eleven acres and forty trailers and nine trucks and I have never, in
fourteen years, been out from under that number for one single day."

The road went under them. There were no other headlights anywhere in the
world.

"Say something," she said, after a while, "or I'm putting the radio back
on."

Adam Marek had his hands flat on his knees and was looking at the
windscreen.

"What was the month," he said.

"What?"

"You said 2012. What month did you come into Chicago?"

Mira frowned at the road.

"April," she said. "The end of April. I remember because there was still
ice in the alley behind the shop and I didn't understand it. It was
twenty-eight degrees in Constanța when I left."

Adam did not say anything for eleven miles.

She glanced over twice. His face, in the dash light, had nothing at all
on it, which she had come to understand in six days was not the absence
of an expression but the most extreme one he had.

"Marek."

"Yeah."

"You went quiet."

"I did," he said. "I'm sorry. It's a long night."

And she let it go, because that was the arrangement, because she had let
his silences alone for six days exactly as he had let hers, and because a
woman who has spent an hour telling a stranger the true shape of her life
does not then interrogate him about a pause.

She would think about that pause for four days.

She would think about it, afterward, for years — about the fact that she
had been handed it, out loud, on a two-lane road in the middle of
Michigan at one in the morning, and had driven straight past it at
sixty-two miles an hour with both hands on the wheel.

## Chapter Ten — Waiting on Water

The mate's name was Doiron and he had owed her since 2021 and he called
back on the twelfth at nine in the morning, and Mira knew before he had
finished the first sentence.

"Mira. Listen—"

"You're off the boat."

"I took a transfer in March. Gates fleet, out of Superior. It's better
money and my mother's in Duluth." He sounded genuinely wretched, which
she registered and set aside. "I'd have told you but I didn't know you
were coming and I didn't have a — Mira, I'd have told you."

"Who replaced you?"

"Guy named Petrosyan. He's alright."

"Alright how?"

A pause on the line.

"He's alright," Doiron said, "but he's got a cousin in Chicago."

Mira stood at the window with the phone against her ear and looked at a
thousand-foot boat unloading limestone across a harbour, and did not say
anything for four seconds.

"Which yard," she said.

"I don't know which yard. He's got a *cousin.* It's a big city, Mira,
maybe it's nothing—"

"It's never nothing," she said. "Thanks, Doiron. We're square."

"We're not square. Mira, we're not—"

"We're square," she said, and hung up, because a man who is trying to
give you something out of guilt will keep you on a telephone for eleven
minutes, and she had eleven minutes' worth of other things to do.

·

"That's the boat," Adam said.

"That's the boat."

"So it's the road."

"It's the road."

She sat down at the table under the window and put both hands flat on it,
and Adam Marek — who had spent twelve days learning this woman the way
you learn a machine you are going to have to operate — got up and put the
kettle on, and did not say a single further word for eleven minutes.

She sat with it.

The boat had been the plan since Chicago. Not a plan she had told him
about until the city limits, but the plan: the one leg of the whole
nineteen days that was hers, that came off her own list, that nobody in
the family had sold her or knew about. Four days on the water and off at
the Soo, and from the Soo everything opened.

And it was gone because a mate had taken a transfer to be nearer his
mother, which was the most ordinary sentence in the world.

That was how it always went. She had known that since she was
twenty-one. The routes never closed dramatically. They closed because
somebody's mother was ill, or a gate got a camera, or a foreman retired
and the new one kept the log properly.

"Right," she said, at eleven minutes. "Atlas."

·

They put the road atlas on the bed, because the table was too small, and
worked on it for four hours.

And it was, Mira thought later, the strangest four hours of the whole
nineteen days, because what happened in them was that she did her job in
front of another person.

She had never done that. Not once. Benny knew the yard and Benny knew
the trucks and Benny had known for eleven years that there were routes he
was better off not being told about, and the not-telling had been an act
of love in both directions.

So the planning had always happened in her own head, at four in the
morning, alone, and had come out of her mouth afterward as an
instruction.

Now there was a man kneeling on the floor beside a bed with a road atlas
open on it, and he had run fourteen trucks for eleven years, and every
forty minutes he said something that made her stop and re-do a leg.

"You're routing around Bay City."

"There's a scale."

"There's a scale that's been closed since the state budget in 2023,"
Adam said. "I know, because we routed *toward* it for two years
afterward, on purpose, because everybody else was still routing around
it. That's forty minutes back in your pocket."

She looked at him.

"Say that again."

"It's closed. Or it was in November. I'd want to see it before I bet
anything on it."

"We'll see it."

He had the same instinct she did about the third yard — that they had to
arrive at Port Huron with time, not on the clock, because a driver who
arrives at the last minute has no options and a driver who arrives early
can look at things. He disagreed with her about the second night's stop
and was right. He was wrong about the crossing at Standish and admitted
it inside two minutes, which was, she registered, the actual difference
between this man and every other man she had ever worked with.

The four hours went like this.

Leg one was the run south out of Alpena, which was easy and which she had
already driven, and which he made her change anyway: not US-23 the whole
way, because US-23 is what a person leaving Alpena does, but inland to
Hillman and down the county roads to Rose City and back out at Standish,
which cost fifty minutes and took them off the shore for a day.

"You'll hate it," Adam said. "It's slow and it's boring and there's
nowhere to get diesel."

"I'll hate it."

"Do it anyway."

Leg four was the Bay City scale, which she had been routing around for
three years and which he was right about, and which they went and looked
at on the fourteenth from a lay-by across the highway with the binoculars
out of the door pocket, and which was closed, and had plywood in the
window.

Forty minutes back in her pocket, on the word of a man who had run
freight for the people who were trying to kill him.

Leg nine was the second night, and they had the only real argument of the
four hours about it, which was that she wanted to be at Port Sanilac and
he wanted to be forty miles short of it.

"You're arriving at the last yard with sixteen hours in hand," Adam said.
"That's a Mira number. That's a *good* number. But you've built the whole
back half so that the last two days are both long, and the long ones
being at the end is how you get to the seventeenth having slept nine
hours in three days."

"I've done worse."

"I know you've done worse. That's not the same as it being right." He had
the atlas flat on the bed with his finger on Sanilac County. "Take the
tiredness early. Put the ugly legs in the middle and get down to the
Sanilac shore in daylight with two nights in hand, and sleep in a bed for
both of them, and then the seventeenth is sixty-one miles."

Mira looked at the map for a long time.

"That's better," she said.

"I know it's better."

"Don't."

"I'm going to have it," Adam said. "I've been in this truck eleven days
and I've been right about a scale house and a belt and a tail, and this
is the first one that was about *you*, and I'm going to have it."

Leg fourteen was the alternate for the alternate, which they built at two
in the afternoon out of a sense that they should, and which neither of
them expected to use, and which was the one they used.

At the end of the four hours they had seventeen legs, five days, four
alternates, and a shape.

"There it is," she said.

"There it is." Adam sat back on the floor with his back against the bed
frame. "Mira. That's a good plan."

"It's an ordinary plan."

"It's a *good* plan, and I'd like you to let me say so, because I've
watched forty men build worse ones with more resources." He tipped his
head back. "Ask me what the best part of it is."

"What's the best part of it."

"That it doesn't have a rescue in it," Adam said. "Every plan I've ever
seen a frightened man build has a moment in it where somebody arrives.
Yours doesn't. Yours is seventeen legs and a fuel schedule and a place to
put the truck at the other end."

Mira looked at the atlas.

"There's no rescue coming," she said.

"I know. That's why it's a good plan."

·

She said the thing about the boat at about four in the afternoon, when
they had been on the floor with the atlas for three hours and neither of
them had eaten.

"I want to say what the boat was."

"You don't have to."

"I know I don't have to." She had her back against the bed frame beside
him now, which had happened at some point without a decision. "The boat
was the only leg of this that came off my own list. Everything else on
that manifest was sold to me or handed to me. Three yards, a route, a
date. The boat was mine.

"And I've been carrying it since Chicago like a coin in a pocket," she
said. "Every time it got bad — Tawas, the photograph, the review — I put
my hand on it. *There's a boat on the twelfth.* It's the only thing that
made any of it survivable and I never told you about it until the city
limits, and I want you to know why."

"Because a passenger with a plan rides differently."

"Because a passenger with a plan rides differently," Mira agreed, "and
because if I'd told you and it had gone wrong, then I'd have taken
something off you, and you've had enough taken."

"And now it's gone wrong and you've told me anyway."

"Now it's gone wrong and it's a *road* problem," she said, "and road
problems are the two of us."

Adam Marek sat on the floor of a room above a chandlery with a road atlas
across his knees.

"Say that again in about four days," he said, "when you've forgotten you
said it."

·

Benny called at half past six.

"Where are you?"

"Alpena."

"Alright." A long pause, which Benny Osei did not do. "Two things and
they're both bad and I've decided to give them to you in the order that
lets you sleep."

"Give them in any order."

"The review's become a hearing," Benny said. "It's on the calendar for
the nineteenth. That's not eleven days, that's a *date*, and once
there's a date there's a docket, and once there's a docket you can't get
it off by explaining anything to anybody."

Mira closed her eyes.

"What's the second."

"There's a car outside my house."

She opened them.

"Since when."

"Since Tuesday evening." Absolutely level. "Grey, two men, they park at
the top of the street where they can see my door and they change at
about eleven. They've made no attempt whatsoever to be discreet about it
and I want you to understand that's the whole point, they're not watching
me, they're *showing* me—"

"Benny—"

"And a man came to the shed on Wednesday," Benny said. "Not to the
office. To the shed."

Everything in Mira Dalca went very cold and very quiet.

"He sat in the chair," Benny said. "The one you sit in. Very pleasant.
He asked about the trucks and he asked about the lanes and he asked how
long I'd had the licence, and then, on the way out, at the door, he
looked back at the floor and he said, *what have you got under there,
Mr. Osei?*"

The room over the chandlery, twelve by fifteen, went absolutely still.

"What did you say?"

"I said, 'a rat problem, and you're welcome to it.'" A dry sound that was
not a laugh. "He thought that was very funny."

"Benny. Listen to me. Take the tin—"

"No."

"Take the tin out from under the plank and burn it in the bin behind the
shop tonight, all of it, and then go to your sister's in—"

"No," Benny Osei said.

Mira stopped.

In eleven years he had never once refused her. He had disagreed with her
perhaps four hundred times, in the specific manner of a man who
disagrees while already doing the thing, and had never in the whole
history of their working life said the word no.

"You'll want it," Benny said.

"Benny, if he finds that tin—"

"I know exactly what's in it and I know exactly what it costs. I've known
for six years, and you've never once asked me whether I knew, and I want
you to notice that." His voice did not change. "You'll want it. Not for
them. Not as evidence, not as leverage, I know you won't spend those
people and I'd knock you down myself if you tried."

"Then why."

"Because one of these days," Benny Osei said, "somebody's going to ask
you what your life has been for, and you're going to want the list in
your hand when you answer, because you've never once been able to say it
out loud without a document."

Mira Dalca stood in a rented room with a telephone against her ear and
found that she could not speak.

"Twelve's got a noise," Benny said. "Call me Thursday."

·

They ran south on the fourteenth.

US-23 down the Huron shore, and it was the best driving of the whole
nineteen days, which she thought was probably obscene under the
circumstances and thought anyway: two hundred miles of two-lane with the
water on the left the entire way, the lake doing that thing it does in
the middle of May where it is a colour that has no name, small towns
every eleven miles with a diner and a bait shop and a sign about the
walleye.

She gave him distances all day and he took them, and by the afternoon it
had become a language.

"Two hundred and six miles."

"Okay."

"Four hours if the bridge is clear. Four and a half if it isn't."

"Okay, Mira."

At about two in the afternoon she said, "Ninety-one to the fuel," and
Adam said "Okay" without looking up from the atlas, and then, after a
moment:

"You've said four distances in twenty minutes."

She did not answer.

"You do it when you're frightened," Adam said. "You did it at the yard in
Tawas after the photograph, you did it at Portage after the walk, you're
doing it now." He closed the atlas. "It's not a criticism. I've just
worked out what it is and I wanted to tell you I know, so that you don't
have to hide it, because hiding it is more work than doing it."

Mira drove for a mile.

"What is it," she said.

"It's the answer to a question nobody asked," Adam said. "Somebody says
*are you alright*, and you can't answer that, because you've never been
allowed to have an answer to it. So you answer a different question, one
that's got a number in it, and the number's true and it's *yours*, and it
gets you through the sentence." He looked out at the lake. "My mother did
it with weather. Forty years."

Mira Dalca kept both hands on the wheel.

"Ninety-one miles to the fuel," she said.

"Okay," Adam said. "I'll be here."

·

He drove for four hours that evening, and she slept.

She had not intended to let him. It came up around Tawas, when she had
been driving for nine hours and had started making the small errors — a
late signal, a mirror check she did twice because she had not registered
the first — and he said, "Pull over and let me have it for four hours,"
and she said "No," automatically, and then sat with the no for eleven
miles and found she could not make it stand up.

Benny had driven her truck. That was the list. Benny, in 2019, when she
had the flu.

"Four hours," she said, pulling into a turnout.

"Four hours."

"You go seventy-two, not seventy-five, because the box wanders over
seventy-three in a crosswind and I don't want to wake up to it."

"Seventy-two."

"And if anything at all — anything — comes up behind us twice, you wake
me. Not once. Twice."

"Twice," Adam said. "I promise."

She woke at half past eleven with a crick in her neck and his jacket over
her.

The truck was doing exactly seventy-two. The mirrors had been moved for
him, obviously, because he was six foot two and she was not.

And they had been moved back.

Both of them, driver's and passenger's, set to her positions — not
approximately, exactly, to the degree, which meant he had noted where
they were before he touched them and had put them back while driving at
seventy-two miles an hour in the dark, at some point in the last four
hours, on purpose, so that she would not have to reach up and fix them
when she woke.

Mira Dalca lay against the window with her eyes open in the dark and did
not say anything for eleven miles.

"You're awake," Adam said eventually.

"How could you tell."

"Your breathing changed at the county line."

"You put my mirrors back."

"Yeah."

"When?"

"About an hour ago. There was a straight bit." He kept his eyes on the
road. "It's a stupid thing. I know it's a stupid thing."

"It's not a stupid thing," Mira said.

·

They stopped for the night at a cabin court outside Port Sanilac on the
fourteenth, because it was the last leg before the shape of the thing
changed, and because Mira wanted a shower and a real bed and did not want
to justify either.

Eight cabins in a row under maples, a hand-painted sign, a woman at the
office who took cash and gave them number six because it was the furthest
from the road.

Two beds. She had asked for two, at the desk, in front of him, in a flat
voice, and had noted the way he stood back and let her do it without any
commentary at all.

They ate at a table in the corner. The window was open. There was a lake
wind coming through the maples and the sound of it was, after twelve days
in a cab, extraordinary.

They had bought the food at a grocery in Port Sanilac and cooked it on two
rings, and it was pasta and a jar of sauce and a tomato Adam had cut up
with a pocket knife on a plate, and it was, Mira registered with some
irritation, the best meal she had eaten in about four years.

He had a way of laying a table. That was the thing she could not get
past. There was no reason for a man to lay a table in a cabin court
outside Port Sanilac, and he had done it anyway, and had put the two
forks on the correct sides, and had folded a paper towel under each of
them, and had not said one word about doing it.

"Your mother," she said.

"My mother." He sat down. "Forty years of laying a table for a man who
came home at eleven o'clock and ate it standing up."

"Is she—"

"2018. It was quick, which everybody said was a mercy, and which I've
never once been able to agree with." He put a paper towel straight.
"She'd have liked this. Not the circumstances. The table."

Mira ate for a while.

"Mine's alive," she said.

Adam went very still, in the specific way she had learned meant *I am
not going to move in case it stops.*

"Constanța?"

"Constanța. She's sixty-six and she has a flat on the ninth floor of a
block near the port and a balcony with tomatoes on it." She turned the
fork over. "I send money four times a year through a woman in Toronto who
takes eleven percent, and she thinks I own a shipping company, because
that's what I told her in 2014 and I have never once found the place to
put the truth in a phone call."

"Does she—"

"She knows there was a debt. She doesn't know what kind." Mira put the
fork down. "She was the one who found the man. In 2012. She sat at a
table with him and paid him four hundred euros she'd got from her sister
and she has spent fourteen years being very proud of what she did for
me, and I have spent fourteen years making sure she goes on being proud
of it, and I want you to understand that I would do that again tomorrow
and twice on Sunday."

The wind came through the maples.

"That's the arithmetic I'm actually good at," she said. "Not fuel.
That one."


And at half past nine Mira Dalca put down her fork and said:

"I want to do the terms."

Adam sat back.

"Alright."

"I'm going to be bad at this."

"You're not going to be bad at this. You're going to be *exact* at this,
and then afterward you're going to think you were bad at it, and I'm
telling you now, in advance, that you weren't."

Mira looked at him.

"That's the single most irritating thing anybody's ever said to me," she
said, "and I'd like you to know that I have written it down."

## Chapter Seven — The One Who Drove

He did not tell her at the fuel stop.

He meant to. He had sixty-one miles to build it and he built it four
different ways, and at the pump he got out and stood in the wind on the
other side of the truck and could not make any of them work, because
every one of them began with a sentence that would end the drive.

Then she came around the back of the box with a receipt in her hand and
said, "Alternator's making that sound again," and he said, "It's the belt,
not the alternator," and she said, "It's the alternator, the belt's four
months old," and they had an argument about it for eleven miles, and by
the time they were done the moment had gone by, and he let it go by, and
he knew exactly what he was doing while he did it.

So it was the shore road at Au Gres, after dark, with the engine off.

She pulled over because she had lost the argument and wanted to hear the
thing properly with the engine idling, and then she killed it entirely,
and the noise of the truck went out of the world and left them with the
lake.

Nine days. It was the ninth day.

"Belt," Adam said.

"Alternator."

"You're wrong, and I'll tell you why. If it were the alternator you'd
have a whine that changes with load, and it doesn't change with load, it
changes with *speed*, which means it's rotational and it's on the
accessory drive, and it goes away when it's damp, which is a belt."

Mira sat back.

"Where did you learn that?"

"I ran a board with fourteen trucks on it," Adam said. "I couldn't fix
one to save my life. But I sat in a shop three days a week for eleven
years with men who could, and I've got a very good memory for other
people's competence."

She looked out at the water. There was nothing out there. Somewhere north
a light was doing something regular and small.

"Say the thing," she said. "You've been carrying it since Tawas and it's
making the cab small."

·

Adam Marek put both hands on his knees.

"In 2019," he said, "November, I had a run out of the Montreal yard. I
told you about it."

"You told me."

"I told you the shape of it. I didn't tell you the rest, and I'm going to
now, and then I'm going to tell you a second thing, and I need you to let
me get to the end of both before you say anything, because if you stop me
in the middle I won't start again."

Mira turned her head.

"Alright."

"It was supposed to be machine parts out of the Montreal yard, down
through Sarnia, into Detroit. It was a run I'd done eleven times. And
about forty minutes north of the crossing I stopped for fuel and I heard
it." He was looking straight ahead through the windscreen at the dark
water. "Not a voice. There wasn't a voice. It was a *knock*. Three, then
a pause, then three. Somebody who'd worked out that a rhythm sounds
different from cargo shifting."

The lake made its noise.

"And I stood at the back of that trailer for — I don't know. It felt like
a long time and it was probably ninety seconds. And I ran the whole thing.
I ran what happens if I open it. I ran what happens if I call somebody. I
ran which of the men I worked with would have been the one to load it,
and I knew which one, and I knew what he was.

"And then I got back in the truck and I drove for six more hours and I
delivered it to a yard in Melvindale and I signed the sheet and I went
home and I slept for eleven hours."

He stopped.

"That's the doing," he said. "You told me not to do the next part, and I
respect that, and I'm not going to. But there's a fact in it that isn't
about my feelings, and the fact is that I never opened the door. I never
knew who it was. I've never known. That's not remorse, that's just an
open item, and I've had it open for seven years."

Mira did not say anything.

"Eight days later I walked into a federal building on Dearborn Street and
I said I want to talk to somebody about transport."

"Eight days."

"Eight days. I want you to have the number because everybody who hears
this story wants to make it a week or a year, and it was eight days, and
in those eight days I did nothing at all. I went to work. I ran the
board. I sent trucks out." He turned his hands over on his knees.
"That's the whole thing. That's the actual biography. Everything I've
said in six months of proffer is four hours long and it all sits on eight
days of a man going to work."

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because I'm not telling it so you'll say it's alright," Adam said. "That's
what I've done my whole life and you called it in the first two days, and
you were right, and I've been thinking about it ever since. I'm telling
you because you're the only person alive who knows what the back of that
truck is like."

He stopped.

And then, because he had said the sentence, and because the sentence had
in it a thing he had been holding for four days, he heard himself go on.

"And because of the second thing."

·

There was a long silence in the cab.

Later, both of them would be able to date the whole of the rest of their
lives to what happened in it, and neither of them would ever be able to
describe it, because what happened in it was nothing.

Mira Dalca had her left hand on the door frame and her right on the seat
back between them. Adam Marek had both hands on his knees, eleven inches
away.

Neither of them moved.

It was not restraint, exactly. It was more like standing at the top of a
flight of stairs in the dark, at the precise moment you understand there
are stairs.

"Don't tell me the second thing tonight," Mira said.

Adam looked at her.

"You want me to—"

"I want you to tell me. I'm asking you not to tell me tonight." Her voice
was not steady and she let it not be steady, which cost her more than the
sentence. "You've got a look on you like a man about to hand me a bill.
And I've had a photograph of Benny in my head since nine o'clock this
morning, and there's a review open on his licence, and I've got eight
days and a yard on a border, and I'm — "

She stopped.

"I'm at capacity," she said. "That's the accurate word. Whatever it is,
it's going to be there in three days. Give it to me at the layover, when
I'm sitting down and there's no wheel in my hands."

"Alright."

"Just tell me one thing."

"Ask it."

"Is it about me, or is it about you?"

Adam Marek was quiet for a moment.

"It's about you," he said. "And I'm in it."

Mira nodded slowly, twice, looking at the black water.

Then she started the truck.

"It's a hundred and forty miles to Alpena," she said, "and the boat's on
the twelfth, and I want a bed before then. Sleep if you can. I'll wake
you at Rogers City if the road's straight."

"Mira."

"What."

"For the record," Adam said, "and I know you don't take these, and you'll
tell me it's an observation and not a compliment. But nobody in seven
years has told me to hold on to something and *given me a day for it.*
Everybody wants it now. Cops want it now, lawyers want it now, my sister
wanted it now." He put his head back against the window. "You just gave
me three days, and I'd like to say thank you before I sleep, and you can
be as rude about it as you want."

Mira pulled out onto the shore road.

"Sixty-one miles to the alternator place," she said, "which is a belt
place," and heard him laugh, once, quietly, and drove north with her jaw
tight and something loose and hot behind her breastbone that she declined
to look at directly for a hundred and forty miles.

·

Alpena, on the tenth day.

She had been aiming at it since Chicago. It was the point of the route
that was hers and nobody else's, the one thing on the whole nineteen days
she had built rather than been handed, and she had not told him about it
because until the shore road there had been no reason to.

She told him at the city limits.

"There's a boat."

"A boat."

"A carrier. A thousand-footer out of the cement plant here — they run
limestone and clinker up and down the lakes from April to January." She
took the turn down toward the harbour. "There's a mate on one of them who
owes me from 2021. A crew berth is a bunk and a card and a name on a
crew list, and a crew list doesn't get checked the way a border does,
because a boat isn't a border, it's a workplace that happens to cross
one."

Adam sat up.

"You can put me on a boat."

"I can put you on a boat with a crew card that says you're an oiler, and
in four days you're off it at the Soo, and from the Soo you're — "

She stopped.

"Where you want to be," she said. "Wherever that is."

Which was, Adam noted, the first time in ten days she had left an
opening in a sentence for him.

"When?"

"The twelfth. Two days." She pulled in under a sodium light on a street
of low buildings by the harbour. "It's late. It should have been the
ninth. There's weather at the Soo and the whole system backs up behind
it, and a carrier that's late is late for everybody downstream, so we
wait."

"We wait where?"

She looked up through the windscreen at a building with a chandlery on
the ground floor and two windows above it, one of them lit.

"There," she said. "It's a room over a ship's store. Woman named Ruth
rents it by the week to men waiting on boats and she doesn't ask names
because half her trade can't give one." She killed the engine. "Two
nights. Maybe three."

They went up an outside stair at the side of the building.

The room was twelve by fifteen with a slope on the ceiling under the
roof, and it smelled of rope and diesel and the tar they put on rope, and
it had a hot plate and a kettle and a chair and a table under the window
that looked out over the harbour to the breakwater and the freighter
channel beyond.

And it had one bed.

Mira Dalca stood inside the door for three seconds and counted the exits.

Door. Window over the fire escape at the gable end — she crossed to it,
put a hand on the sash, tested it, found it moved. Two.

Then she turned around and Adam Marek was standing in the middle of the
room with his bag on his shoulder, watching her.

"You always do that," he said.

"Do what."

"Count the doors." He set the bag down. "Every room. The office in your
yard on the first morning, every fuel stop, that motel in Standish, this.
It takes you about three seconds and you do it before you do anything
else."

Mira looked at him.

"So do you," she said.

"Yeah," Adam said. "I know."

And neither of them said anything about the bed for another four hours.

## Chapter Three — The Way They Count

They came back through Portage on the third day, because the manifest
said so.

Adam had not understood that at first — why a route from Chicago to the
Ontario border should double back on itself two hundred miles into the
run — and had spent most of day two working it out, and by the time they
turned south off I-94 he had it, and hated it.

It was not a route. It was a *ledger*. Every yard on the manifest was a
place where somebody looked at the truck and made a phone call, and the
phone calls were the product: three yards over nineteen days, at fixed
windows, on a fixed schedule, so that when it was over there would be a
sequence of independent human beings who could say *yes, the Dalca woman
came through, on time, with the load, exactly like she was supposed to.*

They were not tracking her. They were *witnessing* her. Building a
record. Which meant that somewhere at the end of it there was going to be
a need for a record.

He kept that to himself too.

The second visit ran differently from the first, in the way that all
second visits run differently, and Adam noticed every part of it because
noticing was, at this point, the entire content of his life.

The scale-house man came out before they stopped rolling.

That was the first thing. On the first visit he had made them wait eleven
minutes, which was correct — you make a driver wait, always, on principle,
because a driver who is not made to wait starts to think of himself as a
guest. On the second he was out the door with his clipboard while the air
was still bleeding off the brakes.

The second thing was that he did not look at the paperwork.

Mira handed the second page in through the window and the man took it and
folded it in half without reading it and put it in his back pocket, which
was a thing that no yard man on earth does with a document he intends to
copy a number off.

The third thing was that he walked the truck again.

Same walk. Down the driver's side at that unhurried, professionally bored
pace, a hand on the box at the halfway mark the way you'd steady yourself
on a fence you did not need steadying on. At the rear he stopped.

And this time Adam Marek watched him in the mirror and counted, because a
number is the only thing you can do anything with.

Six seconds at the rear of the box. He did not look at the doors. He did
not look at the seal, and there was a seal, and a yard man who is
checking a load looks at the seal within the first two seconds because
that is what a seal is for.

He looked past the truck, through the fence, at the slip.

Eleven seconds.

Then he turned around and came up the passenger side, and Adam kept his
eyes forward and let the man look at the side of his face, and heard him
say something about the wind, and heard Mira answer something about the
wind, and then the man went back inside the scale house.

He was in there for four minutes. When he came out he did not have the
clipboard.

·

Mira climbed up and shut the door and sat for a second with her hand on
the key.

"Say it," she said.

"Say what?"

"You've had a face on you since Michigan City and you've got a worse one
now." She turned the key. "You've been sitting there working on something
for two days like a man doing sums under a desk. Say it or stop doing it,
because I can't drive with somebody thinking that loud beside me."

Adam looked out at the fence going past.

He had planned this. He had had eleven hours to plan it and he had
planned it as carefully as he had ever planned anything, and every
version he built came apart on the same problem, which was that he was a
man in her custody and everything he said was contaminated by that fact.

*He's going to kill me* is what a frightened passenger says. It is also,
sometimes, true, and the trouble is that the sentence is identical in
both cases and there is no way to tell them apart from the outside.

So he did the only thing he had left, which was to give her the
observation and not the conclusion.

"He didn't look at the seal," Adam said.

Mira's hand stopped on the shifter.

"What?"

"Twice now. Both walks. He came down the driver's side, he stopped at the
back of the box for six seconds, and he never once looked at the seal."
Adam kept his voice absolutely level. "The first time I thought I'd
missed it. I didn't miss it."

"There's no load. He knows there's no load."

"Then why walk the truck at all?" He turned his head. "You don't walk a
truck for the pleasure of walking it. You walk it to check the load, the
plates, the tag and the seal. If there's no load, there's nothing to
check, so you don't walk it — you take the paper, you make your phone
call, you go back inside where it's warm. That's the whole job. I ran
that job for eleven years."

Mira sat very still.

"So what was he doing."

"He was looking at the water," Adam said.

He watched her take it. He watched her *not* take it — watched a woman
who had spent a decade building a professional apparatus specifically
designed to prevent her from having thoughts like this one put her hands
back on the wheel and pull out of the gate at a steady eight miles an
hour.

"That's a walk and a look," she said. "That's not a fact."

"No. It's not."

"People look at water."

"They do."

"It's the *lake*, Marek. There's water on every side of everything for
four hundred miles."

"That's right," Adam said. "And I've been in forty yards that had water
next to them, and I know exactly what it looks like when a man is
enjoying a view, and exactly what it looks like when he's measuring a
distance, and I'm telling you what I saw. That's all I'm telling you. I'm
not telling you what it means."

She drove.

She got them onto the ramp and up to speed and out into the flat
grey-green of northern Indiana, and for eleven minutes she said nothing
at all, and then, at the interchange, she did a thing that Adam Marek
would remember for the rest of his life, because it was the first
evidence he had that he was in the truck of a person and not the truck of
a policy.

The manifest said the day's fuel stop was the Pilot at exit 34.

She went past exit 34 at seventy miles an hour, took 39, came off, ran
four miles north on a county road, and fuelled at an independent station
with two pumps and a hand-lettered sign, where a woman in her seventies
took cash and did not have a camera.

She did not explain it.

Adam did not ask.

They stood on either side of the truck in the wind while it filled, and
neither of them said one word about what had just happened, and both of
them understood that they had just done something together.

·

She waited until he was asleep.

It took until the second night — he did not sleep easily, and she had
noticed that he only went under when the road noise was constant, which
meant a long flat stretch, which meant she had to want it and wait for
it. She got it at half past eleven on a straight run of US-31 north of
Holland, and when his breathing changed she reached over and turned the
dash light down to nothing and pulled off into the dark apron of a closed
farm-equipment dealership.

She left the engine running so the sound would not change.

Then she got out the manifest.

She had read it four times. She had read it the way you read a route
sheet — for the yards, the windows, the contact names, the crossing. She
had not read it the way she read everything else in her life, which was
as a document produced by a person with an intention, and now she sat
under the map light with a pen and a fuel receipt and did the thing she
had done since she was twenty-one and Ted Wisniewski had taught her, on
the theory that a business is not a business until somebody in it can
read.

Page four was the fuel and mileage allowance.

It was, she registered again, unusually detailed. Not a lump sum. A
schedule: a per-leg allowance, out and back, with tolerances, in the
style of a company that has been reimbursing drivers for forty years and
has stopped trusting any of them.

Out: Chicago to Portage to the shoreline route to the crossing to the
lay-over to the border yard. Nineteen days, with the waits built in.

Back: Port Huron to Chicago. Three hundred and forty-one miles.

She checked the outbound allowance against her own numbers, because she
had run every foot of that road and knew what the truck did on it. It
came out within four percent, which was fine, which was normal, which was
a real number written by a real person who knew trucks.

Then she checked the return.

And it was wrong.

Not wildly. Not by an amount anybody would ever query. She sat with the
pen in her hand and worked it a second time on the back of the receipt,
in the small square figures she had been making since a school in
Constanța, and got the same answer, and then worked it a third time
because the answer had to be wrong.

The return allowance was low.

Three hundred and forty-one miles at the rate they had used for the
outbound legs came to a number. The number on the page was less than
that, by an amount that a person who did not do this for a living would
never notice — call it four percent the other way, call it eleven
dollars.

Eleven dollars.

She sat in a dark truck on the apron of a farm-equipment dealership with
a fuel receipt on her knee and eleven dollars of arithmetic in front of
her, and understood that she was looking at the whole thing.

Because the outbound rate had been calculated for a loaded truck.

And the return had been calculated for an empty one.

That was the four percent. That was the eleven dollars. Somebody in an
office had sat down with a rate card and worked out, honestly and
carefully and with the diligence of a man who takes pride in his
paperwork, that on the way out the vehicle would be carrying a payload of
roughly one hundred and ninety pounds, and that on the way back it would
not be.

Mira Dalca looked at the number for a long time.

Then she looked at the passenger seat, where a thirty-four-year-old man
who had lost eighteen pounds in six months slept badly with his head
against the window.

She did the arithmetic once more, because she was not a woman who
accepted a total she had run only three times.

It came out the same.

She got a lighter out of the door pocket and burned the fuel receipt in
the ashtray, which had never been used, and watched it go, and the smell
of it was strong and wrong in a cab that had never had a smoker in it.

Adam moved in his sleep and said something that was not a word.

"Sleep," Mira said, quietly, in Romanian, which she had not spoken aloud
to another human being in four years.

Then she put the truck in gear.

She drove north for two hours with both hands on the wheel and her jaw
set, past the exits she had planned to take, past the motel she had
budgeted for, and somewhere around one in the morning, north of Muskegon,
in the dark, she began — for the first time since a container ship in
2012 — to make a plan that had nobody's permission in it at all.

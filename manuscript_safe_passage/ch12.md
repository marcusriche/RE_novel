## Chapter Twelve — The Twelfth Name

She called Benny from a payphone outside a hardware store in Lexington on
the fifteenth, because the burner had started making a sound she did not
like and because there are moments when a woman wants a telephone that is
bolted to a wall.

It rang eleven times.

She was about to hang up when the line opened and there was a room behind
it — not the shed, not the shop. A room with other people's conversations
in it and a woman's voice somewhere saying a number.

"Benny."

"Ah," he said. "Good."

"Where are you?"

"I'm in the hallway outside a hearing room in Springfield," Benny Osei
said, "which is not where I'm meant to be until the nineteenth, but they
moved the pre-hearing conference up to today, which I found out at nine
o'clock last night."

Mira put her free hand flat on the cold metal of the phone housing.

"They moved it up."

"They moved it up." A pause. "I've got a fellow here from a firm on
Wacker who I could not possibly afford and who told me at eight this
morning that his retainer's been taken care of, and when I asked by whom
he did the thing lawyers do with their faces."

"Benny—"

"I know. I know exactly what it is." He said it evenly. "Somebody's put a
very good lawyer in my corner so that I understand precisely how much
better my day could go if the right people are pleased with the road
situation. It's rather elegant, actually. Nobody's threatened me with
anything at all. They've just made my problem *solvable* and left it
lying there."

The hardware store's sign creaked in the wind off the lake.

"They took the file," Benny said.

"What?"

"The licence file. The physical one, from the yard. Wednesday. Two men
with a state letterhead that I checked and which was real, because that's
the other thing, Mira, everything they're doing is real — it's a real
review off a real discrepancy and it'll be a real hearing with a real
officer." A short breath. "That's what I keep — there's nothing to fight.
There's no lie in it anywhere. There's just a set of true things arranged
into a shape."

"The man in the shed," Mira said. "Wednesday. Has he been back?"

"He has not been back," Benny said, "and I am going to tell you the rest
of that and then I want you to say nothing for a moment, because you'll
want to say something and it won't help."

"Tell me."

"He sat in your chair," Benny said. "Not mine — yours, the one by the
board, and he sat in it the way a man sits in a chair he's decided
about. And he asked me about the trucks, and he asked me about the lanes,
and he asked how long I'd had the authority. All perfectly ordinary. And
then on the way out he stopped at the door and looked back at the floor
and said, 'What have you got under there, Mr. Osei?'"

Mira closed her eyes.

"The plank," she said.

"The plank."

"He knows."

"He doesn't know," Benny said. "If he knew he'd have taken it, and I'd
have been in a room with the state and the family both, and my licence
would be the least of anybody's difficulty. He was *fishing*. He fished
badly, because he's about thirty and he wanted me to see how clever he
was." A dry sound. "But he'll fish again with somebody older, Mira, and
that man won't ask, he'll just come at four in the morning with a
crowbar."

She stood at a payphone outside a hardware store and did the arithmetic,
which was very short.

"Take it out and burn it," she said. "Tonight. In the bin behind the
shop where we do the rags. Every page. Then go to your sister's in
Evanston and don't sleep at the house."

"No," Benny Osei said.

Mira opened her eyes.

"What?"

"No," he said again. "Not the second part — I'll go to Adaeze's, I'm not
a fool and there's a car at the top of my street. The first part. No."

In eleven years he had disagreed with her four hundred times, always
while already doing the thing. He had never said the word.

"Benny, if that tin—"

"I know what's in the tin," he said.

The line hummed.

"I've known since about 2020," Benny said, "and you have never once asked
me whether I knew, and I want you to notice that, because I've been
noticing it for six years."

"Benny—"

"I know that there are eleven names in it and I know approximately what
they are, because I am not a stupid man and I have been running a yard
for you for eleven years and I have watched you come back from four runs
in seven years looking like a woman who had done something." His voice
did not change at all. "And every year twice I have told you to burn it,
and I want to tell you why I'm not telling you today."

"Because it's evidence—"

"It is not evidence and you will never spend it," Benny said, "and if you
tried I'd knock you down myself, and we both know that isn't the question.

"I'm not telling you to burn it because one of these days somebody is
going to ask you what your life has been for."

The wind came along the storefronts.

"And you are going to want that list in your hand when you answer,"
Benny said, "because I have known you for eleven years and you have never
once, not one single time, been able to say a true thing about yourself
out loud without a document in front of you."

Mira Dalca stood at a payphone in Lexington, Michigan, and could not
speak.

"There," Benny said. "That's the whole of it and I've been wanting to say
it since about 2021." A door opened somewhere behind him and a voice said
a name that was not his. "I'm going in. It'll be twenty minutes and it'll
be nothing — it's a scheduling conference, they'll set the date they've
already set."

"Benny."

"Twelve's got a noise," he said. "Call me Thursday," and was gone.

·

She did not go back to the truck for eleven minutes.

She stood in the wind at the end of a block in a lake town of nine
hundred people with her hands in her pockets and looked at nothing, and
what went through her head was not Benny and was not the hearing.

It was a sentence.

*Somebody is going to ask you what your life has been for.*

·

They ran south down the shore that afternoon with the lake on the left
and neither of them said very much.

At about two she said, "Ask me about the tin," and Adam said, "No," and
she drove for four miles and then said, "Why not," and he said:

"Because you've spent eleven days deciding what to hand me and you've
never once got it wrong, and I'm not going to start taking things off you
now."

"That's not an answer, that's a policy."

"It's the same policy you run," Adam said. "You just don't like it from
this side."

Mira Dalca drove for a while with her jaw set.

"There are eleven names in it," she said.

"I know."

"You don't know. You've guessed. There's a difference and I've been the
difference for fourteen years." She checked the mirror. "Eleven names,
eleven dates, eleven places, and a word at the end of each one, and the
word is the same word every time and I'm not going to say it to you in a
truck."

"Alright."

"Ask me the question you actually want to ask."

Adam looked out at the water.

"Are you on it," he said.

The truck went four hundred yards.

"No," Mira said.

"Okay."

"That's the whole architecture, Marek. That's the thing I built. There's
them and there's me. There's eleven people in a tin and then there's the
woman with the keys, and the woman with the keys is not on any list
anywhere, because a carrier who's on the manifest isn't a carrier."

"Okay," Adam said again, in exactly the voice of a man who has been told
a number he does not believe and has decided not to say so, and she heard
it, and drove for eleven miles being furious about it, and at the end of
the eleven miles could not have said which of them she was furious with.

·

Adam gave her the number on the shore road, forty miles further south,
sitting in the passenger seat with a fuel receipt on his knee and a pen
he had taken out of the door pocket.

"I want to give you something and I want to say a few things about it
first."

"Say them."

"It's the only thing I own," Adam said. "That's not a figure of speech. I
have got a jacket that doesn't fit, a testimony date, and this."

He wrote on the back of the receipt in a driver's blocky capitals: ten
digits, and under them, a name.

"That's the duty line at the Northern District," he said. "It's twenty-four
hours and it's answered by a human being, which is rarer than you'd
think. And that name is the AUSA who's had my file since November."

Mira glanced at it.

"Raman."

"Priya Raman. She built the whole Moretti prosecution off—" he stopped.
"You'll have read about it. Everybody read about it."

"The bookkeeper," Mira said.

"The bookkeeper." He turned the receipt over in his fingers. "Raman
doesn't lose things. I've sat across a table from her three times and she
has never once been in a hurry and she has never once asked me a question
she didn't already know the answer to. She's the only person in that
building I'd put anything in the hands of."

He held it out.

Mira did not take it.

"That's your line," she said. "That's the thing that gets you to the
nineteenth."

"It's the thing that gets *somebody* to the nineteenth," Adam said,
"and if it goes wrong at that yard on the seventeenth it won't be me, and
then that number is the only part of me that's left."

She took the receipt.

She looked at it for a while, and then she did something she did not
plan: she folded it in half and put it inside her coat, in the inner
pocket, over her ribs — which was, precisely, where the coffee tin would
have gone if the coffee tin had been in the truck.

Adam watched her do it.

Neither of them said anything about it.

"You didn't say the rest," Mira said, eventually.

"What rest?"

"You said it's the thing that gets somebody to the nineteenth."

Adam Marek looked out at the water.

"It's a phone number," he said. "Anybody can call a phone number. That's
all I'm going to say about it, because the alternative is that I sit here
and tell you what to do with your own life, and I've got a term about
that and I'd like to be able to say I kept it."

She drove for eleven miles.

"Say it anyway," she said.

"No."

"Marek—"

"No," Adam said. "You're going to get to it. You get to everything about
four days before I do and then you sit on it, and I've been watching you
do that for two weeks, and I'm not going to take it off you by saying it
first."

·

She got to it at dawn on the seventeenth, in a lay-by above the water
south of Lakeport, with sixty-one miles left.

She had not slept. She had got up at four out of the passenger seat where
she had been not-sleeping and had taken the road atlas and a pen out to a
picnic table on the bluff, and had sat there in the grey with her coat
buttoned wrong, and had written the list out from memory.

She did it because she wanted to know whether she still could. She had
not looked at the paper in the tin since February. Eleven entries — name,
date, place, route, and the word at the end.

She wrote them in order. Her hand did not shake, which she noted with
professional interest.

*1. F. and son, 9, Kosovar. Aug 2019. Melvindale yard. The Coyle cut.
Alive.*

*2. A. and D., brothers, Bijeljina. Apr 2021. Toledo–Fort Erie. Alive.*

*3. Woman, 40s, Guatemala City. Nov 2021. The Portage yard. The sand
road. Alive.*

*4. Man, 30s, Hargeisa. Mar 2022. Melvindale yard. The Coyle cut. Alive.*

*5. Woman, 20s, and her sister, Chișinău. Jul 2022. The Portage yard. The
sand road. Alive.*

*6. Man, Ho Chi Minh City, name unknown to me. Oct 2022. The Portage yard
— Thunder Bay via the Soo. Cried four hours in the floor and said thank
you in a language I don't have. Alive.*

*7. Man, 30s, name he gave me was not his. Feb 2023. The Portage yard —
the Soo. Alive. (See the Hammond paper, June.)*

*8. Woman, 19, Tirana. Aug 2023. The sand road. Alive.*

*9. Woman, 40s, and daughter, 18, Kharkiv. Nov 2023. The Portage yard —
Fort Erie, false floor. Alive.*

*10. Man, 50s, Fujian. Jun 2025. Toledo–Fort Erie. Alive.*

*11. Two brothers, Ecuador. Oct 2025. Melvindale yard. The Coyle cut.
Alive.*

She wrote all eleven. It took nine minutes. Number seven was the one that
always cost, because number seven had a name she had learned four months
afterward from a newspaper and had added to the list in a different pen.

Then she got to the bottom of the column, and stopped.

She sat with the pen in her hand above the ruled line under the eleventh
entry and could not make the page do what it was supposed to do.

Because the arithmetic would not close.

She had been staring at it for perhaps ninety seconds before it arrived,
and when it arrived it did not arrive as a thought. It arrived the way a
column arrives when you have added it four times and it has been wrong
four times and then on the fifth time you see the transposed digit in the
third line and the whole page reorganizes itself around it in one motion.

*2012. Constanța. Nineteen years old. A container to Toronto and a truck
to Chicago. Eleven thousand dollars, priced against future work, by a
person she never saw again.*

Mira Dalca sat at a picnic table above Lake Huron at four-forty in the
morning on the seventeenth of May and wrote, on the twelfth line, in her
own handwriting:

*12. M.D., 19, Constanța. Apr 2012. Toronto–Chicago. Alive.*

And then she put the pen down and put both hands flat on the table on
either side of the atlas and did not move for a long time.

·

She heard him come up the path from the truck at about five. He stopped a
few feet away and did not come closer, and after a moment sat down on the
other end of the bench, facing out at the water, so that they were side
by side and not opposite.

He did not look at the page.

"There are twelve," Mira said.

Adam Marek did not ask what she meant.

"Yes," he said.

The water did what it does at that hour, which is almost nothing.

"How long have you known that," she said.

"Since the breakwater. Since about four seconds after I said it out
loud." He had his elbows on his knees. "I didn't say it because it isn't
mine to say. It's yours, and it's the only thing in this whole business
that is, and if I'd handed it to you on a breakwater you'd have taken it
from me and it would have been a thing a man told you."

She looked at the twelve lines.

"Fourteen years," she said.

"Yeah."

"Fourteen years I have got up at four in the morning and walked a fence
line and counted two gates, and I have paid a debt every single day of
it — not in money, they never wanted money, that's the — " She stopped.
"That's the whole design. They never once asked me for eleven thousand
dollars. If they'd asked I'd have paid it in 2016 and been finished. They
wanted *the debt*, not the money. A debt's an asset. Money's just money."

"I know," Adam said. "I loaded them."

"And I've spent fourteen years being extremely careful," Mira said,
"about the difference between the people I moved and the people who moved
me. Extremely careful. It's the whole architecture. There's *them* — the
eleven, in a tin, in a shed, itemized in my own hand — and then there's
me, who's the carrier."

She put one finger on the twelfth line.

"And I've been on the manifest the entire time."

·

The sun came up somewhere behind the cloud, which is what a sunrise on
that lake usually is.

"Say the rest of it," Adam said.

"There isn't a rest of it."

"There's a rest of it. You've had it since you put the pen down."

Mira Dalca sat with her hands flat on either side of a road atlas.

"A debt for something that was done to you," she said, "is not a debt."

The wind came up off the water.

"That's it," she said. "That's the whole sentence. It took me fourteen
years and a picnic table. Nobody carried me — I was nineteen and I was
*taken*, on a boat, in a box, and I was priced by a man I never met, and
at no point in the entire transaction was I asked anything, and there is
no arrangement in law or God or arithmetic under which a person can be
made to owe a fee for their own abduction."

"No," Adam said. "There isn't."

"Which means there has never been anything to discharge," she said. "Not
in 2012 and not now and not on the seventeenth at eight o'clock in the
evening. There's no debt. There has never been a debt. There has been a
man with a story about a debt and a woman who believed him for fourteen
years, and I've built eleven acres and forty trailers and a whole
personality on top of a thing that was never true."

She looked at him.

"And I still have to drive into that yard tonight," she said, "because
Benny Osei's licence hearing is on the nineteenth."

"Yes," Adam said. "You do."

"So what good is it," Mira said. "What actual good is it, knowing? It
doesn't move one single thing on this road."

Adam Marek sat on a picnic table bench above Lake Huron on the seventeenth
of May and looked out at the water for a while.

"It changes what you're prepared to spend," he said.

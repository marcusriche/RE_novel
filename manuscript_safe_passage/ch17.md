## Chapter Seventeen — Safe Passage

He came to the gate on foot at a quarter to eleven at night, from a bus.

That was the argument. He had spent most of the day constructing better
ones and had thrown all of them away on the Halsted platform at about
nine o'clock, because they were all speeches, and because he had realized
somewhere between the Loop and Eighty-seventh Street that the only
statement available to him that could not be misread was arriving on his
own legs at a gate nobody had driven him to.

Nineteen days in a passenger seat. Six months in a rented house. Eleven
years before that in the cab of somebody's truck or the office of
somebody's yard, and every single mile of it in a vehicle that belonged
to an organization.

So: a bus, then the Red Line, then another bus, then eleven blocks along
Torrence Avenue in the dark past the tank farms and the rail spur, with
the fog coming up off the Calumet and the sodium lights making their
orange holes in it, and the sound of the interstate a mile off like
water.

It took him two hours and forty minutes. He had checked the distance
before he set out — twenty-one miles — and had done it in his head the
whole way as a fuel calculation out of pure habit, and had laughed once,
out loud, alone, on a bus on Ninety-fifth Street.

The gate was a chain-link slider on a track with a padlock and a sign
that said WISNIEWSKI CARTAGE and under it, in smaller letters, NO
ADMITTANCE WITHOUT AUTHORIZATION.

Adam Marek stood outside it and did not go in.

There was a light on in the office, four hundred feet away across the
gravel. He could see her through the window: sitting at the desk with the
board behind her, doing paperwork at eleven o'clock at night, because of
course she was.

He put his hands on the fence.

Then he took them off, because a man's hands on a fence at eleven at
night is a shape he did not want to be, and he stood back from it and
called her name once, in an ordinary voice, across four hundred feet of
gravel.

"Mira."

·

She came out fast. He saw the door go and heard the gravel and she was
across a third of the yard before she had properly registered that
nothing was on fire, and he stood at the gate and let her come, and did
not say anything to slow her down, because he had spent nineteen days
learning that this woman moved first and processed afterward and that the
moving was the tell.

She stopped on the other side of the fence.

"How did you get here?"

"Bus," Adam said.

"From the—"

"From Dirksen to the Red Line to the ninety-five bus to the eleven blocks
up Torrence." He put his hands in his pockets. "Two hours forty. I
brought nothing. Nobody drove me and nobody knows I'm here and I have got
a receipt for a bus fare in my pocket, and I would like you to understand
that all of that is on purpose and is, in fact, the entire content of
what I came to say."

Mira Dalca stood on her own side of her own gate.

"Say it anyway."

"I'm going to. I want to do it out here first."

"Adam, it's eleven o'clock, come inside—"

"No," Adam said, "and this is the last time I'll be difficult about a
door, I promise you. I want to say it from out here because out here I
haven't been let in yet, and the whole point is that I'm asking."

She stood very still.

"Alright," she said. "Ask."

·

"On the eighteenth, on the breakwater, I withdrew it," Adam said. "All of
it. Everything we agreed at that table in the cabin court on the
fourteenth.

"I want to say first that I meant it and I'd do it again and I'm not
apologizing for it. I was a man who had been in somebody's custody for
nineteen days — yours, and before that the government's, and before that
theirs — and a man in custody can't agree to anything. He can only be
relieved. And you have built your entire life out of not being able to
tell those two things apart, and I was not going to be one more person
who blurred it for you at six in the morning while you were still cold."

"I know," Mira said. "I got that at the time. I got it about four hours
later."

"Good."

"It was still the worst thing anybody's ever done to me."

"I know that too," Adam said. "I've had two days of knowing it."

The fog moved along the fence line.

"I've been a free man for about eleven hours," he said. "That's the
count. I gave my name to a court at ten in the morning on the nineteenth
and I walked out of that building at four in the afternoon and stood on
Dearborn for a while and nobody in the world had any claim on me at all.
No agreement, no protection order, no debt, no route, no seat. Nothing.
Eleven hours."

"And?"

"And I want to say it again from here," Adam said. "From this side of
your fence, at night, on my own legs, with nothing on me. That's the
only place it counts. Everything I said to you in that truck I said
sitting in a seat you were driving, and I'm not letting one word of it
stand."

Mira Dalca put both hands on the chain-link.

"Say it, then."

"I'd like the terms back," Adam said. "All of them. And I'd like to widen
one."

·

He had worked out the wording on a bus.

"On the fourteenth we agreed to a set of conditions and every one of them
had a fence round it," he said. "In the cab. Not in front of anybody.
Nothing on the road. Nothing at a yard. And that was right — that was
exactly right for two people in the middle of a delivery, and I'd sign it
again for those nineteen days.

"I don't want it now," Adam said. "I want it anywhere. In front of
anybody. In a yard, in a diner, in a corridor at the Dirksen building
with four lawyers watching, at Benny Osei's kitchen table, at the gate,
in daylight.

"I've spent my whole life being moved quietly," he said. "Eleven years of
driving loads that weren't on any manifest, seven years of being a man
with a different name in a rented house, nineteen days in a passenger
seat with the window up. I've never once had a thing about me that
happened where people could see it. And I'd like this one to be that,
and I'd like it to be that from the start, and I'd like it to be that
even if the answer's no, because you should get to say no in daylight
too."

The fog came along the fence.

"That's the whole ask," Adam said. "Scope: anywhere. Everything else the
same as the fourteenth. You can take a week."

Mira Dalca stood with her fingers through the chain-link.

"No," she said.

Adam went absolutely still.

"Not *no* — Christ." She closed her eyes for a second. "That was — I've
been doing that my whole life, I lead with the word and then I put the
sentence after it. No, I'm not taking a week."

"Ah."

"Give me a minute. I have to do this the unfluent way and I've been
practicing since Sunday and I'm still bad at it."

"Take the minute," Adam said.

·

She took about forty seconds.

"Here are my terms," Mira said. "Yes to all of yours and I'll say that
first so it isn't hanging over the rest, and yes to anywhere, and I want
you to know that the anywhere is the part I'd never have asked for and
the part I want most.

"Now mine. Three."

"Go."

"One." She held up a finger against the fence. "I don't want to route
you. I want to *know* you. And I have to tell you that I do not know how
to do that, and I'm not being modest, I mean it as a technical statement:
I have never in my adult life known a single person and let them know me
at the same time. I've got Benny, and Benny got there by standing next to
me for eleven years and not asking. That's my entire body of experience.

"So I'm going to be bad at it," she said. "For about a year. I've done
the arithmetic and a year is what I'd budget. I'm going to give you
distances when you ask me how I am. I'm going to decide something in a
diner and not tell you. I'm going to do it *again*, Adam, that's not a
theory, that's a forecast — and when I do, I need you to say the word and
not carry it for four days being decent about it. The clause holds. But
you have to enforce it, because I won't see it."

"Agreed," Adam said. "That's fair."

"Two." Second finger. "I'm going to be in court for four years. Traversa's
in the spring, and then the transport counts, and then whatever's after
that, and I'll be doing it under my own name with my whole history read
out loud by strangers in rooms with no windows. Some of it's going to be
in the papers. All of it is going to be true and none of it is going to
be flattering and I am not going to be a good person to be near in
February."

"I've been the witness," Adam said. "I know exactly what February is
like. Next."

"Three." She stopped.

"Three," Adam said.

"Three is a job," Mira Dalca said, "and I want you to hear that it's not
the third thing, it's the first thing, and I put it last because I'm a
coward about the order of a list."

·

"I filed for authority in my own name last night," she said. "Benny
signed as sponsor. It'll take about ninety days and then this yard runs
on my licence, which it has never done, in eleven years.

"When it does I'm short a dispatcher, because Benny's fifty-eight with a
bad hip and I've been working him six days a week since 2015 and I would
like him to be alive in 2040." She had her hand flat on the fence now.
"So there's a board with eleven trucks on it, and it's going to be
fourteen by the autumn, and every one of them legal and boring and
carrying steel and produce and nothing else, and I need somebody who can
run it who understands lanes.

"I can't pay you what you're worth for about two years. The hours are
bad. It's four in the morning and the heater doesn't work and the man in
the shed will disagree with you about everything for the first six months
on principle."

She stopped.

"That's the offer," she said. "Six a.m. Wednesday. Bring a coat you can
sleep in."

Adam Marek stood on the other side of a chain-link gate in the fog on
Torrence Avenue and put his head back and laughed — properly, the way he
had not done in seven years, so that it carried across the gravel and
came back off the shed.

"What," Mira said.

"You did it as a delivery term."

"I—"

"Six a.m. Wednesday, bring a coat you can sleep in." He wiped his eyes
with the back of his wrist. "That's the same grammar as *nothing I carry
gets left on the road*. That's the same woman in the same yard in
Portage, Indiana, on the second of May."

"It's a job offer."

"Mira." Adam put both hands on the fence. "Is it the job or the other
thing?"

And Mira Dalca — who had spent fourteen years fluent in gates and fuel
and distances and mute in every other language available to a human being
— stood in her own yard, under her own lights, and made herself say it in
the plain way.

"It's both," she said. "It's both, and I did it in a delivery term
because that's the only register I've got, and I told you I'd be bad at
this and here we are eleven seconds in.

"The job is real and I need a dispatcher. And I want you to come at six
in the morning on Wednesday and I want you to be here on Thursday and I
want you to still be here in about forty years, and I do not have a
sentence for that, Adam, I've been trying to build one since Sunday and
every one I make comes out as freight."

"That one was fine," Adam said.

"That one was terrible."

"That one was the best sentence anybody's ever said to me," Adam Marek
said, "and I'd like you to open the gate."

·

She opened the gate.

It ran on its track the way it had run since 1961, badly, with the noise
it made in cold weather that Benny had been going to fix since 2019, and
it took her both hands and a shove at the end, and Adam Marek walked into
Wisniewski Cartage on the nineteenth of May at eleven minutes past
eleven at night, on foot, carrying nothing, with nobody's manifest on
him.

He stopped just inside.

"Ask me," Mira said.

"I've got a standing grant. You said anywhere."

"Ask me anyway. I'd like to hear one."

"May I," Adam said.

"Yes."

·

Afterward, neither of them was able to give a coherent account of the
next few minutes, and both of them tried, separately, years later, and
the accounts did not match on a single detail except two.

The first was that it happened in the middle of the yard, in the open, in
the gravel, forty feet inside the gate, under the light pole with the
working lamp, where anybody coming along Torrence Avenue could see it,
and that this was deliberate on both sides and neither of them ever said
so out loud.

The second was that at some point she started shaking and could not stop
and was extremely angry about it, and that he said, into her hair, "It's
just the adrenaline coming off, it took me eleven days in 2019," and she
said, "Shut up, Marek," and did not let go.

·

There was a third thing, which neither of them put in either account,
because it did not occur to either of them that it was unusual.

At some point Adam Marek said, "How far is it?"

And Mira Dalca, with her face against his collarbone in the gravel of her
own yard at eleven o'clock at night, said, "Twenty-one miles," without
having to think about it, and then, after a second, "Two hours forty on a
Tuesday. Nearer three if you miss the ninety-five bus."

"I did miss the ninety-five bus."

"Then it was three hours."

"It was three hours and eleven minutes," Adam said, "and I'd like it
minuted that I did every single one of them on purpose," and she said,
"Noted," into his shirt, and neither of them moved for a while longer.

·

They sat in the office afterward with the door open and the fog coming in
and drank tea out of two mugs that said things about trucking.

The board was up. Eleven trucks. Four out.

"That one's got a noise," Adam said, looking at it.

"Twelve," Mira said. "Yes. Everybody knows. It's had a noise since 2021
and Benny brings it up eleven times a week and I have personally driven
it four hundred miles listening for the noise and there is no noise."

"There's a noise."

"There is no noise."

"Mira," Adam Marek said, "I have run fourteen trucks for eleven years and
I am telling you that when a man who has run a yard for you since 2015
says a truck has got a noise, the truck has got a noise, and on Wednesday
morning at six o'clock I am going to find it."

Mira Dalca sat behind a desk in a shed on Torrence Avenue at midnight and
looked at a man who had arrived on a bus.

"Wednesday," she said.

"Wednesday."

·

At about half past midnight he asked her about the tin, because it was
the only thing left in the room that had not been said.

"It's gone."

"Gone how?"

"Bin behind the shop, with the oil rags, at half past ten." She had her
hands round a mug. "Benny stood there and I read all twelve out loud
first and it took nine minutes and then we burned it, and he fished the
tin out with a bar because tin doesn't burn, and put it in the skip."

Adam Marek sat with that for a while.

"Twelve."

"Twelve."

"You read yourself out."

"I read myself out," Mira said. "Out loud. To a man in a shed. And I want
to tell you what it was like, because you'll ask me in about a year
anyway and I'd rather do it now while I'm still able to."

"Tell me."

"It was boring." She turned the mug around. "That's the whole report. I'd
built it up for fourteen years into a thing that would take the roof off,
and it was eleven names and a date and a place and a word, and it took
nine minutes, and Benny didn't say anything, and at the end of it I said
*alive* and then I said *that's the last one* and he said *that's the
last one* and we went outside and set fire to a piece of paper in a bin."

She looked up.

"And then I went in and filled out a form."

"That's the part that got you."

"That's the part that got me," Mira said. "There's a box on it that says
DATE OF ENTRY INTO THE UNITED STATES and I sat and looked at it for about
four minutes, and there was nothing to put in it except April 2012, and
so I wrote April 2012, and it's going to be read by a clerk in
Springfield who won't look at it twice."

Adam put his mug down.

"Say the thing about it."

"There's nothing to—"

"Mira."

She was quiet for a second.

"I have spent fourteen years being a person whose life could not survive
being written down," she said. "That's the whole architecture. Everything
in a tin, everything in my head, nothing in my name, nothing on any piece
of paper anywhere that a person could pick up and read.

"And it turns out you can just write it in the box," she said. "It's a
box. It's a quarter of an inch high. It's got a *line* under it."

·

At some point after one in the morning she took him out into the yard and
showed him the sign.

It was the original. Painted in 1961 on two eight-foot sheets bolted to
the frame above the gate, and repainted twice, the second time in 2016 by
a man in Hegewisch who had matched the lettering off a photograph.

WISNIEWSKI CARTAGE.

"Everybody's asked me for eleven years why I never changed it," Mira
said. "I always said it costs money and it attracts reading."

"Both true."

"Both true and neither of them was it." She had her hands in her coat
pockets, looking up at it. "Tadeusz Wisniewski put that sign up in 1961
and died in 1979 owing nobody anything. One truck, then four, then
eleven, and he paid tax on every one of them.

"His son ran it for thirty-two years and sold it to me in 2015 for eighty
percent of what it was worth and a handshake, and told me twice to put my
own name on the board, and died the spring after." She kept looking up.
"And I have never once in fourteen years been able to work out how a
person gets to be that, and I thought if I kept the name over the gate I
might learn it by standing under it."

Adam looked up at the sign.

"And?"

"And it turns out that's not how it works," Mira said. "You don't learn
it off a sign. You get it in a beige room off a woman with a folder, in
about eleven seconds, and then it takes a year."

She took her hand out of her pocket and pointed at the bottom right
corner of the second sheet, where there was a rectangle of clean primer
about the size of a road atlas, still tacky, that had not been there in
the morning.

"Benny did that this afternoon," she said. "Before he printed the form.
He didn't ask me and he didn't tell me and when I came in at nine he was
sitting in a chair backwards pretending he hadn't."

"What goes in it?"

"The licence number," Mira Dalca said. "When it comes. And my name under
it, small, in the corner, the way it goes on a carrier's board.

"The sign stays," she said. "I've grown fond of a man who didn't owe
anybody anything. But there's going to be a line under it, and it's going
to be mine, and it's going to be in a place where anybody driving past on
Torrence Avenue can read it."

They stood in the fog under the sodium lights and looked up at a rectangle
of primer.

"That's the whole book," Adam said.

"What is?"

"Safe passage." He had his hands in his pockets. "I've thought about
those two words every day for nineteen days and I had them wrong the
entire time. I thought it meant getting out. A route, a gate, a border,
somebody delivering you to the far side of something."

"It's not that."

"It's not that," Adam Marek said. "It's arriving somewhere and being
allowed to stay."

The fog came off the Calumet. A mile away the interstate ran, and beyond
it the tank farms, and beyond those the lake, which was doing what it had
done for eleven thousand years.

"Six a.m. Wednesday," Mira said.

"I'll be here."

"Bring a coat you can sleep in."

"I've got a coat."

"You've got somebody else's coat," said Mira Dalca. "We'll get you one."

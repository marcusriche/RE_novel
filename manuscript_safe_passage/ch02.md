## Chapter Two — Terms of Delivery

The truck was a 2016 Freightliner day cab with a twenty-eight-foot box, a
Cummins with four hundred and ten thousand on it, and a passenger seat
that Mira Dalca had not had occupied in six years.

She ran the rules at the Torrence gate, because she ran the rules at the
gate, always, before the wheels turned, in the same order, in the same
words. She had said them in three languages and to forty people and she
had never once changed them, and the not-changing was the point: a person
who is frightened remembers a rhythm when they cannot remember a sentence.

"No phone. If you have one, it goes in the box now and I don't care whose
it is."

"I don't have one."

"Then that part's easy. No names at fuel stops — not mine, not yours, not
anybody's. If somebody talks to you, you're my brother-in-law and you're
riding down to help me unload. You don't have to be good at it. You just
have to be boring."

"I can be boring."

"You sleep when I say. Not because I'm the boss of your sleeping, because
I'm going to be driving for eleven hours and I want you awake for the
part where I'm not." She looked at him for the first time since the
gate. "You eat what I hand you. If you need something, you say it before
we're rolling, not forty miles in."

"Understood."

"And you don't get out unless I open the door."

He was quiet for a second. In the mirror the yard was going small behind
them, the sign with its green field and white letters, Benny at the shop
door with a rag in his hand, watching them out.

"Say that one again," Adam Marek said.

"You don't get out unless I open the door."

"Okay." He put his hands on his knees. "I want you to know that I heard
it. That's all."

She pulled onto Torrence and headed for the Skyway, and did not answer,
because there was no answer, because the rule was the rule and it had
never once in six years occurred to her that it was worth remarking upon.

·

He asked how long, somewhere around Gary.

She gave him a distance. It was automatic; she did not think about it at
all until she heard herself do it.

"Three hundred and forty road miles to the first yard," she said. "It's a
four-hour drive. I'm not going to do it in four hours."

"How long, then?"

"Eleven. Maybe twelve." She checked the mirror. "We'll stop twice for
fuel and once for nothing, and I'll take the frontage road east of
Michigan City instead of the interstate, and if the wind stays like this
I'll want a break at Sawyer because that stretch beats up the box."

Adam looked out at the flat grey water going past on their left, the
steel mills stacked up along it like a country's worth of iron laid on
its side.

"That wasn't an answer to my question," he said. Not unkindly.

"It was the accurate one."

"It was a distance."

"Distance is what I've got." She said it flatly, and it came out harder
than she meant it, and she let it stand rather than repair it. "You asked
how long. I could say *a while*, which means nothing, or I could say
*eleven hours*, which is a guess, or I could tell you the actual shape of
the day. You want to know how long. I told you what's between here and
there. That's the same information with the guessing taken out."

He thought about that for a mile.

"Yeah," he said. "Alright. That's fair."

·

She did not want to know anything about him.

This was not indifference. It was method, and it was the oldest and most
carefully maintained piece of equipment she owned, and she had assembled
it in the first year and had never once had cause to revise it.

You do not learn the passenger.

You learn the *route*. You learn where the county line runs and which
scale house is manned at what hour and which of the three ways over a
river has a camera on it. You learn the weight and the fuel and the
distance. And you keep the person in the seat beside you at exactly the
distance at which they remain a logistics problem, because a logistics
problem can be solved and a person cannot, and because — this was the
part she would not have said aloud to anyone alive — if you learn them,
and then something happens to them, you have to carry that too, and she
had capacity for a great many things but not for that.

Eleven people. Eleven. She could recite the names, and did, once a week,
under a plank in a shed, and she could not have told you the face of a
single one of them, and that was on purpose, and it had cost her
something she had made a decision not to price.

So: cargo. Not cruelty. Method.

She had been doing it for eleven miles when he said, "You keep checking
the mirror."

"That's the job."

"Nine times in the last mile," Adam said. "I counted."

Mira's hands did not move on the wheel.

"Why," she said, "would you count that."

"Habit." He was still looking out the window. "You can't do anything with
a number until you've got one."

·

The first yard was in Portage, Indiana, in the flat industrial sprawl
under the Burns Harbor stacks — nine acres of gravel with a chain-link
fence, a scale house, a portable office on blocks, and forty containers
stacked three high in rows that had not been re-inventoried since the
Obama administration.

They got there at twenty past seven in the evening. The manifest said the
yard contact would be available between seven and nine, and it also said,
in a footnote so specific that Mira had read it four times at a fuel stop
in Michigan City, that the truck was to be inside the fence for the
duration of the wait.

"Forty minutes," she said, killing the engine. "Maybe more. Stay in the
cab."

"What's the stop for?"

"Nothing. That's the point. There's no cargo here, there's no paperwork
here, and there's nobody here who needs to see us." She swung down. "It's
a check. Somebody looks at us and calls somebody. Every yard on this
route is going to be that. It's how they know I'm doing what I said."

She stopped with her hand on the door.

"That's the first time I've told you something I didn't have to," she
said. "Don't make a thing of it."

The scale house had a light on. She went and stood at the window and
handed in the manifest's second page and a man in his fifties with a
clipboard came out and copied a number off it, and made conversation
about the wind, and did not once mention the load, because there was no
load.

Then he walked the truck.

He came down the driver's side at an unhurried pace with his clipboard
against his thigh. At the rear he stopped and looked at the box, and then
past the box, out through the fence toward the slip, where the water lay
grey and flat under the last of the light and a barge was tied up with
nobody on it.

He looked at the water for four seconds. Then he came back up the
passenger side, and glanced in at the man in the seat, and said something
pleasant about the wind again, and went back into the scale house.

Mira, standing at the front bumper, did not register any of it. She was
looking at the manifest.

·

Inside the cab, Adam Marek had gone entirely still.

He knew that walk. He had done that walk. He had done it in this yard and
nine others, for eleven years, on eleven hundred nights, and he could have
described it in a deposition and had, in fact, described it in one, to a
woman named Raman with her glasses up in her hair, in February.

*What's the walk for?*

*You're checking the load and the plates and the tag, if there's a load.*

*And if there isn't a load?*

*Then you're not checking the truck.*

*What are you checking?*

*Where it's going to happen. And how much of it there's going to be.*

He watched the scale-house man look at the water and understood two
things in the same second: that the man was estimating the distance from
this fence line to that slip, and that the entire remainder of Adam
Marek's life was a scheduling question.

He did not move. He kept his hands on his knees and his eyes forward and
he thought, very clearly: *if you say it out loud right now, she is going
to think you are a man trying to save himself, and she is going to be
right, and she is going to stop listening to you for the rest of the
route.*

*Wait until she sees something herself.*

She came back and got up into the cab and started the engine and said
nothing for eleven minutes, and then, at the on-ramp, changed her mind
about the fuel stop she had told him about at Michigan City and took a
different exit.

Adam looked out at the road going by and did not say a word about it.

·

He asked her, at the second fuel stop, at ten at night, standing outside
the truck in a wind coming off the lake, because it was the only question
that mattered and because he had spent four hours deciding how to ask it.

"What happens if they change their minds about where I'm going?"

Mira Dalca was holding the fuel nozzle and watching the numbers turn over.

She did not look at him. In her experience the ones who asked were the
ones who had already worked something out, and the correct answer was
always the same, and she had given it thirty-odd times, and it had been
true every single time she had given it.

"Nothing I carry gets left on the road," she said. "You arrive on the day
I said, on your own feet, with your own name on you."

Adam Marek stood in the sodium light of a truck stop outside Benton
Harbor and something went through him that took him a second to identify,
because he had not felt it in six months, and had not expected to feel it
from a woman who had been handed him like a pallet.

"Say it again," he said.

She looked up.

"Why?"

"Because I want to be able to repeat it back to you later," he said,
"word for word, and I'd like to get it right."

The pump clicked off.

"Nothing I carry gets left on the road," she said, slowly, the way you
read out a number to somebody writing it down. "You arrive on the day I
said. On your own feet. With your own name on you."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank a carrier before delivery." She racked the nozzle. "It's
bad luck and it's bad arithmetic. Thank me on the eighteenth."

"Alright."

"And Marek—" She stopped, because she had used his name, which she did
not do, and because she was about to explain herself, which she did less
often than that. "That's not a comfort. That's a term. It's what I'm
selling. If I don't deliver it I'm not a business, I'm a woman with a
truck. So don't take it as kindness. Take it as the thing I'm actually
paid for."

"I'll take it as a term," Adam said.

He said it in exactly her register, without any irony in it at all, and
she looked at him for a second longer than she needed to and then got up
into the cab.

·

Two hours later they were on US-12 in the dark with the dunes on the left
and nothing on the right, and he started talking.

Mira had seen this before. It came, always, at the same hour and under
the same conditions: dark cab, road noise, four or five hours of a person
sitting in one place with nothing to look at, and the fact that a
windscreen at night is very like a confessional in that both parties face
the same direction.

She had a policy. The policy was: let them.

"I drove for them eleven years," Adam said.

She kept her eyes on the road.

"I started at twenty-three. My uncle got me on. It was — you have to
understand, it was a *job*. It was the best job anybody in my family had
ever had. Benefits, actual benefits, through a leasing company, and I ran
a board out of a yard in Melrose Park with fourteen trucks on it by the
time I was twenty-eight." A pause. "And I never asked what was in the
back. Not once in eleven years."

"Nobody asks," Mira said.

"That's what I told myself."

"It's also true."

"It's both." He shifted. "And once it was a person."

The road went under them. She did not slow down and she did not speed up
and she did not turn her head, and afterward she would think about the
fact that she had done none of those things, and about how much practice
it must have taken to be able to do none of those things.

"In 2019," he said. "November. I had a run out of the Montreal yard that
should have been a container of machine parts, and it wasn't, and I found
out about forty minutes outside of Sarnia, and I kept driving." He said
it flatly and in order, the way he said everything, the doing first and
the reason nowhere near it. "I kept driving for six hours. I delivered
it. I signed for it. I went home and I slept, because I'd been up for a
day and a half, and that's the part—"

He stopped.

"That's the part I can't—"

"Don't," Mira said.

"What?"

"Don't do the next bit."

He turned his head. In the dash light she was a shape with two hands on a
wheel.

"You're going to tell me you couldn't sleep afterward," she said. "Or
that you drank. Or that you went to a church, or you didn't, or whatever
the next line is. And then I'm going to say something, because there's
only about four things a person can say, and then you'll feel better."

Silence in the cab.

"I'm not doing that," she said. "I'm not a priest and I'm not a
girlfriend and I'm not a bartender, and you don't get to put that down in
my truck and get out lighter at the other end. It doesn't work like that.
It's not a load."

Adam Marek sat in the dark for eleven miles.

"You're right," he said, at last.

"I know."

"No — I mean you're actually right, and I've never had anyone say it."
He was looking out the windscreen. "That's what I do. That's exactly what
I do. I say the worst thing about myself in the first four minutes so
that whoever it is has to decide about me right away, and then it's
theirs. It's not confession. It's — offloading."

Mira said nothing.

"Six months of proffer sessions," he said. "A federal prosecutor with her
glasses in her hair, three times, and it took a woman in a truck two days."

"It's a hundred and ninety miles to the next fuel stop," Mira said.

"Okay."

"There's a coffee in the door."

"Okay."

And that was all, for a hundred and ninety miles.

But in her head, in the dark, at eighty kilometres an hour on a road she
had driven forty times, Mira Dalca did an arithmetic she had never once
permitted herself and could not now stop.

*2019. November. Montreal to Sarnia.*

She had come in 2012, out of Constanța, in a container to Toronto and a
truck to Chicago.

Somebody had driven that truck.

She had spent fourteen years not wondering who, on the specific and
deliberate grounds that it was the single most useless piece of
information in the world, and that a woman who starts wondering that has
begun a project with no end in it.

She looked at the road, and the road was the road, and behind her in the
box was nothing at all, and beside her a man who had run the same line
sat with his hands on his knees, looking at the mirror, watching the
road behind them.

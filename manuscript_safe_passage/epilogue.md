## Epilogue — Open Water

The fog comes earlier in October.

By the first week it is on the yard at five in the morning instead of six,
coming up off the Calumet and standing between the container stacks in
slabs, and the sodium lights make their orange holes in it exactly as
they have done every autumn since 1961, and Mira Dalca walks the fence
line at four-forty with a torch she does not switch on.

She still counts the doors. She has stopped expecting that to change.

What has changed is the arithmetic at the end of the walk, which used to
come out as a list of things that could be taken and now comes out, most
mornings, as an inventory: eleven trucks on the board and three more on
order, fourteen drivers, two dispatchers, four hundred and six thousand
miles run since June, and not one single load on any of it that could not
be read aloud in a courtroom.

The yard is boring now. She had not anticipated how much she would like
that.

·

The authority came through on the eleventh of August.

It arrived in an envelope with a window in it and she opened it standing
in the gravel by the mailbox at seven in the morning and read it four
times, and then went and sat in the cab of a truck that was not going
anywhere for about eleven minutes, which is where Benny Osei found her
and from which he tactfully withdrew.

The number went up on the sign in September. A man came out from Hegewisch
with the same lettering off the same photograph and did it in an
afternoon, and it says, in the bottom right corner of the second sheet,
under WISNIEWSKI CARTAGE, in letters four inches high:

*M. DALCA — MC 1147822*

Adam took a photograph of it on his phone from across Torrence Avenue,
from the far side of four lanes, because he said the point was that you
could read it from there.

She has looked at that photograph more times than she is prepared to
discuss.

·

The trial is in the spring.

Gino Traversa has four counts and the transport arm has eleven defendants
between them, and Okafor says the spring is optimistic and that she
should plan for a year of continuances, and Raman says nothing at all
about the schedule, which Mira has learned to read as agreement.

She will testify under her own name. Everything will be read out: the
container, the price, the fourteen years. She has stopped rehearsing it,
which took until about the end of August, and which happened not through
any decision but on a Tuesday afternoon in the shed when she noticed she
had not run the sentences in her head for three days.

She sees a woman in Beverly on Thursday evenings. This was Adam's
suggestion, made once, in September, in the form of an observation about
his own July, and never mentioned again by either of them.

The eleven were never named. They are not in any document, in any
filing, in any exhibit list, and they will not be, and four separate
lawyers have now explained to Mira Dalca that this was a significant
strategic cost, and she has agreed with all four of them and not moved.

·

Adam Marek dispatches Tuesday through Saturday and is, to the documented
irritation of every driver on the board, extremely good at it.

He found the noise on twelve at ten past nine on his first Wednesday. It
was a heat shield. It had been a heat shield since 2021.

He has an apartment on Avenue O with a lease in his own name that he
signed with a hand that was not entirely steady, and he stays there
perhaps three nights a week, and neither of them has said anything about
the arrangement out loud because neither of them is in any hurry
whatsoever. He owns a coat. He owns a bicycle, a cast-iron pan, four
books, and a cribbage board that a retired deputy marshal named Halvorsen
mailed to him in July with a note that said *111–1, and I want a rematch*.

He is bad at Saturdays. Saturdays are the day the fear comes, and it
comes at about four in the afternoon for no reason at all, and Mira has
learned to be in the yard on Saturdays with a job that needs two people.

She is bad at the middle of the week. She decided something in June
without telling him — a route change, four trailers, nothing dangerous —
and he said the sentence, and she was furious for a day and a half and
then apologized in the ugly halting unfluent way she has, standing up, in
the shed, with her hands doing nothing.

It has been five months. It is going about as well as she budgeted for,
which is to say badly, in the right direction.

·

The envelope came on the fourteenth of October.

No return address. A Canadian stamp and a Thunder Bay postmark, and her
name and the yard's address in a careful hand that had been taught
letters in a different alphabet first.

Inside there was a photograph and nothing else.

A girl of about twenty-two in a graduation gown, on a lawn, squinting
into the sun, holding a rolled paper. There is a woman's arm across her
shoulders, cropped at the elbow, and you cannot see the woman's face.

No note. No names. Nothing on the back.

Mira Dalca stood in a yard on Torrence Avenue in the fog at seven in the
morning and looked at a photograph of a girl she had last seen at
eleven o'clock at night in November 2021, aged eighteen, in the false
floor of a trailer between Portage and Fort Erie, with her mother's hand
over her mouth.

Number nine on a list that no longer exists.

She has no way to reply. That is the design and it is her own design, and
she taught it to the mother herself, in a yard, in the dark, in 2021:
*nothing that can be traced back, ever, in either direction.*

So there will never be a name, and there will never be a note, and there
will never be another one.

It is the only receipt any of the eleven will ever send.

It is the only one she will ever keep.

It is in a frame in the dispatch shed above the board, where a fire
extinguisher inspection certificate used to hang, and every driver who
has asked about it has been told it is a cousin.

·

She does the morning manifest at ten to five, at the desk, in her own
hand, because she has tried the software four times and it makes her
nervous.

The nineteenth of October. Fog. Two drivers: Kowalczyk, who is
sixty-two now and still hates her, and a new woman called Reyes who has
been on the board for eleven days and is going to be excellent. One
load: forty thousand pounds of coil steel, Burns Harbor to Melrose Park,
which is a run they now do four times a week and which pays badly and
which she took because it is legal and dull and because a yard that runs
coil steel four times a week is a yard that exists in five years.

Sheet number. Date. Carrier.

In the corner of the carrier box, where it has gone on every manifest
since September, in block capitals in her own handwriting:

*WISNIEWSKI CARTAGE — M. DALCA, MC 1147822.*

Nothing owed. Not to anybody, by anybody, in either direction.

She hears the gate at five, and the gravel, and the office door.

"Two hundred and eleven miles," Adam says, hanging his coat on the nail
that is his now, "if you go the way you're going to go, and a hundred and
ninety-four if you'd let me route it, and I've brought the coffee."

Mira Dalca signs the manifest and puts the pen down and gets up.

Outside, the fog is going off the Calumet the way it does when the sun
gets under it, and the lake is out there past the tank farms doing what
it does, and eleven trucks are on a board with nothing hidden in any of
them, and the whole of the road is only a road.

"Let's go to work," she says.

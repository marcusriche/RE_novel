## Chapter Twenty — The Door Stays Shut

Three days of a closed studio door.

Nadia does her shifts — she doesn't miss a single one, which I
understand, eventually, is its own kind of statement, a woman making
absolutely certain that whatever else is broken, her actual job is not
one of the casualties — but she does them with both headphone cups
sealed for the first time since I've known her, and texts read and not
answered, and a version of professional distance so complete it makes
the first week of this whole arrangement look intimate by comparison.

The theatre contract, I notice, is no longer quite so unopened.

I try every register I own. Apology — three different versions,
increasingly specific, all of them, in retrospect, doing exactly what
she's spent six weeks asking me not to do, which is manage the
situation instead of simply sitting in it. Explanation, which lands as
a worse kind of apology. Silence, which I try for one entire day and
find I am constitutionally incapable of sustaining. More apology.

Every single one of them lands as management. Every single one of them
is, structurally, the precise thing she asked me, at the very start,
never to turn this into.

Priya, for her part, is barely functioning. She offers to resign twice
in the first two days, both times in the specific dramatic register of
a seventeen-year-old who has never before been the cause of a real
adult catastrophe and has, as a result, no proportionate way to hold
the feeling. I refuse both times. The second time, I sit her down
properly and tell her the thing I actually believe, which is that a
station run entirely without the possibility of human error isn't a
station, it's a machine, and machines don't run community radio, and
the day I start firing seventeen-year-olds for automation mistakes is
the day this place stops being worth saving in the first place.

She doesn't fully believe me. I'm not sure I fully believe myself. But
she stops offering to resign, and starts, instead, quietly rebuilding
the entire automation system from scratch in her spare time, a
project nobody's asked her to undertake and that I don't have the
heart to tell her isn't strictly necessary.

"I'm not asking you to fix it," Nadia says, once, through a half-open
door, the only time in three days she opens it even that far. "I'm
asking you to understand that you can't."

I don't have an answer for that either. I go home, and I do the thing
I have spent my entire adult life doing instead of grieving anything
properly, which is make myself indispensable to a problem that, this
time, does not want fixing.

·

Kirsty finds me at a coffee shop near the Chambers, off the clock,
in a coat instead of a blazer, which I notice before she's said a
single word.

"I heard," she says, sitting down without asking. "The whole panel's
heard. I wanted to say something before it comes up in a meeting where
it'll sound like policy instead of like a person talking to another
person."

"Go on, then."

"Douglas is already drafting language about *reputational exposure*,"
Kirsty says, with the specific distaste of someone who has spent eight
years learning to speak that language fluently and has never once
stopped finding it repellent. "I want you to know that before you walk
into whatever room he eventually calls this in. He's not thinking
about Nadia. He's thinking about a line in next year's funding
application."

"That's reassuring."

"It's not meant to be reassuring. It's meant to be useful." She
wraps both hands round her cup, finally, without drinking from it.
"There's a version of the next few weeks where you spend all your
energy managing Douglas's language and none of it on the actual
person this happened to. I've watched you do exactly that for six
years, over smaller things. I don't think you can afford to do it over
this one."

"I don't know how to do anything else," I say, and it comes out more
honestly than I mean it to. "Managing it is the only tool I've got."

"It isn't, though," Kirsty says. "It's just the one you reach for
first. There's a difference, and I think you know there's a
difference, because you wouldn't be sitting across from me looking
this wretched if some part of you hadn't already worked out that
managing it isn't going to be enough this time."

"I left breakfast radio eight years ago," she goes on, "and I've spent
every one of those eight years telling myself I made the ambitious
choice. National reach. Bigger platform. All the things you say when
someone asks why you left." She turns her cup a quarter-turn on the
saucer. "I made the safe choice, Robyn, and I
called it ambition, because staying meant being seen failing at it in
public, on a local station, in front of people who'd known me for
years. Leaving let me fail privately instead, somewhere nobody I grew
up next to would ever hear about it."

I don't say anything. I'm not entirely sure, yet, what this has to do
with me, or with the closed door I've spent three days standing
outside of.

"You're the one who stayed," Kirsty says, "and did the hard, visible,
unglamorous thing, in front of everyone, for six years, and I have
never once said that to you properly. I'm saying it now. Late. Over
coffee. Because it's easier than saying it in a meeting."

It isn't absolution. I don't think it's really about Nadia at all,
strictly speaking. But it hands me something I didn't have this
morning — the actual, specific distinction between performing safety
and choosing something, out loud, where it can be refused, which is
precisely the choice I've been failing to make in front of a closed
studio door for three days.

I leave the coffee shop knowing exactly what I have to do next, and
still, entirely, not knowing whether it will work.

"There's one more thing," Kirsty says, catching my sleeve as I stand
to go, in a gesture so unlike the professionally warm distance she's
kept for eight years that I actually stop moving. "For what it's
worth — whatever happens with the renewal, whatever Douglas ends up
saying in whatever meeting he calls this in — I'll argue for the
show. Properly. Not because it's good optics. Because I've listened
to it, every week, and it's the best thing on this station's schedule
in a decade, and I'm not going to sit in a room and let anyone call
that a liability without saying so."

"Thank you," I say, and mean it more than the two words let on.

"Don't thank me yet. Douglas is going to be extremely tedious about
it first."

"I'd expect nothing less."

She almost smiles at that — the first almost-smile I've had from her
in eight years that isn't professionally calibrated for a room full of
other people — and lets me go, and I walk back toward the station with
something in my chest that isn't quite hope, not yet, but is at least
no longer only dread.

·

Priya is waiting for me at the bottom of the basement stairs when I
get back, sitting on the second step from the bottom in a way that
tells me, before she says anything, that she's been rehearsing this
conversation for a while.

"I want to apologise properly," she says, standing up. "Not the
resigning kind. The actual kind, where I say the specific thing I did
wrong and don't make you talk me out of feeling bad about it."

"Okay."

"I got complacent," Priya says. "I've done that queue a hundred times
and I stopped checking it as carefully as I should have, because it'd
never gone wrong before, and I told myself that meant it couldn't. I
was wrong. I'm sorry. Not just for the mistake — for how long it took
me to actually look at why I made it instead of just how bad I felt
about it."

It is, I think, standing on a basement staircase with a seventeen-
year-old who has clearly done more genuine reflection on her own
error in three days than most adults manage in a year, one of the
more remarkable things anyone has said to me this whole strange
autumn.

"Thank you," I say. "That's the actual apology. I accept it."

"Is Nadia going to accept it too?"

"I don't know yet," I say, honestly. "But I think she'll hear it, when
you're ready to say it to her directly, and I think that'll matter
more than anything I could say on your behalf."

Priya does go to her, in the end, on the second day, standing outside
the same closed studio door I've been failing to talk my way through
for two days running.

She tells me about it afterward, in the kitchen, in a much quieter
voice than her usual one. "I said the thing I said to you. The whole
of it. She didn't open the door either. But she said, through it,
'thank you for telling me properly instead of just feeling bad about
it where I couldn't hear you,' and I think that's the most an apology
can actually do, isn't it. Just be heard, even through a door."

"That's exactly what an apology can do," I say. "It's more than most
people manage."

"Do you think she'll forgive me?"

"I think she already has," I say, "in the sense that matters. She's
not angry at you, Priya. She's frightened, and hurt, and those aren't
the same thing as angry, even though they can look the same from the
outside for a while."

Priya nods, slowly, taking that in with the specific gravity of
someone filing away a distinction she suspects she'll need again
someday, and goes back to the automation queue, quieter than usual,
but steadier too, in a way that tells me the apology did exactly what
it needed to do, for both of them.

·

Fiona rings again that evening, checking in properly this time, having
heard from me only in fragments over the previous three days.

"You sound tired," she says.

"I am tired. Three days of a closed door will do that."

"Is she talking to you yet?"

"A little. Through the door, mostly. She let it open a few inches
yesterday, which felt like a genuine landmark at the time, and today
it's shut again, which I'm trying very hard not to read as a step
backward, because I don't think it is one. I think grief doesn't move
in a straight line, and I think I'm only just starting to actually
believe that, rather than just knowing it as a fact."

"You've never been good at things that don't move in a straight line,"
Fiona says, not unkindly. "You like problems with a fix at the end of
them. A roof you can patch. A budget you can balance. This one doesn't
have a fix, and I think that's the actual test in front of you right
now — not whether you can solve it, but whether you can stay standing
next to it without needing to."

"I'm trying."

"I know you are. That's all anyone can ask of you right now, including
her, whether or not she's able to say so from behind a door this
week." A pause, warmer. "You'll get it right eventually. Not perfectly.
Eventually. That's usually enough."

I sit with that for a long time after we hang up, in a flat that feels
quieter than usual without a single word from the other side of a
studio door, and let myself believe, for the length of one phone call
at least, that eventually might actually be close enough to console
myself with tonight.

·

Dev turns up on the third day, unprompted, with a box of shortbread
that has, by now, become something of a running joke about his
particular method of processing guilt, except this time it isn't his
own guilt he's trying to atone for.

"I heard what happened," he says, setting the box down carefully on
the kitchen counter. "I don't really know Nadia well. I know you,
though, a bit, from four years of shifts, and I know you're the kind
of person who'll try to carry all of this yourself rather than let
anyone help, so I brought biscuits, because it's the only language I've
got for *I'm thinking about you and I don't know what else to do*."

"You didn't have to."

"I know I didn't. I wanted to." He hovers for a moment, clearly
working up to something. "For what it's worth — and I recognise I'm
not really in a position to have deep insight into your relationship —
but the version of you I've watched running this place for four years
is somebody who fixes things. I just wanted to say, in case it's
useful, that maybe this one isn't a fixing problem. Maybe it's a
staying problem. Just — being there, steadily, without trying to solve
it. I don't know if that helps."

It helps considerably more than he probably realises, and I tell him
so, and he goes slightly pink with the specific embarrassment of a man
who has said something genuinely wise and doesn't quite know what to do
with the fact of it landing.

"I'm not generally good at the wise thing," he says. "I mostly just
bring biscuits."

"The biscuits help too."

"Good," Dev says, visibly relieved to have discharged his duty, and
leaves the shortbread on the counter, and goes back to his shift,
leaving me with a box of biscuits and a sentence I turn over for the
rest of the evening — *maybe this one isn't a fixing problem* — that
turns out to be exactly the reframe I needed to hear from someone
standing far enough outside the situation to say it plainly.

## Chapter Six — Four Words About a Cable

She tells me, unprompted, on the Friday before the first broadcast, at
gone midnight, in the specific voice she uses when she's decided
something has to be said and is going to say it exactly once.

"Before this goes any further," Nadia says, "there's a reason I don't
do my voice in public, and I'm not going to explain it. I need you to
not need me to."

I have been running a station for six years on the theory that every
problem can be solved if you get enough information about it. It is,
I will admit, not always a theory that serves me well. But something
about the way she says it — not defensive, not bracing for an
argument, just stating a fact about the shape of her the way she'd
state a fact about a fader — makes the correct response arrive in me
fully formed, for once, without a detour through six other responses
first.

"Okay," I say.

That's it. That's the whole of what I say. I do not ask what the
reason is. I do not ask if it's serious, or recent, or something I
should know for the show's sake, or any of the forty questions that
are, at this exact moment, queuing up behind my teeth like traffic.

Nadia looks at me for a second like she's checking the sentence for a
trap door and not finding one.

"Okay," she says back, and it costs her something, visibly, to have
said even the four words she's said, and she changes the subject to a
fader that's been sticking since March, and I let her, because I am,
apparently, capable of this after all. I just didn't know it until
someone needed me to be.

I think about it on the walk home more than I probably should. Not the
mystery of it — I am, against every instinct I have cultivated over
six years of running a building on information and spreadsheets,
genuinely uninterested in solving it — but the fact of her having said
anything at all. Eleven words in three years, four of them about a
cable, and now this: a whole sentence about herself, offered
unprompted, at midnight, to a woman she's known properly for exactly
five days. I don't know what to do with the honour of that except try
very hard not to drop it.

·

The final rehearsal happens on the Wednesday, a full run-through with
Priya in the producer's seat and an empty studio standing in for
whatever audience Thursday actually brings.

It goes badly, in the specific way things go badly when everybody
involved is too aware of the stakes to relax into them properly. I
fluff a link twice. Nadia, normally unshakeable, mistimes a cue by a
full three seconds, which for her is practically a public breakdown.
Priya, watching the two of us fail to be even remotely convincing as
either presenters or a couple, eventually stops the run entirely and
puts both hands flat on the desk.

"Right," she says. "Stop. Both of you. You're doing the thing where
you try so hard to get it right that you forget how to just be two
people in a room."

"That's rather the whole problem, Priya. We're not two people in a
room. We're pretending to be a couple for a funding panel."

"No," Priya says, with the specific patience of someone repeating
something she considers self-evident, "you're two people who *like*
each other, badly hiding it behind a format, and the second you stop
trying to perform the liking and just let the actual liking show,
you'll both be fine. You don't need to act. You need to stop acting."

Neither of us has an answer to that. We run the segment again, without
trying quite so hard, and it comes out considerably better — not
perfect, not smooth, but recognisably itself, two people who like each
other doing an imperfect job of proving it on the record.

"Better," Priya announces, with the satisfaction of a director who
has just solved a problem two supposed adults couldn't solve
themselves. "Do that tomorrow. Just that."

·

The night before the first broadcast, we end up on the fire escape,
because the small meeting room has Priya's admin in it and the
overnight studio has three days of unattended cables in it, and the
fire escape has rain, moving up the Clyde in visible sheets, and
nothing else.

"Can I ask you something," I say, "off the record. Not the show's
record. Mine."

"Go on."

"If the segment wants a kiss. At the end of the hour, for the cameras
— well, for the microphones, there aren't any cameras, but you know
what I mean — is that on the table."

I say it plainly, because I have learned, in three weeks, that plainly
is the only register she trusts, and because the whole reason I'm
asking off mic, the night before, standing in the rain, is that there
is a version of this job — the fake-relationship, engagement-metric,
audience-identifiable version — that would use tonight's silence as
permission and never actually ask, and I would rather lose the show
than be the kind of person who does that to her.

Nadia thinks about it properly. I've learned to recognise the specific
stillness that means she's actually weighing something, rather than
just being quiet, and I wait through it without filling the silence,
which is, for me, its own small victory.

"Yes," she says, eventually. "On the table. Scripted. On air. At the
end of the hour, and nowhere else, and if it ever stops feeling like
that, I'll say so."

"Understood."

She looks out at the rain for a second. "You asked."

"I asked."

"Most people wouldn't have."

I don't say anything to that, because I don't entirely trust my voice
right at this moment, and because there's something else I need to
say and I want to get it right the first time.

"I need to say a thing too," I say. "And I need you to hear it as the
actual foundation this whole thing sits on, not as a nice sentence I'm
saying to make you feel better about tomorrow."

"Say it."

"What's real off that mic stays off the record," I say. "Every time.
I promise you that."

The rain does its thing on the corrugated roof above us. Nadia looks
at me for a long moment — longer than the kiss question got, longer
than anything's got so far — and something moves behind her face that
I don't have a name for yet.

"That's a strange promise to need," she says.

"Yeah," I say. "I know."

Neither of us says anything else about it. But I feel it settle into
place between us the way a fact settles once it's been said out loud —
solid, load-bearing, the kind of thing you find out later you've been
leaning your whole weight on.

We stay on the fire escape a while longer than either of us needs to,
watching the rain do its thing over the rooftops toward the river,
neither of us in any particular hurry to go back inside and be two
professionals preparing a segment instead of whatever we currently
are, standing in the wet, having just built something between us with
words instead of a contract.

"Nervous," Nadia says, eventually, not quite a question.

"Terrified," I say. "You?"

"I engineer live radio for a living. Nerves are a Tuesday." A beat.
"This is different nerves."

"How can you tell the difference?"

"The Tuesday kind, I know what I'm frightened of," she says. "This
kind, I don't. That's the tell."

I don't have an answer for that, so I don't try to give her one. We
just stand there, in the rain, letting the not-knowing be its own
kind of company, until the cold finally drives us both back in.

Priya's left a note on the whiteboard when we come back through — GOOD
LUCK TOMORROW, DON'T MESS IT UP, in her small square handwriting, with
a drawing of a microphone that is, generously, more confident than
accurate — and Nadia looks at it for a long moment before saying,
quietly, "she's put a lot of faith in something that's mostly held
together with a cue sheet and a promise."

"She's seventeen. Faith's cheap at seventeen."

"It shouldn't be cheap anywhere," Nadia says, and wipes a smear of rain
off her sleeve, and heads for the door, and I follow her out into a
Glasgow night that has, for once, nothing further to say about any of
it.

·

I don't sleep at all that night, in the end, though not for the
reasons I'd expected.

I lie awake running through the format one more time, not because it
needs it — Nadia's built it properly, better than anything I'd have
managed alone — but because running through things is the only
mechanism I have for managing a feeling too large to sit still with.
Three call slots. Thirty minutes. Music beds under the advice, silence
kept for the moments that have earned it. A kiss at fifty-four minutes,
consented to on a fire escape, in the rain, by a woman who has said
roughly eleven words to me across three years and has just, in the
space of one evening, said several hundred more, unprompted, about
things that plainly cost her to say.

I think about the phrase she used — *this is different nerves* — and
turn it over until the sky starts going grey at the edges of my
curtains, and get up, eventually, having slept perhaps forty minutes in
total, to go and do the single most nerve-inducing thing I have ever
volunteered myself for on a Thursday evening in September.

·

Priya's already at the station when I arrive, hours before she needs
to be, and she's added something to the wall of fame overnight: a
fresh corner cleared, deliberately, with a single pin waiting in it and
a note underneath in her small square handwriting that reads FOR
TONIGHT'S MESSAGES — LEAVE ROOM.

"You didn't have to do that," I say.

"I know I didn't. I wanted to." She's rearranging cue cards that don't
need rearranging, the specific busywork of someone managing her own
nerves by managing something smaller than the actual thing. "Sandra's
already texted to say she's got the whole street listening tonight.
Grant from the allotments says he's recording it, which I've told him
he's not allowed to do, and he's said he's doing it anyway, which is
very on brand for Grant."

"No pressure, then."

"Enormous pressure," Priya says, entirely cheerfully. "The good kind.
The kind where people actually care whether you turn up." She stops
rearranging the cards and looks at me properly, for a second, in a way
that's older than her seventeen years usually let her look. "You're
going to be fine, you know. Both of you. I've watched you build this
whole thing out of nothing in three weeks, and I don't think either of
you understands yet how much of it wasn't nothing at all."

I don't ask her to explain that, because I suspect I already know what
she means, and because there isn't time before the first callers start
ringing in to warm up the lines, and because some sentences are better
left exactly as generously vague as she's left this one, to be turned
over properly later, once the first broadcast is behind us and I've
got the room to actually think about what she's just said.

·

Nadia arrives at six, exactly eleven minutes before her usual mark
even though tonight isn't, technically, her shift at all, and finds me
already at the desk, rundown in hand, reading the same paragraph for
what must be the fifth time without absorbing a word of it.

"You've read that line eleven times," she says, not a question.

"Have I?"

"You moved your lips the same way each time. I counted." She takes the
rundown gently out of my hands and sets it face-down on the desk. "You
know this already. You wrote half of it. Reading it again isn't going
to make you know it any better. It's just going to make you more
frightened of it."

"That's uncomfortably accurate."

"I'm an engineer," Nadia says. "Accuracy's the whole job." She looks at
me properly, in the specific way she has, head tilted a few degrees off
centre. "For what it's worth — and I don't say this kind of thing
often, so take it as the rare event it is — I think we're going to be
alright tonight. Not perfect. Alright. That's usually enough."

"Since when are you the reassuring one? I thought that was meant to be
my job."

"Since about ten minutes ago, apparently," Nadia says, entirely
unbothered by the reversal, "because somebody has to be, and you've
clearly used up your whole supply of it on yourself already this
evening, badly, on a rundown that doesn't need reading an twelfth
time."

I laugh, properly, the tension breaking for a moment, and she almost
smiles back — the real one, the one around the eyes — before Priya
calls through that the lines are open and there's no more time left
for either of us to be frightened about anything except, now, simply
doing the thing.

·

I catch sight of the wall of fame on my way to the booth, still bare
except for Priya's cleared corner and the single waiting pin, and think,
briefly, about how strange it is that a corkboard by a kettle has
become, this week, something I check the way other people check a
horoscope — not for prediction, just for reassurance that the ordinary
furniture of the place is still exactly where it should be.

Malcolm's voice arrives in my head, unbidden, the way it does at moments
like this one: *don't let it become boring, and don't let it close.*
I've never once, in nine years, understood quite how much weight those
eleven words could carry until tonight, standing outside a studio door
about to broadcast a relationship that started as a lie and has become,
somewhere in the last three weeks, the truest thing in my life.

Nadia's talkback crackles once, testing. "Thirty seconds," she says,
flat, certain, the same voice she'll use for every cue tonight and every
Thursday after this one, and I take my seat, and settle my headphones,
and wait for the red light that is, in this particular basement, never
actually red at all, only ever the same patient, unfixed amber it's
always been.

## Chapter Seventeen — Static

"Before anything else happens," Nadia says, very late, in her own flat,
with the record player finally silent, "there's a thing I need you to
know. I need to say it once, and I need you not to make a face about
it while I do."

I put my mug down. "Okay."

"I'm Static."

I don't make a face. I want that on the record, because it costs me
something not to, and I think she can see that it costs me something,
which is, I suspect, exactly why she trusts me enough to keep going.

Static is the station's most-loved segment and has been for two years
— an anonymous late-night voice, unpaid, uncredited, on air between
midnight and one, talking about nothing and everything in a register
so unguarded it's become, without anyone officially naming it, the
actual reason half our overnight listeners tune in at all. I've spent
two years assuming Static was some rotating cast of volunteers, or a
syndicated feed, or — once, embarrassingly, out loud, to Priya — a
retired presenter doing us a favour. I have never once, not for a
second, suspected the woman sitting six feet away from me with her
headphone cup permanently half off.

"How long," is all I manage.

"Two years. Since the podcast." She says it like she's naming a
country she used to live in. "I stopped being a person people talked
about and started being a voice with a desk in front of it. It's the
only shift I've ever felt safe on. Nobody's ever looking at Static.
They're just listening."

I think back through two years of nights I've spent in this building
— the ones I stayed late for no good reason, the ones I told myself I
was just checking the overnight schedule — and try to place Static
against them, and find that I can, more easily than I'd like. The
segment that ran the week after the transmitter nearly failed, gentle
and unhurried, when the whole building had spent the day in crisis.
The one about doors — an entire fifteen minutes on doors, why some
swing open and some you have to pull, why some rooms only ever have
one way out — that I'd listened to twice, alone, in my car outside my
own flat, without once wondering who was behind it.

"I used to think Static sounded like someone who'd been hurt and
decided to be kind about it anyway," I say. "I told Priya that once.
She said I was reading too much into a radio segment."

"You weren't."

"The touring years," I say, carefully, because I know, roughly, the
shape of this now, from the fringe theatre envelope and the four words
about a cable and everything she hasn't said in between. "The
relationship that ended in public."

"The podcast made about it still exists," Nadia says. "I've never once
listened to it all the way through. People send me clips sometimes, by
accident, thinking I'll find them funny. I don't find them funny."

I don't say I'm sorry, because I've learned, by now, that sorry is a
door she doesn't want opened on her behalf. I say the only thing I
actually know to be true.

"I know exactly which nights Static ran," I say, "and which nights you
looked like you hadn't slept. I've known for two years and never once
put the two things together, because I wasn't looking for the pattern.
I'm looking now."

Something moves in her face — not relief, exactly. Recognition, maybe.
The specific look of a person who has just found out the thing they
were most afraid of being seen for has, in fact, already been half-seen,
gently, by someone who didn't make it a story.

·

We don't move from where we're sitting for a long time. When we do,
it's Nadia who says the next thing, in the tone she uses for
everything that actually matters — flat, exact, no room in it for
either of us to mishear.

"I want to say the rest properly," she says. "Not for the segment.
For us."

"Say it."

"This isn't for the panel," I say first, because I want it said before
she has to ask. "I need you to hear that as its own sentence, separate
from everything else I've ever said to you in this building."

"I know," Nadia says. "I wouldn't be here if I thought it was."

We talk, properly, for the first time, about what we both actually
want — not the segment's version, not the six-week version, the real
one. I say the true thing about my own fear, out loud, in full, maybe
for the first time in my adult life: that I am afraid of asking for
something and being told no where anyone could count it, which is
exactly why I have spent a decade making myself so indispensable that
nobody ever has the occasion to say no to me at all.

Nadia says hers back, just as plainly: that she is afraid of being
loved as a bit, a story, a thing people talk about at parties, rather
than as a person anyone actually knows.

"I want this," she says, at the end of it. "All of it. I'm asking you
properly, so there's no version of tonight where either of us gets to
say afterward that it wasn't discussed."

"You have it," I say. "Same back. Properly asked."

"There's one more thing," Nadia says, "and it's smaller, and I want to
say it because I think it matters more than it sounds like it should."

"Go on."

"Ask me again next week. And the week after that. Not because you
think the answer's going to change — because I need to know this
isn't a box you tick once and then assume stays ticked. That's the
thing the podcast took from me. Not the relationship. The ability to
believe that anyone's consent to anything is still active unless
they've said otherwise."

"I'll ask you every week," I say. "For as long as there are weeks."

"That's a big promise for a Tuesday."

"It's not a Tuesday promise," I say. "I've only got the one kind."

She tells me the rest of it, eventually, in pieces, over the course of
the evening, the way people tell you things when they've decided to
trust you but haven't yet worked out how to hand it all over at once.

The relationship itself wasn't the story, she says. That's the part
that took her the longest to accept — that the actual thing between
her and the person the podcast is about was, for two years, ordinary
and good and entirely private, the way relationships are supposed to
be, and that the story only started once it ended, once one half of
it decided the ending was interesting enough to build a following
around. Twelve episodes. A named host, an anonymous "source close to
the situation" who was never, technically, named, though anyone who
knew where to look could work it out inside a week. Nadia never
listened past episode two. She didn't need to. She'd lived episode
two. She knew, roughly, the shape the rest of it would take, because
grief follows a formula whether or not somebody's monetising it.

"The worst part wasn't being talked about," she says. "It was that
people I actually knew — people at work, people I'd toured with for
years — started treating me like a character in something, rather
than a person who'd had a bad year. They'd ask how I was in this
particular tone, this sort of hushed, delighted tone, like they were
checking in on a plot development. I stopped being able to tell the
difference between someone who cared and someone who was just
collecting material."

"Is that why you stopped touring?"

"That's why I stopped touring in front of people who knew who I was,"
Nadia says. "Overnight radio in a basement in Partick is about as far
from that as I could get and still be doing the thing I'm actually
good at. Nobody here was listening to the podcast. Nobody here cared.
I could just be an engineer. It took about eight months before I
stopped flinching every time somebody said my name into a live mic."

I think about the two years I've spent walking past that door,
assuming — because it was easier, because it required nothing of
me — that she simply preferred it quiet in there. I think about how
wrong easy assumptions usually are, when you finally get close enough
to check them.

"Thank you," I say, "for telling me the whole of it. I know it isn't
small."

"It isn't small," Nadia agrees. "But it isn't the only thing about me
either, and I think that's what actually mattered — not that I told
you, but that telling you didn't turn me back into the story again. It
just made me a person you know a bit better than you did this
morning."

"Can I ask one more thing," I say. "About the name. Static. Was that
deliberate, the pun, or did it just arrive the way On the Level did?"

"Deliberate," Nadia says. "Completely deliberate. Static's the sound
between stations — the bit nobody wants, the bit you tune past to get
to something clearer. I liked the idea of being the thing people
usually skip past, except choosing to make it worth staying on. It
felt like the most honest name available for what I actually was for
two years: the noise nobody expected to matter, doing its best to
matter anyway."

"It mattered," I say. "It still does."

"I know that now," Nadia says, and there's something in the way she
says it — quiet, certain, entirely without her usual guardedness —
that tells me this particular evening has done more work in both of
us than either of us has fully reckoned with yet.

We stay up talking long past any sensible hour, the way we did the
first night we admitted anything real to each other, except this
time there's nothing left unsaid between us, no envelope in a drawer,
no reason left to guard. She tells me about the fringe theatre
company properly, this time, the actual work rather than the reasons
to avoid it — a two-hander about grief that toured village halls for
three winters, a lighting rig she rebuilt from scratch out of
spare parts because the budget didn't stretch to a new one, the
specific pride of making something work with almost nothing that she
says she's never quite managed to explain to anyone who hasn't done
it themselves.

"You'd have liked it," she says. "The work, I mean. Not the touring."

"I believe you."

"I mean it. You'd have understood the making-do. That's the whole of
what you do here, isn't it — keeping a building running on almost
nothing, out of sheer refusal to let it stop."

"That's rather generous."

"It's accurate," Nadia says, "which is better than generous," and I
don't argue with that, because by now I've learned there's very
little point arguing with a sentence that's simply true.

Nothing about the rest of that evening is for anyone but the two of
us, which is, in the end, the entire point.

·

I ring Fiona the next morning, mostly because I need to say some of it
out loud to someone who isn't in the room where it happened, and tell
her, in careful, general terms, that Nadia's told me something
significant about her past, and that I'm not going to repeat the
specifics because they aren't mine to repeat, but that it's changed how
I understand nearly everything about her.

"Are you alright?" Fiona asks. "Not her. You. Carrying somebody else's
weight like that is its own kind of work."

"I'm alright. I think I'm honoured, actually, more than anything else.
That she trusted me with it."

"That's the right response," Fiona says, approvingly. "The wrong one
would have been to immediately start trying to fix whatever it is.
I take it you didn't do that."

"I didn't do that."

"Good. You're learning." A pause, warmer this time. "I'm glad you've
found someone who tells you the true, difficult things rather than
just the easy ones. That's rarer than people think, and harder to
build than the easy kind of closeness. I hope you know how lucky that
makes you, having someone willing to do it."

"I know," I say, and mean it more than the two words let on, and we
talk for a while longer about smaller things, the ordinary furniture of
two sisters checking in on each other, before Fiona rings off with her
usual efficiency, leaving me sitting with the whole of the previous
evening a while longer, turning it over gently, grateful for every
piece of it Nadia's chosen, so far, to hand me.

·

Priya, entirely without meaning to, gives me the clearest picture yet
of how much has changed between me and Nadia when she mentions, in
passing, that she's stopped bothering to leave the room when we talk
quietly at the desk.

"I used to give you two space," she says, "back when it still felt
like watching two people rehearse something. I don't do that anymore.
It doesn't feel like a performance to leave space around now. It just
feels like two people talking, the way anyone talks, and there's no
reason for me to make a whole production of tiptoeing out."

"When did you stop noticing the difference?"

"I don't think there's a single moment," Priya says, considering it
properly. "It was more like — one week I was still doing the
tiptoeing thing, out of habit, and the next week I just wasn't, and I
only noticed I'd stopped when I caught myself not doing it. It's a bit
like the automation queue, actually. You don't notice a system's
started running properly on its own. You just notice, one day, that
you've stopped checking it every five minutes."

I think about that comparison for a long time afterward — a fake
relationship becoming so unremarkably, ordinarily real that even the
building's sharpest observer stopped clocking the seam — and find it,
somehow, the most reassuring thing anyone's said about the two of us
in weeks, delivered, as most of the important things in this building
seem to be, by a seventeen-year-old who wasn't even trying to say
anything profound at the time.

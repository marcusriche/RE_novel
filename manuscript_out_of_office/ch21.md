## Chapter Twenty-One — What She's Not Rehearsing

I go to her flat with nothing rehearsed, for the first time in the
whole of this — no cue sheet, no format, no line I've managed into
shape on the walk over.

That's not quite true, if I'm honest with myself, which I am trying
very hard to be at this specific stage of the story. I did try to
rehearse it. I got as far as the second sentence, three separate
times, on the walk from the subway, and abandoned each attempt somewhere
around the bit where I would have started explaining myself, because
I have finally, painfully, started to understand that explaining
myself is not the same thing as being honest, and that the two have
been getting confused with each other in my head for the better part
of a decade.

So I knock, and I stand there with nothing prepared, which feels, if
I'm being accurate about it, like standing somewhere very high up
without a railing.

The door doesn't open. I can hear her on the other side of it,
though — not moving, just present, the specific quality of quiet that
means someone hasn't walked away.

"I can't fix what happened to you," I say, through the wood, because
I've learned by now not to assume I've earned the right to say it to
her face. "I'm not going to try. I know trying would just be the same
thing I've done every other time, dressed up as an apology. I'm asking
for one thing. Thursday. Not for the panel. It's the last scheduled
show, and I would like it to be honest, and I can't make it honest
without you, and I know that's not a fair thing to ask of someone I've
just watched get hurt because of me."

Silence, for long enough that I start composing, in my head, the
version of this evening where I go home having said it into an empty
stairwell, into a door that never opens, into a Thursday that never
comes.

"You'd be asking me to go back on air," Nadia says, eventually, "after
the last time I was on a recording that got out without my say-so cost
me two years of my life."

"I know."

"That's not a small thing to ask."

"I know that too. I'm asking anyway, because I've run out of things to
offer you that aren't just more careful versions of the same
management I've been doing since the day I met you, and I'd rather
ask for something real and get told no than keep offering you
apologies you've already told me you don't want."

Another silence, shorter this time.

"Why does it have to be Thursday," Nadia says. "Why not just — let it
end. Quietly. The renewal's probably gone either way. Nobody would
blame either of us for just letting the whole thing lapse."

"Because if we let it lapse," I say, "the last thing anyone ever hears
of On the Level is an accident. Forty-one people at half past midnight
and a recording neither of us chose to make public. That's not the
ending. That's just where the accident happened to stop. I don't think
either of us gets to decide anything real again until we've put
something real back in its place, on purpose, where the accident was."

"That's a very tidy argument."

"I know. I've had three days to build it. I'm aware it might not be
enough."

"I'll think about it."

That's the whole answer. Flat, through the wood, no warmth in it that
I can detect. But it's the first uncertain yes this entire book has
had, and I walk home in the rain not knowing — which, for a woman who
has spent a decade managing every outcome down to a known result, is,
I am slowly coming to understand, its own small and terrifying kind
of growth.

I don't call Priya to tell her how it went. I don't call anyone. I
just walk, in the rain, past the chip shop with its shutters half up
for the evening trade, over the humped bridge where the Kelvin is
running high and fast after three days of weather, and let the not-
knowing sit in my chest the whole way home, uncomfortable and
entirely unmanaged, which is, I suspect, exactly what it's supposed to
feel like.

·

Wednesday passes with no word. I don't text. I don't call. I go to the
station and run the ordinary business of the place — a broken cart
machine, a volunteer's holiday request, a conversation with Priya
about the rebuilt automation queue that she's finished a full week
early and is, understandably, enormously proud of — and let the
silence from Nadia's direction simply exist, without trying to fill
it or manage it or fix it into something more comfortable.

It is, I think, the hardest single day of self-restraint I have
managed in six years of running this building, and nobody watching
would have any idea, from the outside, that anything unusual was
happening at all. I answer emails. I check the roof fund. I make tea
for a volunteer who's had a difficult week and doesn't especially want
to talk about it, and let him not talk about it, because I am, it
turns out, actually rather good at that particular skill once I stop
trying to apply it to myself.

Thursday morning, at ten to six, my phone buzzes once. *I'll do it.
Six fifty-eight, same as always. Don't make a thing of it when I get
there.*

I don't make a thing of it. I sit with the message for a long moment,
in the grey half-light of a Thursday that is about to become, one way
or another, the most important broadcast this station has ever put
out, and then I get up, and get dressed, and go to work.

I text Priya on the way in, the only person who needs to know before
the desk needs to know. *She's coming. Six fifty-eight. Whatever
happens tonight, I need you steady on the automation queue, no
surprises, nothing off script that we haven't planned for.*

*Understood*, Priya writes back, and then, a moment later: *I've
already triple-checked everything twice. I'm not letting anything go
wrong tonight. Not this one.*

I believe her completely, which is itself new — six weeks ago I'd have
checked the queue myself regardless of what she told me, out of the
same reflex that's run this building for nine years. This morning I
just put the phone away, and trust her, and keep walking.

I ring Kirsty, too, before I reach the station, because I've decided,
somewhere in the small hours, that if tonight is going to be honest,
it should be honest with the people who might be able to help as well
as the ones who might be hurt by it.

"I need a favour," I say, "and I need you not to ask why until
afterward."

"Go on."

"If tonight's broadcast goes the way I think it might, the panel's
going to hear about it fast, and I'd like someone in that room who
understands it wasn't a stunt before Douglas gets to frame it as one."

There's a pause, brief, and then: "I'll be listening. Live, from home,
not from the office. And if it's what I think it's going to be, I'll
say so to whoever needs to hear it, first thing tomorrow."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet," Kirsty says, echoing, without knowing it, almost
exactly what she said to me over coffee weeks ago. "Just do the thing
properly. I'll handle my end of it."

I hang up feeling, for the first time all week, that the odds might
not be quite so stacked against us as they've felt every single day
since the automation queue picked the wrong file.

·

Fiona rings that same afternoon, having heard some garbled version of
events through the group chat's ever-widening circle, and doesn't ask
me to explain the whole of it, which I'm grateful for, because I don't
currently have the words to make it make sense to someone who hasn't
lived every hour of the last three days.

"I just want you to know I'm here," she says, "whichever way tonight
goes. You don't have to manage me through this one either. I just
wanted to say it before it happens, in case there isn't a good moment
after."

"Thank you."

"Is she going to be alright? Really?"

"I don't know," I say, which is the only honest answer I've had all
week for that particular question, and Fiona, for once, doesn't push
for a more comfortable one.

"You've done the right thing, asking rather than managing," she says,
eventually. "I know it doesn't feel like enough. It rarely does, when
it's the right thing rather than the easy one. But it is the right
thing, and I wanted you to hear that from someone who isn't currently
standing in the middle of it with you."

I sit with that for a while after we hang up, in the office, with the
amber bulb glowing faintly through the wall, and let myself feel,
properly, for the first time in days, how frightened I actually am —
not managed-frightened, not filed-away-for-later frightened, just
plainly, uncomfortably afraid of how tonight might go, and of how
little control I actually have over the outcome, however carefully
I've built the case for it.

It doesn't fix anything. But it does, somehow, make the fear easier to
carry into the evening than it would have been left unexamined,
which is, I am slowly learning, most of what feeling a thing properly
is actually for.

·

Priya finds me still sitting there twenty minutes later and doesn't
say anything at first, just puts the kettle on, its usual four-minute
cycle ticking over in the background while neither of us fills the
silence with anything at all.

"You're allowed to be frightened too, you know," she says, eventually,
handing me a mug I don't remember asking for. "Everyone keeps checking
on Nadia. Rightly. But you've been managing a catastrophe for four days
straight and nobody's asked you how you're actually doing, including
me, until right now."

"I'm alright."

"You're not, but I appreciate you saying so anyway." She sits on the
edge of the desk, mug in hand, in the specific posture of someone
settling in for a conversation she's decided is necessary regardless of
whether the other person wants to have it. "You've built this entire
building out of being the person who's alright so everyone else doesn't
have to worry about you. I've watched you do it for four years. I just
want you to know that some of us have noticed, and some of us don't
actually need you to be alright all the time for the building to keep
standing."

"When did you get so wise, Priya?"

"I've always been this wise," she says, with enormous, entirely
justified confidence. "You've just been too busy managing everyone
else's crises to properly notice." She softens, slightly. "Tonight's
going to be hard, whichever way it goes. I need you to actually let it
be hard, rather than managing your way past the hard part before
you've let yourself feel any of it. That's the only thing I'm asking."

I don't have a ready answer for that, so I just drink the tea she's
made me, and let the kettle's quiet click as it finishes stand in for
whatever words I don't currently have, and am grateful, not for the
first time this autumn, for a seventeen-year-old who sees rather more
of me than I generally let anyone see.

·

I ring Fiona once more before the evening's out, mostly because I've
promised her I would, and mostly because saying it all out loud twice
in one day seems to be doing more good than saying it once.

"She said yes," I tell her. "Or something close enough to a yes that
I'm choosing to treat it as one."

"How do you feel?"

"Terrified. Hopeful. Both, constantly, in a way that doesn't seem to
resolve into just one or the other no matter how long I sit with it."

"That sounds about right, for something that actually matters," Fiona
says. "The stuff that doesn't matter resolves easily. The stuff that
does tends to just sit there being both things at once until it's
over, one way or the other."

"That's not very comforting."

"I'm not trying to comfort you. I'm trying to be accurate," Fiona
says, and I recognise the phrase immediately as pure Nadia, filtered
somehow through my sister after two months of secondhand reports, as
though the whole of my life has quietly started running on the same
vocabulary from every direction at once. "Go to bed. Sleep if you can.
Tomorrow's going to need whatever you've got left in the tank, and
none of it's going to arrive by staying up worrying about the version
of tonight that hasn't happened yet."

I do try to sleep. I manage perhaps two hours of it, in patches,
before the grey light starts at the edges of the curtains and there's
nothing left to do except get up and go and find out, at last, which
version of tomorrow we're actually going to get.

·

I think, in the last stretch of dark before I finally give up on
sleep entirely, about everything this building has taught me across
nine years, and about how little of it, until this particular autumn,
ever touched the parts of me that actually needed teaching.

I learned how to source a transmitter part from a decommissioned
hospital in Dundee. I learned how to write a letter that changes a
councillor's mind. I learned, at nineteen, how to be the reliable one,
and I got so good at it that reliability quietly became the entire
architecture of my adult life, load-bearing and mostly invisible, the
way the best-built things in an old building tend to be, right up
until the day something tests them properly for the first time.

Tonight is testing it properly. Not the roof. Not the panel. A woman
behind a door who has told me, plainly, that she can't be fixed, only
accompanied, and a version of myself that has spent a decade equating
love with usefulness, now being asked to prove it knows the
difference.

I don't know, lying in the grey half-light, whether I've actually
learned the difference in time, or whether tonight is simply the exam
I've been unconsciously revising for across this entire strange
autumn, without ever quite realising that's what all of it was for. I
get up, eventually, without an answer to that either, and go to find
out the only way there ever is: by showing up, properly, and doing the
thing.

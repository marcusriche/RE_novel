## Chapter Twenty-Two — On the Level (Broadcast Six)

She's at the desk at six fifty-eight, headphones on, both cups on this
time, which I clock the second I walk in and don't trust myself to say
anything about.

"We're live in ninety seconds," Nadia says. "Get to the desk."

I get to the desk. I have a rundown in my hand that I wrote three days
ago, in a version of this evening where everything had gone according
to plan, and I know, looking at her, that we are not doing that
rundown tonight.

Priya's on the desk beside her, headset on, more composed than she has
any right to be given the week we've all just had, and she catches my
eye once, briefly, in a look that says, as clearly as words could,
*I've got the desk, whatever you two are about to do, I've got the
desk.* I have never in my life been so grateful for a seventeen-year-
old.

"You came," I say to Nadia, quietly, under the pre-show chatter, the
only thing I let myself say that isn't strictly business.

"I said I'd think about it."

"You did."

"I thought about it," Nadia says, "for three days, and then I thought:
if I don't come tonight, the last thing anyone hears from this show is
an accident. I'd rather the last thing be mine."

We go live. The rundown lasts about two minutes — long enough for an
opening link, not long enough to reach the first scripted question —
before I do the thing I came here to do, the only thing I actually
planned, walking over: I hand her the second mic.

"This is Nadia," I say, on air, live, to however many people are
listening at seven o'clock on a Thursday. "She's been the voice behind
Static for two years, and she's about to tell you why, if she wants
to. And if she doesn't, that's the whole segment, and we'll play a
song."

I mean every word of it. I have no idea, saying it, which way this
goes.

Nadia is quiet for a second that feels considerably longer than a
second, on live radio, with dead air doing exactly what dead air does
to two people who have spent six weeks being professionally terrified
of it.

The phone lines start lighting up almost immediately, which none of
us expected, four calls in the first ten seconds, all of them, Priya
tells me afterward, from regulars who've clocked exactly what
"Static" means and have simply been waiting, patiently, for weeks, for
somebody to say the word out loud. Priya doesn't put any of them
through. She just watches the board light up, and watches Nadia, and
waits, along with the rest of us, for whatever comes next.

Then she talks.

She tells the truth about Static, in her own words, on her own terms —
the two years, the reason, the podcast that still exists and that she
has never listened to all the way through. She tells it plainly,
without performing it, the way she's told me every true thing since
the night on the fire escape, and somewhere in the middle of it I
understand why it had to wait exactly this long: not because anyone
was hiding it from the listeners forever, but because it had to wait
until it was hers to give, on her own schedule, in her own voice,
chosen rather than taken.

"Two years ago," she says, "somebody made a podcast about the worst
year of my life, using my name, without asking me. Twelve episodes.
I've listened to two of them. I don't need the other ten, because I
lived them, and I know how the story goes, and I've spent two years
letting a desk and a set of headphones be the version of me that
nobody could turn into content. That's what Static was. It still is,
some nights. But I'm tired of it being the only version of me anybody
gets, and I decided, about a week ago, that I'd rather risk being seen
badly than keep being unseen carefully, forever, on principle."

There's a caller Priya finally lets through, near the end, an older
woman who says only, "hen, that took some nerve," and hangs up before
Nadia can answer, which turns out, somehow, to be exactly the right
amount of response for the moment to hold.

"I stopped being a person people talked about," she says, near the
end of it, "and started being a voice with a desk in front of it. I'm
choosing, right now, live, not to be that anymore. Not because anyone's
making me. Because I want to try being seen and see what's actually
left standing."

I let the silence that follows sit for exactly as long as it needs to.

Then, because I can hear her breathing change, I say the only thing
that's actually mine to say into that silence: "You don't have to say
anything else. That's enough. That was always going to be enough."

"I'm not finished," Nadia says, and something in her voice has
shifted — steadier now, less like a person confessing and more like a
person deciding. "I want to say the other thing too, the one I've
never said on any recording, private or otherwise. I was frightened
of exactly this. Being a story again. And I did it anyway, because
somewhere in the last six weeks I found out there's a difference
between being a story and being known, and I'd rather risk the second
one than keep hiding from both."

Then I say the thing I promised her on a fire escape, six weeks and a
lifetime ago, out loud, on the record, in front of everyone.

"What's real off that mic stays off the record," I say. "Every time.
I promised her that on the night before our first broadcast. And
everything she's just told you, she chose to put on the record
herself. That's the difference. It's the only one that's ever
mattered."

·

Off air, the ON AIR bulb goes dark — amber, still, never fixed — and
we stand in a basement that smells of damp and cable insulation and
say nothing useful to each other for a while, because nothing useful
is required.

"You didn't have to hand me the mic," Nadia says, eventually.

"I know," I say. "I wanted you to have it before you decided whether
you wanted it."

Priya comes and stands with us for a minute, not saying anything, just
present, the three of us in a basement that smells of damp and cable
insulation, having just done the strangest and most honest hour of
live radio any of us will ever be part of.

"The phones haven't stopped," she says, eventually, holding up a
handset. "I stopped answering about four minutes in. I didn't think
you'd want an audience for whatever this was going to be."

"Thank you," I say, and mean it more than the two words can carry.

"Don't thank me. Go home. Both of you. I've got the desk, and I've
got the phones, and I've got, apparently, a great deal more sense than
either of the two adults currently running this station." She says it
with a smile that undercuts the sternness entirely, and shoos us both
toward the stairs like she's the one who's been managing this building
for six years and not the other way round.

We go up the stairs into a Glasgow evening that has, for once, no
rain in it at all, just a clear, cold September dark with a moon low
over the tenements, and stand on Dumbarton Road for a moment, not
saying anything, letting the last hour settle into something we can
actually hold rather than something still happening to us.

"I need to say the last thing," Nadia says, eventually, "and then I'm
done talking for the night."

"Say it."

"I'm not fixed. What happened on Wednesday doesn't get undone because
tonight went well. I'm going to have bad days about this for a long
time, probably. I wanted you to know that going in, so tonight
doesn't get to be the ending of it in your head, even though it's
going to feel like one."

"I know," I say. "I wasn't expecting fixed. I was expecting you, and
I got you, and that was always the whole of what I was hoping for."

We walk for a while without any particular destination, down toward
the river in the cold clear dark, past the chip shop, over the humped
bridge where the Kelvin runs black and fast below us, retracing, more
or less exactly, the route of the first ordinary evening we ever
spent together, chips on a wall on Byres Road, before any of this had
a name.

"Do you remember," Nadia says, at the bridge, "the first time we
walked back from that chip shop, and neither of us closed the gap
between our hands, on purpose, because we'd agreed to that without
saying so?"

"I remember."

"I think about that walk more than almost anything else from the last
six weeks. Not tonight. Not the broadcast, or the mic, or any of it.
That walk, where nothing happened at all, except two people deciding,
without a single word between them, that something was worth being
careful with."

"Why that one?"

"Because it's the one where it was still entirely ours," Nadia says.
"Before the panel knew. Before the podcast. Before any of it got
complicated by anyone else's opinion about what it meant. Just two
people on a bridge, not touching, on purpose, because the not-touching
mattered more than touching would have."

I take her hand properly this time, there on the bridge, and she lets
me, and neither of us says anything else about it, because some
things, having been said once, don't need saying twice to be true.

·

We stand there a while longer, the Kelvin running black and fast below
us, the city's ordinary Thursday-night noise carrying up from
Dumbarton Road behind us, and Nadia says, eventually, quietly, "I want
to tell you the rest of it. Not tonight. Soon. The whole of what the
podcast actually cost me, properly, not in pieces."

"Whenever you're ready."

"I know. That's rather the point of telling you now — that I trust the
whenever, finally, instead of bracing for you to need it sooner." She
looks out at the water. "Three years ago I'd have said this bridge was
just a bridge. Tonight it's the place I told you the truth had to wait
until it was mine to give. I don't think I'll ever cross it again
without thinking of that, and I find, oddly, that I don't mind."

"Neither will I."

"Good," she says, and that's the whole of it, standing on a bridge in
a clear cold September dark, two women who've spent six weeks building
something out of scraps and found, in the end, that it held.

We walk the rest of the way home slowly, in no hurry at all, past the
chip shop with its shutters half up for the late trade, and Nadia
stops once more, outside my own close this time, before we go up.

"I want to say one more thing," she says, "and then I really am done
talking for the night, properly this time."

"Go on."

"Thank you for handing me the mic before you decided whether I wanted
it. I don't think I've properly said that yet, not the actual thank
you, underneath everything else I said on air. It mattered more than
the broadcast did. It mattered that you did it without knowing which
way I'd go."

"I'd do it again."

"I know," Nadia says. "That's rather why it mattered in the first
place," and we go up together, into a flat that has slowly, over six
weeks, stopped being a place I arranged to survive being alone in, and
become, instead, somewhere I actually live.

I ring Fiona before I sleep, briefly, just to say it went well,
without going into the whole of it, because some of tonight belongs
only to the bridge and the two of us who stood on it.

"That's all you're giving me? 'It went well'?"

"For tonight. I'll tell you properly another time."

"Fine," Fiona says, with theatrical, entirely good-natured
reluctance. "I'll allow the withholding, this once, because you sound
happier on the phone right now than I've heard you sound in about a
decade, and I'm choosing to take that as sufficient information for
one evening."

"Goodnight, Fiona."

"Goodnight. Go to sleep smiling. You've earned it," she says, and rings
off, and I do exactly that, in a flat that no longer feels like
somewhere I merely arranged to survive being alone in.

I lie awake a while longer anyway, not out of nerves this time, just
the specific reluctance to let a good day end before you've turned it
over properly at least once. I think about the whole of it, start to
finish — a Tuesday, a folder of bad numbers, a sentence I said out loud
before I'd checked whether it was true, and everything that grew,
slowly, out of a lie that turned out to have more true parts hiding
inside it than either of us had accounted for at the start.

I think, too, about Malcolm's two rules, and my mother's cassette tape
saying almost exactly the same thing years before either of us
compared notes, and Priya's laminated certificate on my fridge, and
Fiona's voice down a phone line telling me, patiently, for months, that
staying wasn't the same as settling. All of it, somehow, arriving here,
on this particular ordinary Thursday night, in a basement that still
leaks in three places and a flat that finally has something worth
keeping on its walls.

Whatever finishes here finishes privately, off any record at all,
which is exactly and only how either of us would have wanted it.

# OUT OF OFFICE

### Kelvin Row, Book One

**Wren Halliday**

---

*For the people who keep the lights on in buildings nobody funds.*

---

**Content notes:** on-page grief for a parent · closed-door sexual content
· alcohol use · a past relationship made public without consent.

---

I tell the funding panel we have a couples hour. Live, Thursdays, six
weeks, an actual segment with an actual couple in it, the words out of my
mouth before I've checked whether either of those things is true.

Neither is. The roof leaks in three places I can name by bucket size. The
only person in the building both discreet enough and present enough to
fake a relationship with me is the overnight engineer, who has said
roughly eleven words to me in three years, four of which were about a
cable.

She says yes anyway, with conditions, written on the back of a cue sheet
in block capitals like a technical spec.

Six weeks. Six broadcasts. A kiss that's scripted and a kiss that isn't.
A secret she's kept off the record for two years and a promise I make her
before either of us knows what it will cost.

Somewhere around week four, live, on air, a caller asks how we knew we
could trust each other completely, and neither of us reaches for the
scripted answer.

We have two broadcasts left. I am running out of format.

---


## Chapter One — The Panel

I should say, before any of this, that I am not a person who does
things like this. I file paperwork on time. I keep a spreadsheet of
which volunteer owes which shift to which other volunteer, going back
four years, cross-referenced by favour and grudge. I have never once,
in six years of running a community radio station out of a basement
that used to be a coal store, said a single sentence in an official
meeting that I had not already rehearsed, out loud, on a bus, twice.

Then a Tuesday happens, and apparently that's the version of me you
get instead.

Here is the sentence I say to the funding panel, out loud, with my whole
chest, before I have checked whether a single word of it is true:

"Yes, of course we have a new couples hour. Six weeks, live, Thursdays."

I want it on the record — my own record, the one I keep in my head for
later, at four in the morning, when I run back over every stupid thing
I have ever said out loud — that I did not plan to say this. I had a
folder. The folder had numbers in it, and every column of numbers was
bad, and I had rehearsed, on the bus, a version of this meeting that
involved words like *restructuring* and *engagement pathway* and
absolutely nothing about anybody's love life.

The room is a committee room in the Chambers annexe, the kind with a
carpet designed specifically to disguise thirty years of spilled
coffee and a ventilation system that makes everyone sound slightly
underwater. There are six people at the table and a folder in front of
each of them, all identical, all containing the same set of numbers I
have spent a fortnight arranging into the most flattering order
available, which is still not a flattering order.

Douglas opens with the transmitter contract, which is not going well.
Then he opens with the roof, which is going worse. Then, somewhere
around minute eight, a woman in a blazer I don't recognise — new to
the panel, evidently, because everyone else at the table has the
specific weary fondness of people who've sat through four years of my
folders — leans forward and asks, not unkindly, whether Kelvin Row has
"any relationship-facing content in the pipeline."

I have never heard the phrase *relationship-facing content* before in
my life. I will hear it in my sleep for the rest of this book.

"It's part of the newer engagement criteria," she adds, when I don't
answer fast enough. "Audience-identifiable, relatable, ideally
recurring. Stations doing well on renewal this cycle have all had
something like it. A cookery segment with a couple. A local sports
double-act. That sort of thing."

I look at my folder. My folder has, among its other failures, no
cookery segment, no local sports double-act, and absolutely nothing
resembling a couple, recurring or otherwise, because the entirety of
my own relationship history for the past four years could be summarised
as *occasionally texts back*.

And something in me — six years of running a station on a leaking roof
and a prayer, probably, if I'm being generous to myself, which I try
not to be — just goes.

"Yes."

The room does the thing rooms do when somebody has said something
interesting. Three people who were checking their phones stop checking
their phones.

"A couples hour," I say, because apparently I am doing this now. "Live.
On air. Real people, working through it, in real time." I have no idea
what *it* is. I have no idea who the real people are. I am, as far as I
can currently establish, the only real person in this scenario, and I
am lying to a room full of civil servants about my own love life, which
does not exist, in order to save a transmitter.

"Six weeks," I say. "Thursdays."

I don't know where the six comes from. It sounds like a number a serious
person would say. Six is a good number — long enough to be a
commitment, short enough that nobody has to actually watch me fail at
this in real time for more than a month and a half.

I look properly, for the first time since I sat down, at the far end
of the table, and understand, with an unhelpful lurch, why the room
felt different the moment I walked in. Kirsty Lennox is on the panel
now. Eight years ago we did breakfast together, three hours a day,
five days a week, and now she's in a blazer with a lanyard that says
GLASGOW COMMUNITY MEDIA RENEWAL PANEL and she is writing something
down without once looking up at me.

"That's very encouraging," Douglas says, in the tone of a man who has
never once, in four years, said anything that wasn't a euphemism.
"The panel would want to see that content develop properly across the
renewal period. Something the community can follow. Root for."

"Of course," I say.

"We'll look forward to it," Douglas says, and that's it. That's the
whole meeting. Twelve minutes, one folder of numbers nobody looked at,
and a promise I have made in public, in a room with minutes being
taken, about a relationship that is currently occupied by exactly
nobody.

I walk out into the rain on George Street not knowing one single fact
about the show I have just committed the station's entire licence
renewal to, including — and I want to be honest about the scale of this
— who is in it.

·

The thing about Glasgow rain in September is that it doesn't fall so
much as arrive, sideways, off the Clyde, in sheets you can watch coming
up the street like weather with an agenda. I stand under the awning of
the Chambers annexe for four minutes deciding whether to get the subway
or just accept the rain as a fact about the rest of my day, and
somewhere in those four minutes I stop thinking about the meeting and
start thinking about the roof.

The roof is not a metaphor. The roof is an actual roof, over an actual
basement, and it leaks in three specific places that Priya has labelled
by bucket size, and every single one of those buckets is downstream,
structurally and financially, of what just happened in that room.

I get the subway. I do not enjoy it. The Glasgow subway is a small
orange circle that takes you nowhere quickly and everywhere eventually,
and I have never once, in six years, managed to have a thought on it
that wasn't interrupted by the specific screech the tunnel makes
between Kelvinbridge and St George's Cross, which today feels like
the whole transport network personally weighing in on my decision-
making.

By the time I'm coming down Dumbarton Road toward the tenement, I have
run the maths four times and got four different answers, none of them
good, and the sign over the close mouth — KELVIN ROW RADIO, hand-painted,
1987, repainted twice since and never once redesigned — is doing the
thing it does in rain, which is look exactly as permanent and exactly
as precarious as it actually is.

I go down the basement stairs into the smell that means home to me now
in a way I try not to examine too closely: damp concrete, warm dust off
old equipment, and somewhere underneath it, always, cold tea.

I came here first at nineteen, a volunteer with a media studies
diploma nobody had asked for and an ability to stay calm on a phone
line that turned out to matter considerably more than the diploma
ever did. The founder, a retired shipyard electrician called Malcolm
who ran the station out of pure stubbornness for eleven years before
his knees gave out, handed me the keys on his last day with the exact
words, "don't let it become boring, and don't let it close," which I
have taken, ever since, as the entire job description, more useful by
a distance than anything in my actual contract.

I became manager at twenty-five, not because I was ready, but because
there was nobody else standing in the room when the question got
asked, and I have spent the nine years since discovering that most of
running a community station is exactly that: being the one still
standing in the room when a question needs answering, whether or not
you feel qualified to answer it. The roof has been failing since
before I arrived. The transmitter is older than most of our
volunteers. None of that has ever once been the actual point. The
point is a red bulb over a door that tells the street outside somebody
is talking to somebody else in this city right now, live, uncut, and
that the somebody talking might, on a good night, say something worth
hearing.

·

There are three buckets in the corridor before you even reach the
studio, and I know them by name because Priya has named them. SMALL
LEAK is a margarine tub under the fuse board. MEDIUM LEAK is an actual
bucket, from an actual builder's merchant, under the join where the
extension meets the original close. BIG LEAK — capital letters, Sharpie,
underlined twice — sits directly under a patch of ceiling in the main
studio that has been the colour and texture of a well-used teabag since
before I started here.

Priya is sitting under the ON AIR bulb with a laptop, a headset half on,
and the specific expression of a seventeen-year-old who has been running
a Saturday shift alone since two in the afternoon and has strong
feelings about it that she is choosing, for now, not to escalate.

"How was the panel," she says, not looking up, in the tone of someone
asking how the dentist went.

"They want a couples hour."

"They want *what*."

"Audience-identifiable relational content." I say it the way Douglas
said it, all six syllables of *relational* laid out like a man setting
a trap he doesn't understand is a trap. "Live, Thursdays, six weeks."

Priya looks up properly now. She has the specific stillness of a
teenager deciding whether the adult in front of her has finally lost it,
weighing that against four years of evidence that the adult in front of
her has never, technically, had anything to lose it from, because there
has never been very much to begin with.

"You don't have a couple," she says.

"No."

"You have a roof problem and a Tuesday."

"I'm aware."

She closes the laptop, which from Priya is the equivalent of standing
up and pacing. "Robyn. I want to be supportive. I do the gossip *and*
the desk, I am extremely well positioned to be supportive. But you have
just promised a licensing panel a segment that requires two consenting
adults who are, ideally, dating, and the only two consenting adults in
this building right now are you and me, and I am seventeen, and even
setting that aside I would frankly rather eat the mic foam."

"I'm not asking you."

"I would like that on record too."

I sit down on the good chair — the one with all four wheels, which
Priya has clearly claimed as her own and which I am, this once, taking
back by seniority — and put my head in my hands for exactly as long as
I allow myself these things, which is about four seconds.

"The roof," I say, into my palms. "The transmitter contract. Your
stipend, which is the only wage that comes out of this building that
isn't mine, and mine doesn't count because I'd do this for nothing and
we both know it."

"I know what the renewal pays for."

"So I need a show. A real one. Live."

Priya looks at the ceiling — at BIG LEAK, specifically, which has
chosen this exact moment to produce a single, theatrical drip into its
bucket — and back at me.

"You don't have a couple," she says again, more gently this time, "but
you might have a candidate."

"Don't."

"I'm not saying anything."

"You're saying something."

"I'm saying," Priya says, picking her laptop back up, entirely too
pleased with herself for someone who is about to go back to running a
Saturday shift alone, "that there's a woman two rooms over who's been
here every single overnight for three years, who nobody looks at twice,
who could not be less interested in being *seen*, which — and I want
you to notice I am not naming her — is precisely the qualification you
currently need."

I don't say anything.

Priya, watching me not say anything, allows herself the smallest,
most infuriating version of a smile, and puts her headset back on.

"I'm not doing it," I say, eventually, to the back of her headset,
which does not respond, because she has, with characteristic
efficiency, already gone back to whatever she was doing on the
laptop and is choosing not to dignify the sentence with a reply.

"I mean it, Priya."

"I heard you the first time," she says, without looking up. "You said
it very firmly. I noted it down. I've also noted down that you're
still standing in this doorway forty seconds later instead of leaving,
which tells me something rather different."

I leave. Properly this time, up the stairs, past BIG LEAK's bucket,
which has filled another quarter-inch since I last looked at it, and
out into a rain that has, if anything, got worse in the last hour, as
though the whole city has opinions about my afternoon and has decided
to express them meteorologically.

I don't go back to my flat straight away. I walk the long way, down
past the overnight studio's external door — the one that opens onto
the lane rather than the foyer, the one only the overnight engineer
ever really uses — and stand there for a moment in the rain doing
absolutely nothing useful at all, before telling myself, sternly, that
this is not a reconnaissance mission, and going home.

My flat, when I finally get there, is on the second floor of a
tenement two streets from the station, chosen nine years ago
specifically for its proximity rather than any charm of its own,
which it does not particularly have. I make tea I don't drink, which
is becoming something of a theme for the evening, and sit by the
window watching the rain do its thing to Dumbarton Road, and think,
for longer than is strictly useful, about the precise expression on
Nadia Quinn's face through a pane of studio glass three years running,
and what it might mean that I've apparently been cataloguing it
without ever quite admitting to myself that I was doing so.

·

My sister rings at nine, which is either very good or very bad timing,
depending on how the next forty minutes go, because Fiona has never
once in her life let a piece of family news go un-interrogated, and I
have not yet decided how much of today I'm prepared to hand over.

"You sound strange," she says, by way of hello, before I've said
anything at all beyond my own name. "Strange how?"

"I promised the licensing panel a couples hour."

There's a pause on the line long enough that I can hear her doing the
thing she does when she's recalibrating — a small exhale, the specific
sound of a woman who left the family business of worrying about Kelvin
Row behind eleven years ago and has never quite managed to stop doing
it from a distance. Fiona wanted the bigger thing, always did, and got
it — a proper job, a proper flat, a proper distance from a basement
that smells of damp and cold tea — and some nights I think she's the
only person in my life who understands exactly what staying cost me,
because she's the one who watched me choose it.

"With who," she says, eventually.

"I haven't worked that out yet."

"Robyn."

"I said yes before I'd worked out the who. I know how that sounds."

"It sounds," Fiona says, in the specific tone she's used on me since
we were children and she was twelve minutes older and considerably
more sensible, "like you did the thing Mum used to do. Say yes to the
big thing first and work out the mechanics standing in the rain
afterward."

I don't have an answer for that, because it's accurate, and because
hearing our mother mentioned so plainly, on a random Tuesday, still
does something to my chest six years on that I haven't fully learned
to brace for.

"How's the roof," Fiona asks, gentler now, which is her way of letting
me off the hook for the Mum comment without either of us having to
name that she's doing it.

"Leaking in three places. Priya's named the buckets."

"Of course she has." A smile in her voice, finally. "You know you
could just let it close, don't you. Nobody would think less of you.
You've given it nine years. Nobody gets nine years out of a person and
then calls them a failure for stopping."

"I know."

"You're not going to let it close, though."

"No," I say, "I'm not," and she laughs, not unkindly, the specific
laugh of someone who asked a question she already knew the answer to
purely to hear me say it out loud, and we talk for a while longer
about nothing in particular — her new flat, a nephew's school play,
the ordinary furniture of two sisters staying in each other's lives at
a manageable distance — before she rings off with the same thing she
always says, half joke and half genuine instruction: "don't do anything
I wouldn't do," to which I no longer have a good answer, given what I
appear to have already done.

·

I don't sleep well, which is not, on its own, remarkable — I haven't
slept well on a Tuesday in roughly six years, on the theory that
Wednesday's admin always arrives whether or not I'm rested for it —
but tonight it's a different flavour of not-sleeping, the kind where
the mind keeps circling back to one specific fact rather than the
usual scattered worry about rotas and roofs.

This is not the first time the panel has come close to closing us.
Three years ago it was the transmitter, a fault that would have cost
more to fix than the station's entire annual grant, and I spent six
weeks that spring learning more about second-hand broadcast equipment
than any reasonable person should ever need to know, sourcing a part
from a decommissioned hospital radio service in Dundee that, by any
sensible measure, should not have worked, and did, for reasons I still
don't fully understand and have chosen not to examine too closely in
case examining it makes it stop.

The year before that it was a funding review that very nearly folded
four stations like ours into one regional service, run out of a proper
building with proper heating and none of Kelvin Row's actual history
in it, and I spent that entire summer writing letters, individually, by
hand, to every councillor who'd listen, until the review quietly
changed its own mind and left small stations alone, at least for that
cycle.

I have never once lost this building. I have come close enough times
that the closeness itself has become a kind of muscle memory, a
specific tightness in my chest that arrives whenever a meeting runs
long or an email from the panel's admin address lands with no subject
line. Tonight, lying awake with a woman's name circling in my head
that has nothing to do with the roof at all, I notice, for the first
time in longer than I can place, that the tightness isn't there. Just
the ordinary nerves of having said something reckless out loud in a
committee room, and underneath it, something else entirely, that I
don't yet have a name for and am not in any hurry to find one for
tonight.


## Chapter Two — The Only Candidate

I run the actual list at midnight, sitting in the office with a mug of
tea I made forty minutes ago and have not touched, because running lists
is a thing I do instead of sleeping, and midnight is when the lists get
honest.

Sanjay presents well but tells everyone within a week, because Sanjay
has never once in his life kept a secret that wasn't actively illegal.
Fiona would do it, and would be good at it, and would also treat it as
an audition for something bigger, which is fair enough — Fiona wants a
bigger thing, and good for her — but is not what a six-week format needs
from its second lead. Marcus would overact it so hard the panel would
think we'd hired actors.

I've run through worse lists than this one, if I'm honest. I ran a
version of this exact exercise nineteen years old, having just arrived
at the station as a volunteer with nothing to offer except that I
never once missed a shift, working out which of the six other
volunteers I could trust to cover for me if my mum had a bad week.
There is a version of me that has been running this particular kind of
list — who's reliable, who's discreet, who can be trusted with
something that matters — since before I was legally allowed to sign
for the equipment I was operating, and there is a tired, four-in-the-
morning part of me that recognises exactly what it says about a life
that the skill has transferred so cleanly from *who can cover the
Tuesday slot* to *who can pretend to love me on the radio*.

I don't examine that thought properly either. There's a lot, this
particular week, that I am choosing not to examine properly, and I am
building, without noticing it, quite a substantial queue.

And then there's Nadia, who engineers the overnight slot, who has said
roughly eleven words to me in three years.

I know this because I counted, once, out of the specific boredom that
sets in around three in the morning when you're the only two people
awake in a basement and one of you is behind glass. Eleven words.
Four of them were about a cable.

The case for Nadia, run coldly, the way I run everything when I'm
tired enough to be honest about it: she's here six nights a week
already, so the schedule solves itself. She has no on-air persona of
her own to protect, no reputation as a presenter that a fake romance
could tarnish. She is, by a very wide margin, the most discreet person
on the payroll, in the sense that she has said, cumulatively, less to
me in three years than Priya says to me in a single shift.

I do not, at midnight, examine why that last part might be a problem
rather than a qualification. I will have plenty of time to examine it
later. Later is coming for me at a pace I have not yet appreciated.

I stand in the corridor outside the overnight studio for a count of
ten before I knock, if you can call it knocking when the door is glass
and she can see me the entire time I'm working up to it.

Through the glass: Nadia at the desk, one headphone cup on and one off,
the way she always sits, listening to the room the way other people
listen to music. There's a level meter in front of her doing something
gentle and green, and she is watching it the specific way you watch a
thing you trust completely.

She's compact, sturdy in a way I've always associated, probably
unfairly, with people who've spent a decade lifting flight cases in
and out of vans, forearms that look like they know things my own
don't. Grey's coming in at her temples early and she's made no attempt
at all to do anything about it, which I find, standing here working up
the nerve to ask her a genuinely unreasonable favour, unaccountably
reassuring. A woman who has stopped negotiating with her own grey
hairs is a woman who has, at minimum, made peace with something.

She doesn't look up. She knows I'm there. Of course she knows I'm
there — the whole point of her, professionally, is that she notices
everything and says almost none of it.

I knock anyway, because standing in a doorway working up the nerve to
ask someone to be your fake girlfriend is not something you get to do
silently.

·

I over-explain. I want that in the record too, because I am going to
spend a great deal of this book learning not to do it, and it would be
dishonest to start the story with a version of myself who already
knows how to stop.

I explain the panel. I explain the roof, again, though she was in the
building for the first explanation and does not, technically, need the
second. I explain Douglas's face when he said *audience-identifiable*,
and Priya's spreadsheet, and the transmitter contract.

"There's a woman on the panel now," I say, somewhere in minute four,
apropos of absolutely nothing Nadia has asked me, "who used to co-host
breakfast with me. Kirsty. She left for national radio eight years ago.
I mention this only because it's relevant to nothing and I appear to
be telling you anyway, which is a symptom of something."

Nadia says nothing. She doesn't even look up from the fader.

"The point is the show," I say, correcting course, or trying to,
"which needs to be believable, which means it needs two actual people
in it, and I have thought about this at length and there is, currently,
exactly one person on this payroll who fits the profile, discretion-
wise, and I recognise that puts you in a strange position, and I want
to be upfront that I've thought about the strangeness of the position
and I don't think that makes the position less strange, it just means
I've thought about it, which I suppose is its own kind of—"

"Robyn."

"Yes."

"You're doing the thing."

"What thing."

"The thing where you explain a request until the request stops sounding
like a request and starts sounding like weather. Something that's
simply happening to both of us, that neither of us decided."

I stop talking, mostly out of shock at being caught doing something I
didn't know I had a name for, and — because apparently there is no
natural end to over-explaining once it starts and stopping mid-flow
leaves an actual silence behind it that has to be filled with
*something* — I explain my own five-year plan for the station instead,
unprompted, entirely irrelevant, purely because I have run out of
relevant things to say and the silence where Nadia would normally
answer is doing something to me that talking seems, incorrectly, like
it might fix.

Nadia lets me finish. She has both headphone cups off now, folded
around her neck, which I will learn later is the equivalent, for her,
of putting a phone face-down on a table.

"No," she says.

I keep going. I am aware, dimly, distantly, the way you're aware of
traffic while you're crossing against a light, that I am doing the
thing where being told no is treated as information about the *shape*
of the ask rather than information about the ask itself, and I say it
again, differently — smaller stipend increase this time, a mention of
flexibility, the word *hypothetically* deployed twice in one sentence.

"You're not hearing me," Nadia says. Flat. Not unkind. Just entirely,
completely flat, the way a level meter is flat when there's nothing
coming through the desk at all. "I heard you the first time. I engineer
that segment because nobody looks at me while I do it. You're asking me
to be looked at. On purpose. By strangers, live, every Thursday, for
six weeks."

I go quiet.

This is, I want to note, an extremely rare event. I do not, generally,
go quiet. Going quiet is not a skill I have cultivated, professionally
or otherwise; I am a person who fills silences the way water fills a
bucket labelled BIG LEAK, immediately, and without being asked.

But there isn't anything left to fill it with, because she's right, and
I know she's right, and the only true thing available to me is smaller
than everything I've already said.

"I know," I say. "I'm asking anyway. Because I've run out of people
I'd rather ask."

Nadia looks at me for a long moment — the kind of look I will come, in
time, to recognise as her actually thinking, rather than her simply
waiting for me to stop talking — and doesn't say anything at all.

"For what it's worth," I say, standing in the doorway, coat already
half on, "I'm sorry. For asking. Properly sorry, not the version of
sorry where I then explain why I had to."

Nadia looks up at that — actually looks up, headphone cups still round
her neck — in a way she hasn't done through the whole of the previous
five minutes.

"Don't be sorry for asking," she says. "Be sorry if you ask again the
same way."

I don't have anything to say to that, so I don't try to manufacture
something. I just nod, and leave, and stand in the corridor for a
second afterward turning the sentence over, because it is, I am
starting to suspect, the single most useful piece of feedback anyone
has given me about myself in about a decade, delivered by a woman who
has, as far as the official record shows, said fewer than a dozen
words to me in three years.

I take the silence as a no, because it is, currently, the only
sensible thing to take it as, and I go home in the rain, past the
overnight studio's external door — which I don't stop at this time,
having already learned my lesson about that once tonight — not
knowing that the actual answer is going to arrive in about ninety
minutes, on the back of a cue sheet, in block capitals.

I think, walking home, about how little I actually know about her, for
someone I've just asked to be my girlfriend on public radio. I know
what shift she works. I know she takes her tea with milk, no sugar,
from three years of half-noticed observation rather than any actual
conversation. I don't know where she grew up, or what she studied, or
whether she has family in the city, or whether the reason she's so
good at being unseen is a skill she was born with or one she built,
deliberately, brick by brick, for a reason I haven't earned the right
to ask about yet.

It occurs to me, letting myself into my flat, dripping onto a mat that
has given up trying to cope with six years of Glasgow weather, that I
am about to spend six weeks constructing an elaborate public fiction
about a woman I know almost nothing true about at all, and that this
should probably worry me more than it currently does.

·

I do the thing I always do when a problem won't resolve, which is
make a list, on paper, at my own kitchen table, of everything I
actually do know about Nadia Quinn, assembled entirely from three years
of passive observation rather than a single direct question.

She takes her tea milk, no sugar. She arrives at the station eleven
minutes before every shift, never ten, never twelve, always eleven,
with a regularity I'd always assumed was coincidence and now suspect
is deliberate. She has a habit of touching the door frame of the
overnight studio on her way in, once, briefly, which I've never asked
about and have always found, without knowing why, quietly moving. She
laughs at almost nothing, and when she does, it's brief and completely
unguarded and gone again within a second, as though laughing for
longer than that would cost her something she isn't willing to spend.

It is, I realise, looking at the list, an inventory of a person built
entirely out of the outside of her — habits, timings, small
consistencies — with nothing at all about what any of it actually
means. I have catalogued a woman the way you'd catalogue a piece of
equipment you rely on but have never once opened up to see how it
works.

I fold the list and put it in a drawer, mostly out of embarrassment at
having made it, and go to bed telling myself, without much conviction,
that six weeks of proximity will surely fill in some of the blanks
whether either of us means it to or not.

·

I ring Fiona before I go to bed, which is against my own rule about not
calling her after ten, because I need to say the plan out loud to
someone who isn't Priya and isn't Nadia and isn't currently deciding
whether to be part of it.

"You want to what," Fiona says, in the specific flattened voice she
reserves for the moments when I have genuinely surprised her, which
does not happen often, because she's spent our whole lives assuming
she's seen every version of me there is to see.

"Ask the overnight engineer to be my fake girlfriend for a funding
panel."

"Robyn."

"I know."

"Do you even know this woman?"

"Not really," I admit, which costs me something to say out loud,
because I have spent the last hour composing an inventory of a person
built entirely out of tea preferences and doorframe habits, and hearing
myself describe the actual scale of what I don't know about her is
worse than I expected it to be. "I know her shift pattern. I know how
she takes her tea. I've counted the words she's said to me over three
years and it's not many."

"That's not a relationship, Robyn, fake or otherwise. That's a
surveillance file."

"It's temporary."

"Everything you've ever called temporary has lasted at minimum four
years," Fiona says, with the specific accuracy of a sister who has
been keeping her own quiet tally. "The station was temporary. The
manager job was temporary. You've been not-officially-dating this
station for nine years under the heading of temporary."

"This is different."

"Is it?" A pause, softer now. "I'm not trying to talk you out of it. I
think if you've decided to do it, you'll do it properly, because
that's the only setting you have. I just want you to notice, out loud,
to me, right now, before it starts, that you've built your whole life
out of being useful to institutions that can't love you back, and now
you're about to spend six weeks being visibly, publicly loved by a
woman you've never actually spoken to, and I don't think you've
thought once about what it might cost you to want that to be real."

I don't have an answer for that. I sit with the phone against my ear
for long enough that Fiona, eventually, lets me off the hook, the way
she always does once she's said the true thing and doesn't need to
grind it in further.

"Ring me after the first broadcast," she says. "I want the full
report. Every detail. I'm invested now, apparently, whether either of
us wanted that."

"You're allowed to be invested."

"I know I am," Fiona says. "I'm your sister. It's the one job I never
had to apply for," and rings off before I can answer that, leaving me
alone with a phone gone quiet and a great deal more to think about
than I had an hour ago.

·

I go in early the next morning, before Priya, before anyone, and sit at
the desk in the empty studio with the amber bulb dark and the whole
basement holding the specific hush it only has at that hour, before
the boiler's kicked in and the pipes have started their daily
complaint about the cold.

I run the list one more time, on paper this time rather than in my
head, because I've learned over six years that a problem written down
behaves differently to a problem merely turned over — it gets smaller,
usually, or at least it gets honest about its actual size rather than
whatever size worry has inflated it to overnight.

Nadia's name sits at the bottom of the page, underlined once, and I
look at it for a long time in the grey early light, trying to work out
whether underlining it was an accident of the pen or a decision I made
without quite noticing I was making it. I decide, eventually, that it
doesn't matter which. The name's underlined either way, and I'm not
going to un-underline it just because admitting to a decision after
the fact feels more exposed than pretending it happened on its own.

I fold the page and put it in my pocket rather than the drawer this
time, which I notice, walking down to put the kettle on before anyone
else arrives, is itself a small change in behaviour I don't examine
too closely, in case examining it talks me out of whatever I'm
currently, quietly, deciding to do.

The kettle takes its usual four minutes. I stand there watching it,
the way I'll come to stand and watch it a great many more times before
this particular fortnight is over, and think, not for the first time
this week, that some decisions arrive not as a single moment of
clarity but as a long accumulation of small ones — an underlined name,
a pocket instead of a drawer, a kettle you wait for instead of setting
and walking away from — until one morning you look up and realise
you've already decided, some time back, without ever holding a formal
vote on it with yourself.


## Chapter Three — Terms

She comes and finds me.

That's the part I didn't expect, and the part I'd think about for
weeks afterward: I had gone home, properly given it up as a no, and at
half past midnight my phone lights up with a text that says, in full,
*come back*, from a number I'd had saved for three years under NADIA —
OVERNIGHT and had never once used for anything except the rota.

I come back. Of course I come back. I would come back for considerably
less than this.

The walk from my flat to the station at half past midnight is fifteen
minutes I could do with my eyes shut, and mostly do — down past the
chip shop with its shutters down, along the stretch where the streetlights
go orange-then-white-then-orange again for no reason anyone's ever
been able to explain to me, over the little humped bridge where you can
just about see the Kelvin below, black and moving fast after a day of
rain. I don't let myself hope on the way there. Hoping, at half past
midnight, on a fifteen-minute walk, felt at the time like tempting
something.

She's at the desk when I get there, and she doesn't stand up, and she
doesn't say hello. She holds up a cue sheet — the paper kind, the ones
we still print for the automated overnight log because the software
crashes if you look at it wrong — and turns it over to show me the
back, where she's written three things in block capitals with the
station's own biro, the one on a string that nobody has ever managed
to lose.

"I've got conditions," she says.

"Okay."

"One." She doesn't look at the paper. She knows it already; she's
reading it to me from memory, the paper just there so I have something
to look at instead of her while she says it, which I understand, later,
is its own kind of kindness. "Nothing physical that isn't discussed
first. On air or off. I don't care how small. A hand on an arm gets
discussed before it happens, not apologised for after."

"Agreed."

"Two. My actual job doesn't get mentioned. Not as colour, not as
backstory, not as a fun fact for a caller. The overnight desk stays the
overnight desk. The theatre thing stays off the record entirely." A
beat. "There's a reason for that and I'm not explaining it tonight."

"You don't have to explain it at all."

"I know I don't," Nadia says, and something about the way she says it
— quick, almost defensive, like she'd braced for me to push and is
recalibrating now that I haven't — makes me think this is the first
time in a while anyone's taken *no explanation needed* at face value
rather than as an opening bid.

"Three." She finally looks up. "If I say stop — on air, off air, mid
sentence, doesn't matter — the segment ends there and nobody argues
about it live. Not you, not the panel, not a caller. It ends. Same
minute."

I look at the three lines on the back of the cue sheet, in a stranger's
handwriting that I am about to spend six weeks getting to know very
well, and I have the sudden, entirely inconvenient thought that I have
never once in my adult life had anyone hand me terms like this — not a
partner, not a landlord, not the panel, nobody — and that the fact
they're arriving now, from a woman who has said eleven words to me in
three years, is doing something to my chest that I do not have time to
examine tonight.

"Say roughly eleven words to me in three years," I say, "and then hand
me a legal document."

"Four of them were about a cable."

It's the first thing resembling a joke either of us has ever made in
each other's direction, and it lands so precisely and so drily that I
laugh before I've decided to, an actual laugh, the kind that surprises
you on the way out.

Nadia doesn't smile. She does something very close to it, around the
eyes, that I will learn to read as the real thing, the actual signal
under everything else.

"Is that a yes," I say.

"That's a yes," she says, "with three conditions and a fourth one I
haven't said out loud yet, which is that if this is going to work at
all, you need to stop apologising for asking me for things. You asked.
I said no. You asked again, worse. Then you told me the truth, which
was that you'd run out of people, and that's the only version of the
ask I was ever going to say yes to. So say things like that. Don't
manage me into it."

I don't say anything for a second, because I am doing the thing where
I file that away to take out again later, at four in the morning,
and turn over from every angle.

"Can I ask why," I say, instead of *understood*, because the question
has been sitting behind my teeth since I got the text. "Why you
changed your mind. You don't have to answer that either, obviously,
given everything you've just said about not owing me explanations."

Nadia looks at the cue sheet for a second, not at me. "Because you
came back," she says, eventually. "You went home. I watched you go on
the monitor, actually, out the front, and I thought — she's not going
to ask again, she's decided it's a no and she's letting it be a no.
And then I texted you two words and you were back inside twenty
minutes, in the rain, without asking what I wanted first." A small
shrug, more in the shoulders than anywhere else. "That's the version
of you I could work with. Not the one who explains. The one who
shows up."

"Understood," I say, eventually, and mean considerably more by it than
I did thirty seconds ago.

"There's one more thing," I say, before she can turn all the way back
to the desk. "Money. I haven't worked out what I can actually offer
you, but I'm not going to pretend this is a favour that costs you
nothing and expect you to do it for the good of community broadcasting."

Nadia considers this properly, in the way she considers everything.

"A cut of whatever the renewal secures," she says, eventually. "Not a
number now. A percentage, agreed in writing, once you know what
there is to cut. I don't need it. I'd like it on paper anyway, because
things that stay unwritten have a way of becoming favours, and I've
done enough unpaid favours for one lifetime."

"Agreed. In writing. This week."

"Good." She turns back to the desk, picks the headphone cups back up,
one on, one off. "Six weeks. Thursdays. I'll want the format by
Tuesday."

"You'll want the—"

"I'm producing this," Nadia says, without turning around, in the tone
of a woman informing me of a fact rather than negotiating one. "You're
presenting it. I am not doing this badly, and you don't know how to do
it at all yet, so: Tuesday. Format. Go home."

I go home. I do not, for the first time in about six years, spend the
walk doing arithmetic about the roof.

I spend it thinking about four words about a cable, and what it might
take, over six weeks, to collect the rest of them.

I text Priya from the bridge, halfway home, because I know she'll
still be awake and because somebody has to be told. *She said yes.
Conditions attached. Format due Tuesday.*

The reply comes back in under a minute: *OF COURSE SHE SAID YES. I
TOLD YOU. Also I am going to need to know the conditions, this is now
my business too.*

*It's not your business.*

*I do the gossip AND the desk. It's structurally my business.*

I don't reply to that, mostly because she's right, and partly because
I'm smiling at my phone on a bridge in the rain at one in the morning
in a way that feels, for the first time in longer than I'd like to
admit, like something other than management.

·

I draft the written agreement the next morning, at the kitchen table,
before I've had any tea at all, because I want it done before the
version of me that overthinks everything gets a chance to complicate
it. It ends up being one page: a percentage, defined against whatever
the renewal secures, payable within thirty days of it being confirmed,
signed by both of us, witnessed by Priya, who takes the responsibility
with such gravity that she insists on using her best handwriting for
the date.

"This is very official," she says, examining the document with the
scrutiny of a woman twice her age. "You've even put a clause about
what happens if the renewal falls through entirely."

"I have."

"That's very you. Planning for the version where it all goes wrong
before you've even started the version where it goes right."

"That's called being prepared, Priya."

"That's called being frightened of hoping properly," Priya says, with
the specific bluntness only a seventeen-year-old can deploy without
it landing as cruelty, and hands the paper back before I can construct
a defence, and goes to put the kettle on, leaving me standing there
with a legally sound document and the uncomfortable sense that she's
just diagnosed something about me more accurately than any of the
document's actual clauses manage to.

·

Word gets round the volunteer network faster than either of us
intends, mostly because Priya cannot, structurally, keep a piece of
good news to herself for longer than the time it takes to text it, and
by Thursday lunchtime the corkboard by the kettle — the one Malcolm
put up his first year and nobody's ever taken down, papered thick with
listener postcards and a decade of scrawled thank-yous, which everyone
who's ever worked here simply calls the wall of fame — has acquired a
new addition: a torn-off strip of paper in Priya's handwriting reading
ROBYN + NADIA, COUPLES HOUR, WATCH THIS SPACE, pinned dead centre where
nobody could possibly miss it.

"Take it down," I tell her.

"I will not. It's morale."

"It's premature."

"It's *accurate*," Priya says, with the specific stubbornness of
someone who has already decided this argument is over. "You're doing
the show. The show is happening. The board records things that are
happening. I'm not putting anything on there that isn't true."

Sandra, one of our longest-running regulars — a woman who's rung in
faithfully every single Thursday for four years, mostly to complain
gently about the music selection — corners me by the kettle that
afternoon with the specific delight of someone who's been handed
gossip she didn't have to work for.

"Is it true," she says, "that you've finally got yourself a girlfriend,
after all this time worrying about everybody else's love life instead
of your own?"

"It's a radio segment, Sandra."

"It's about time is what it is," Sandra says, entirely unmoved by my
attempt at professional distance, patting my arm with the specific
firmness of a woman twice my age who has decided she's allowed to. "I've
watched you run yourself ragged for this place for years. Somebody
ought to be running you ragged back, for a change, and I don't mean the
roof."

I don't have a good answer for that either, and I'm starting to
suspect, standing by a kettle with a scrap of paper above my head
announcing a relationship that doesn't technically exist yet, that I'm
going to spend the next six weeks not having good answers for quite a
lot of things.

·

I draft the announcement for the station's own social accounts that
evening, alone in the office, and delete it four times before I settle
on something that doesn't oversell what we've actually got yet, which
turns out to be a harder needle to thread than any funding application
I've ever written.

*Big news from Kelvin Row*, the final version reads, *starting
Thursday — a brand new live segment, On the Level, hosted by two of our
own. Tune in and find out.* Deliberately vague. Deliberately short. I
have learned enough, in three weeks of dealing with a woman who
distrusts anything oversold, to know that the version of this
announcement that would have satisfied me a month ago — breathless,
over-adjectived, doing entirely too much work to convince people of a
feeling before they'd even had the chance to have it themselves — is
exactly the version Nadia would have vetoed on sight.

I show it to her before I post it, which I didn't have to do, and
which I do anyway, because I've started to understand that the
partnership only works if she gets to see things before the public
does, not after, regardless of whose account it's going out under.

She reads it twice. "It's fine," she says, which from her, I am
learning, is close to a rave review.

"You could sound more enthusiastic."

"I could," she agrees, "but then it wouldn't sound like me, and the
whole point of doing this properly is that it has to sound like both
of us, or the audience will clock the seam within a week."

I post it as written. It gets, by station standards, an enormous
response within the hour — shares, comments, Sandra typing an entire
paragraph of exclamation marks that somehow manages to convey more
genuine warmth than most properly punctuated sentences I've ever
received — and I sit there afterward, watching the notifications climb,
feeling the specific vertigo of having set something in motion that is
now, entirely, out of my hands.

·

Fiona rings that evening, having apparently seen the post already
through some mutual connection I've never fully understood the
mechanics of, and doesn't bother with hello.

"Nadia Quinn," she says. "The overnight one. That's who said yes."

"That's who said yes."

"Robyn." A pause, the specific pause of a sister recalibrating a
mental file in real time. "I don't think I've ever heard you say two
words about her in nine years. Not one. And now she's your fake
girlfriend, on the record, with a public announcement and everything."

"I know how it looks."

"I'm not saying how it looks. I'm saying it's interesting that the
person you picked, out of an entire building of volunteers, is the one
person you've apparently never once mentioned to me. That's not
nothing, Robyn. That's actually rather a lot, dressed up as nothing."

I don't have a ready answer for that, because I suspect she's
identified something before I've fully identified it myself, and Fiona,
sensing she's landed the point cleanly, doesn't push further, just
lets it sit there between us on the phone line, satisfied, the way she
generally is once she's said the true thing and doesn't need to grind
it in any further.

"Keep me posted," she says instead, gently, "on all of it. The show.
Her. Whichever parts you're ready to tell me," and rings off, leaving
me to sit with the specific unsettled feeling of having been seen more
clearly by my own sister, from several hundred miles away, than I've
quite managed to see myself.


## Chapter Four — On the Level

The kettle in the small meeting room takes four minutes, which I know
because I have now, over the course of one Tuesday afternoon, watched
it complete an entire cycle three times, and each time I have thought,
*surely this is not a load-bearing detail of my life*, and each time I
have been wrong.

We have a whiteboard. We have forty minutes before Priya needs the
room back for something she's calling "admin" and which I strongly
suspect is homework. We do not, forty minutes in, have a show.

"Two of Us," I say, writing it up, then immediately regretting the
handwriting, which slants downward in a way that makes it look like a
threat. "Or — The Chat. Or—"

"No," Nadia says, not looking up from the fader she's cleaning with a
cotton bud, an activity she appears to find more interesting than
either of my titles.

"You haven't heard the third one."

"I don't need to. You're doing the thing where you make it sound like
a segment on a breakfast show that got cancelled in 2011."

"That's oddly specific."

"I've engineered a lot of segments that got cancelled in 2011."

I write CALL IT SOMETHING on the whiteboard, mostly to give my hands
something to do, and try a different angle. "Okay. What do you call a
signal that's telling the truth?"

"On the level," Nadia says, immediately, still cleaning the fader,
clearly not registering that she has said anything at all. "That's
what the desk says when a signal's honest. On the level. No distortion,
no clipping, just—" She looks up, finally, and sees my face, and
stops. "What."

"That's the name."

"That's a technical term."

"That's the name," I say again, already writing it on the whiteboard
in letters that, this time, slant upward, because apparently my
handwriting has opinions about content. "On the Level. It's got
everything. It's got the pun. It's got the promise. It sounds like
something people would actually tell their pals to listen to."

Nadia looks at the whiteboard for a long moment, the specific stillness
of someone recalculating something they thought was already settled.

"I didn't mean it as a name," she says.

"I know. That's why it's a good one."

She doesn't argue with that, which from her, I am learning, functions
as a form of agreement. She goes back to the fader. I stand there
looking at four words on a whiteboard that used to be a phrase about
signal quality and is now, apparently, the name of my career-saving,
completely fictional relationship, and something about that — the
fact that the whole premise of the thing got built out of her
vocabulary and my desperation, in equal parts, without either of us
deciding it on purpose — makes me strangely, disproportionately happy.

We spend the rest of the forty minutes on the actual bones of the
thing, which turns out to be the part Nadia has genuinely strong
opinions about, once you get her past the naming. Thirty minutes,
she says, not sixty — a hard-out, no matter what's happening on air,
because a caller with a genuinely unsolvable problem will happily eat
your whole runtime if you let them and the show needs to feel finite
to feel safe. Three call slots, screened, never more, because a fourth
slot is where shows like this start performing crisis instead of
having conversations. And music beds under everything except the
actual advice, because — and she says this like it's obvious, the way
she says most things that turn out to be the smartest thing said in
the room — "silence makes people perform sincerity. A bed under it
lets them just be sincere."

I write all of it down. I am aware, writing it down, that I am taking
dictation from a woman I asked to be my fake girlfriend four days ago
and who has, in the intervening ninety-six hours, developed and
delivered an entire theory of live radio intimacy without once being
asked to.

"You've thought about this a lot," I say.

"I've engineered a lot of shows that got this wrong," she says, which
is, I am starting to understand, her version of *yes, extensively,
and it mattered to me*.

·

Save the cat, if you want to call it that, which nobody in this
building would, happens three nights later at three in the morning,
when a volunteer called Dev texts the group chat to say he's got food
poisoning and cannot, physically, be trusted near a live mic for the
next several hours.

I do not wake anyone up. I do not ask Priya, who is seventeen and
should, by any reasonable standard, be asleep. I get in the car and I
go and I sit down at a desk I have not properly operated in four years
and I am, immediately and comprehensively, terrible at it.

I hit the wrong cart. I ride a fader the wrong way and get a wall of
silence that runs eleven seconds too long, which on overnight radio
feels, I promise you, like an hour, like the station has quietly died
and nobody has told the two listeners currently awake to notice.

I want it understood that I used to be good at this. Before I was a
manager, before the roof and the spreadsheets and the licensing
panel, I was a presenter, properly, for six years, and I could ride a
desk in my sleep. It is a specific kind of humbling, at half past
three in the morning, to discover exactly how much of that skill has
quietly atrophied while I was busy being indispensable at everything
else instead.

Through the glass — because of course she's there, she's always there,
it's her actual shift and I am the one intruding on it — Nadia reaches
over from the next desk and fixes it without a word. Slides a level
across. Fires the next cart at exactly the right second. Doesn't look
at me like I'm an idiot, which I am, in this specific and narrow
professional context, being.

We sit together in the dead hour after, two mugs of tea gone properly
cold, and don't talk about the show at all, which — I realise, holding
the mug for warmth it no longer has — is the first time that's
happened since this whole thing started.

"You didn't have to do that," Nadia says, eventually, meaning the
shift, not the fix.

"I know," I say. "I don't know how to not."

She looks at me for a second in the particular way she has, head
tilted a few degrees off centre, listening to something that isn't
sound.

"I noticed," is all she says, and goes back to the desk, and I sit
there in the three a.m. quiet turning that word over — *noticed* — like
it's a fact I've been handed and haven't worked out yet what to do
with.

Priya finds out about it the next afternoon, from Dev, who is
mortified and apologetic and brings in a box of shortbread by way of
penance, which Priya accepts with the specific graciousness of a
seventeen-year-old who has already decided the real story here isn't
Dev's stomach.

"You covered a three a.m. shift," she says to me, in the tone of a
woman assembling a case, "instead of calling me, or Marcus, or
literally anyone else on the volunteer list, at an hour when you
could have called any of us and we'd have understood completely."

"It was three in the morning. I wasn't going to wake anyone up."

"You woke *yourself* up."

"That's different."

"It is not different," Priya says, with the weary patience of someone
explaining a very simple fact to someone who should, by rights, already
know it. "It's the same thing, except you did it to the one person in
this building who's allowed to be tired without anybody minding."

I don't have a good answer to that, so I eat a piece of Dev's
shortbread instead, and let her be right about it, which is, I am
starting to notice, becoming something of a pattern this month.

·

Dev, for what it's worth, recovers fully within two days and turns up
to his next shift with a second box of shortbread and a story about
the specific takeaway he now believes was responsible, delivered with
the wounded pride of a man who has never once in his volunteering
career let the station down and is finding the experience of having
done so, even accidentally, even for a stomach bug entirely outside
his control, close to unbearable.

"You've apologised four times," I tell him. "It's a stomach bug, Dev.
Nobody's angry."

"I know that. I'm angry on the station's behalf."

"The station isn't angry either."

"Well, it should be a *bit* angry," Dev says, with the specific
stubbornness of a man who would clearly rather be blamed for something
than let something go unblamed at all, and I leave him to it, because
some people process a small guilt best by being allowed to hold onto
it a while longer than is strictly necessary, and Dev, bless him, is
one of them.

I mention the whole thing to Nadia later, expecting a shrug. Instead
she says, "he's not wrong to feel bad about it, even if the reason's
daft. It's a good instinct to have around here. People who don't feel
bad about letting the desk down are usually the ones who end up
letting it down properly, eventually, when it actually matters."

I file that thought away too, along with everything else she's said
this week that turns out, on reflection, to be smarter than it sounded
at the time.

·

The format document itself, once Nadia's finished with it, is a
genuinely impressive piece of work — four pages, properly structured,
with a rundown template, a call-screening protocol adapted from
something she clearly learned on tour, and a section titled TONE that
reads, in full: *warm, not saccharine. Honest, not cruel. If in doubt,
underplay it — the audience will do the rest of the emotional work
themselves if you give them room to.*

I read that section three times before I fully absorb it, because it
is, without question, the single best piece of editorial guidance
anyone has given me in nine years of running this station, and it's
come from a woman who, four weeks ago, I'd have sworn had never once
thought about broadcasting philosophy in her life.

"Where did you learn to write like this," I ask her.

"I didn't learn to write like this. I learned to listen to a room and
work out what it needs, and then I wrote down what I noticed." She
shrugs, entirely unbothered by the compliment, in the way she's
unbothered by most compliments, treating them as data rather than
gifts. "It's the same skill as sound design, really. You're not
adding anything. You're just getting out of the way of what's already
true in the room."

I think about that sentence for a long time afterward — *getting out
of the way of what's already true* — and wonder, not for the first
time this month, how much of my own management style has been the
precise opposite: adding, managing, filling every silence with
something, rather than trusting a room, or a person, to hold its own
truth without my intervention.

·

Malcolm turns up on the Wednesday, unannounced, the way he always
does, using the key he was technically supposed to hand back eleven
years ago and that nobody's ever had the heart to ask for, walking
stick first down the basement stairs, moving slower than he used to
but with exactly the same look on his face when he clocks the state of
BIG LEAK's bucket.

"Still not fixed the roof, then," he says, by way of greeting.

"Working on it."

"You've been working on it since I handed you the keys." He lowers
himself into the good chair without asking, which Priya, for once,
allows without comment, out of what I can only assume is inherited
deference to a man who ran this place for eleven years on pure
stubbornness before she was born. "Heard you've promised the panel a
love story."

"Word travels fast."

"Word's always travelled fast in this building. It's the one piece of
broadcast equipment that's never once needed maintenance." He looks at
me properly, the way he used to when I was nineteen and certain I was
about to be told I'd done something wrong. "Is it real?"

"Define real."

"That's not an answer, Robyn, that's a dodge, and I taught you better
dodges than that." He nods toward the studio, where Nadia's shape is
visible through the glass, headphones half on. "Is she going to get
hurt by this?"

"I hope not. I've tried to make sure not."

"Trying's not nothing," Malcolm says, "but it's not the whole job
either." He's quiet for a moment, looking round the basement at
everything that's changed and everything that hasn't in eleven years —
the same cracked ceiling tiles, the same hand-painted sign upstairs,
the same amber bulb over the studio door that's never once been
replaced with a proper red one because, as he told me once, a long
time ago, *amber suits a place that never quite finished becoming
anything.*

"I told you once," he says, "don't let it become boring, and don't let
it close. I never once told you what to do about anything in between
those two instructions, because I didn't know either, and I still
don't. But I'll tell you this for free, since I'm here and you've got
tea on: whatever you're building with that woman in there, real or
otherwise, don't let it become the third thing on the list. Don't let
it become boring, don't let it close, and don't let it become a job.
You've only got the two rules to actually go by. Don't invent a third
one just because you're frightened of the second."

He leaves twenty minutes later, having drunk exactly half a cup of tea
and eaten three of Priya's biscuits without asking, the same as he's
done every visit for eleven years, and I stand in the basement for a
while after he's gone turning his instruction over, wondering how a
man who ran this place on pure stubbornness managed, in the space of
one visit, to say the truest thing anyone's said to me all week.

·

Priya, who has been listening to the whole exchange from the corridor
under the thin pretence of sorting cue cards, corners me the moment
Malcolm's gone.

"He likes her," she announces. "You should know that. Malcolm doesn't
ask questions like that about people he doesn't already like."

"He's met her twice."

"Malcolm decided he liked me within about four minutes of meeting me,
and I was fourteen and extremely rude to him about the tea rota," Priya
says, with the specific authority of someone citing established
precedent. "He doesn't need long. He just needs to watch someone do
one true thing, and he's made his mind up. I'd say bringing tea to the
overnight desk unasked probably counted, if word's got round to him
already, which, in this building, it obviously has."

"Word travels fast in this building."

"It's the one piece of broadcast equipment that never needs
maintenance," Priya says, echoing Malcolm's own line back at me with
such precise mimicry of his gravelly delivery that I laugh out loud for
the first time all week, properly, the kind of laugh that surprises
you on the way out.

"You've been rehearsing that impression."

"I've been rehearsing it for years," Priya admits, entirely unashamed.
"I do a very good Douglas too, if you ever want to hear it, though I
save that one for occasions that really call for it."

"When would that be?"

"Committee meetings, mostly," Priya says, "in my own head, since
nobody's ever going to let a seventeen-year-old into an actual
committee meeting to do it there," and goes back to the cue cards,
leaving me standing in the basement with the specific warm feeling of
a place that, whatever else happens with the panel, has never once, in
nine years, run short of people who care about it properly.


## Chapter Five — The Backstory

Building a shared history that doesn't exist turns out to be one part
comedy and three parts logistics.

We agree on the true parts first, because Nadia insists on it, on the
grounds that a lie built on no true parts at all is a lie with nothing
to hold it up. So: we met at the station. That part's real, if you're
generous about the definition of *met*, which mostly consisted of me
saying good morning to a closed studio door for two years. Eight
months, backdated, because Priya assigns us an anniversary off the
volunteer rota like she's doing us a favour, which, structurally, she
is.

"What do we argue about," I ask, marker in hand, the whiteboard now a
genuine crime scene of crossed-out titles and half a diagram of a fake
relationship.

"The radio," Nadia says, without hesitating. "Safely. Because it's
real, so we'll never get caught out on a detail, and because everyone
who's ever worked in this building will believe two people who work
here argue about scheduling."

"That's very practical."

"I'm a very practical person."

We rehearse an argument about whose turn it is to do the washing-up —
a washing-up that does not exist, in a flat neither of us shares —
and I am, four lines in, surprised at how good she is at lying with
specificity. She invents a dishwasher that's been broken since March.
She invents a brand of fairy liquid she claims to hate. She does not,
however, know how to make small talk to save her life, and the two
things combined — devastatingly precise fiction, absolutely no ability
to ask how my weekend was — is so funny that I have to put the marker
down.

"What," she says.

"Nothing. You're just — you'd be a brilliant witness and a terrible
first date."

"I know," Nadia says, entirely unbothered, and goes back to the
dishwasher bit.

We're four minutes into the washing-up argument — properly into it now,
raised voices, me doing an exaggerated *you never asked me* while she
does something with her eyebrows that could win awards — when it turns
real for about four seconds. I say something about never being asked
what I want, meaning the bit, meaning the fictional dishwasher, and my
own voice does something underneath the joke that I don't authorise
and can't immediately take back.

Nadia notices. Of course she notices; noticing things is the entire
occupation. She doesn't say anything about it. She just lets the beat
sit there a half-second too long before delivering her next line, and
we both know, without either of us naming it, that something slipped
through that wasn't scripted.

I write it all on an index card afterward, because I am the kind of
person who needs an index card. Nadia reads it back once, flatly,
hitting every beat of the joke exactly right, which should not be as
unsettling as it is.

We add other things to the index card over the course of the
afternoon, the way you do when you're building a person out of spare
parts: a favourite film neither of us has actually seen (a French
thing Nadia claims to love specifically because nobody ever asks a
follow-up question about French things), a story about how I supposedly
talked her into applying for the overnight desk in the first place
(entirely fabricated; she applied because the ad was the only one that
didn't require her to be on camera), and a running joke about the
station's ancient, temperamental kettle that we agree to seed into
actual on-air banter because, Nadia points out, a joke a couple
actually has is a joke that survives contact with a live caller, and a
joke invented purely for the segment generally doesn't.

"You're better at this than you should be," I tell her, somewhere
around the fourth index card.

"I used to write show notes for a fringe theatre company," she says.
"You learn to build a person out of a paragraph. It's the same skill."

I don't ask which company, or when, or why *used to*, because I've
already worked out, four days into this, that the fastest way to get
Nadia to close a door is to knock on it before she's said I can.

·

Kirsty comes by on the Friday, "to see the format in person," which
means the panel sent her, which means somebody upstairs wants a report
that isn't just Douglas's euphemisms.

She stands in the foyer in a blazer that costs more than my monthly
transmitter bill and does the thing she's always done, which is be
warm in a way that is somehow also a closed door. "Robyn," she says,
in the tone you'd use for someone you used to know quite well and now
mostly know from a distance, which — I suppose — is exactly what I am
to her.

Eight years since we did breakfast together. Eight years since she
took the offer from national radio and I stayed, because somebody had
to, because the station doesn't run itself and I have never once, out
loud, said that I minded her leaving. I still haven't. I'm not about to
start in a foyer with a lanyard watching.

We were good together, back then, in a way I've never quite managed to
recreate with anyone since. She did the news and the banter; I did the
listeners, the phone-ins, the small human stuff that made people
actually ring in rather than just tune out. We used to finish each
other's links without trying to. When she left, it took me the better
part of a year to stop leaving a half-second gap in the schedule where
her voice was supposed to go.

She never once suggested I come with her. I've turned that fact over
more times than I'd like to admit, in the years since, trying to work
out whether it meant she didn't think I'd want to, or didn't think
I'd get in, or simply never thought of me at all once the bigger job
was in front of her. I have never asked. I'm not about to start now,
in a foyer, with a lanyard watching.

"It's looking good," Kirsty says, meaning the format, meaning the
folder she's presumably read, meaning nothing at all about the two of
us specifically. "Very — real. That's what the panel wants. Real."

"That's the plan," I say.

She glances past me, once, at Nadia, at the desk, headphones half on,
entirely uninterested in being introduced, and Kirsty doesn't ask who
she is, which I notice, and which I file away without knowing yet what
to do with it.

"Good to see you, Robyn," Kirsty says, and goes, leaving behind the
specific, professionally warm nothing she's always left behind, and
I stand in the foyer for a second longer than I need to before I go
back to the desk.

Nadia doesn't say anything about the exchange. Afterward, I notice
that she doesn't ask, and I don't offer, and something about the two
of us not asking each other things yet feels, for now, like the safest
arrangement available.

Priya, on the other hand, has opinions, delivered the moment Kirsty's
out the door and down the close stairs.

"That," she announces, "was extremely tense, and I would like to know
why, and I want to note that I asked Nadia the same question and she
just said *history* and went back to the fader, which I found
extremely unsatisfying as an answer."

"It's not a story, Priya."

"Everything's a story. That's the whole basis of my job here." She
folds her arms, settling in, the specific posture of a teenager who
has correctly identified that an adult is deflecting and has decided
to enjoy watching them do it. "You went very quiet. You're never quiet.
I've catalogued your entire range of behaviours over four years and
*quiet* isn't in it."

"We used to work together. A long time ago. It didn't end badly, it
just — ended."

"Did you want it to end?"

"I didn't get a vote," I say, which is truer than I meant to let it be,
and Priya, mercifully, for once in her life, decides not to push
further, and goes back to her spreadsheet, leaving me with the
specific relief of having said something honest to someone who chose,
this once, not to make me say more of it than I was ready for.

·

I spend that evening, after everyone's gone home, doing the thing I
haven't let myself do properly in eight years, which is pull the old
recordings.

The station keeps an archive going back further than it probably
should, on a hard drive Malcolm bought secondhand sometime in the
nineties and that nobody has ever quite dared to replace, for fear of
losing what's on it. I find a breakfast show from a Tuesday in
October, eight years and one month ago, and I sit in the dark office
with the amber bulb glowing through the wall and listen to two people
who sound, if I'm honest, considerably younger than I remember either
of us being.

We're good. I'd half convinced myself, over the years, that I'd
built the whole thing up in memory, made it better than it actually
was because loss tends to do that to things. We weren't. We were
genuinely, unreproducibly good together, in the specific way that only
happens when two people trust each other's timing completely, and
listening to it now, alone, in an empty basement, I let myself feel the
loss of that properly for the first time since it happened, rather
than filing it, as I have filed everything else that hurts, under
things there simply isn't time to deal with.

I don't play the whole show. I don't need to. I've got the shape of it
back now, and that's enough, for tonight, to be getting on with.

·

Fiona's down from Aberdeen that weekend for a wedding two streets
over, and turns up at my door afterward with a bottle of wine and the
specific look of a woman who has heard, from somewhere, that Kirsty
Lennox is now sitting on the licensing panel, and intends to extract
every detail of that fact before she leaves.

"You didn't tell me she was back," Fiona says, pouring two glasses
without asking whether I want one, which is, at this point, simply how
my sister operates.

"She's not back. She's on a panel. It's not the same thing."

"It's exactly the same thing, and you know it, because you've gone the
specific shade of quiet you go whenever her name comes up, which is a
shade I have been watching you go since we were nineteen and she
turned up at that first breakfast audition looking like she owned the
building."

"I don't go a shade."

"You go a *very specific* shade," Fiona says, entirely unbothered by
my objection, curling up on the other end of the sofa with the
practised comfort of someone who has spent thirty-odd years making
herself at home in whichever flat I happen to currently occupy. "I
watched you not go to London with her. I watched you spend the better
part of a year afterward leaving gaps in conversations exactly the
length of wherever her voice used to go. And now she's sitting across
a table from you in a blazer, judging your funding application, and
you're telling me it's not the same thing."

"It was eight years ago."

"Eight years isn't as long as you think it is," Fiona says, gentler
now, turning her glass by the stem. "I still think about the flat we
grew up in like I could walk back into it tomorrow. Eight years is
nothing, when it's a person, rather than a place."

I don't have an answer to that, and Fiona, mercifully, doesn't push
for one, just tops up my glass and changes the subject to something
easier — her nephew's disastrous attempt at a school play, a work
colleague's ongoing feud over the office thermostat — the ordinary
furniture of two sisters spending a Saturday evening together, and I
let myself be grateful, for a while, that at least one relationship in
my life doesn't currently require a written agreement to function.

·

Fiona stays the night, in the end, on the sofa she's claimed as hers on
every visit for the better part of a decade, and over breakfast the
next morning she brings up Kirsty again, more gently this time, having
apparently spent the intervening hours turning over whatever she
half-said the evening before.

"I don't want you to think I was having a go at you," she says, over
toast neither of us has properly buttered. "About Kirsty. I know it's
not simple."

"It's not simple."

"I just think," Fiona says, carefully, "that you've spent eight years
treating it like a closed file, and closed files still have weight,
even shut. You carry them exactly the same as open ones. Sometimes
more, actually, because you don't let yourself go back and check what's
actually in them."

"What would you have me do? Reopen it? She's on the panel now. There's
a professional line."

"I'm not saying reopen anything," Fiona says. "I'm saying stop
pretending the file's empty. It's not empty. You built a whole career
you never fully wanted around the shape of what happened with her,
without ever once saying so out loud, not even to me, and I'm your
sister, and I had to work most of it out from the outside."

I don't have a ready answer for that, and Fiona, mercifully, doesn't
push further, just butters her toast properly this time and changes
the subject to her own life, her own ordinary furniture of children
and thermostats and a husband who apparently cannot be trusted near a
barbecue, and I let the conversation move on, grateful, while quietly
filing away the phrase *a whole career you never fully wanted* to turn
over again later, alone, once she's back on the train to Aberdeen and
I no longer have to manage my own face while I think about it.

·

Fiona catches the train back on the Sunday, and I walk her to the
station rather than letting her get a taxi, mostly because I'm not
ready for the visit to end quite yet, and we stand on the platform for
a few minutes making the specific small talk two people make when
neither of them wants to be the one to say the actual goodbye first.

"Ring me after the first broadcast," she says again, the same
instruction from the phone call days before, "properly this time, not
just a text. I want to hear your actual voice tell me how it went,
not read three sentences about it."

"I will."

"And Robyn." She pauses, hand on the train door, in the specific way
she does when she's about to say something she's been holding back
the whole visit. "Whatever happens with Kirsty, with the panel, with
any of it — I meant what I said about the closed file. You don't have
to reopen it. But you don't have to keep pretending it's empty either,
not with me, not with yourself. That's all I'm asking."

"I hear you."

"Good," Fiona says, and hugs me, quickly, fiercely, the way she has
since we were children, and gets on the train, and I stand on the
platform until it's pulled fully out of the station, watching it go,
turning her sentence over the whole walk home in a way I suspect I'll
still be turning it over long after this particular six weeks has come
to whatever end it's heading toward.


## Chapter Six — Four Words About a Cable

She tells me, unprompted, on the Friday before the first broadcast, at
gone midnight, in the specific voice she uses when she's decided
something has to be said and is going to say it exactly once.

"Before this goes any further," Nadia says, "there's a reason I don't
do my voice in public, and I'm not going to explain it. I need you to
not need me to."

I have been running a station for six years on the theory that every
problem can be solved if you get enough information about it. It is,
I will admit, not always a theory that serves me well. But something
about the way she says it — not defensive, not bracing for an
argument, just stating a fact about the shape of her the way she'd
state a fact about a fader — makes the correct response arrive in me
fully formed, for once, without a detour through six other responses
first.

"Okay," I say.

That's it. That's the whole of what I say. I do not ask what the
reason is. I do not ask if it's serious, or recent, or something I
should know for the show's sake, or any of the forty questions that
are, at this exact moment, queuing up behind my teeth like traffic.

Nadia looks at me for a second like she's checking the sentence for a
trap door and not finding one.

"Okay," she says back, and it costs her something, visibly, to have
said even the four words she's said, and she changes the subject to a
fader that's been sticking since March, and I let her, because I am,
apparently, capable of this after all. I just didn't know it until
someone needed me to be.

I think about it on the walk home more than I probably should. Not the
mystery of it — I am, against every instinct I have cultivated over
six years of running a building on information and spreadsheets,
genuinely uninterested in solving it — but the fact of her having said
anything at all. Eleven words in three years, four of them about a
cable, and now this: a whole sentence about herself, offered
unprompted, at midnight, to a woman she's known properly for exactly
five days. I don't know what to do with the honour of that except try
very hard not to drop it.

·

The final rehearsal happens on the Wednesday, a full run-through with
Priya in the producer's seat and an empty studio standing in for
whatever audience Thursday actually brings.

It goes badly, in the specific way things go badly when everybody
involved is too aware of the stakes to relax into them properly. I
fluff a link twice. Nadia, normally unshakeable, mistimes a cue by a
full three seconds, which for her is practically a public breakdown.
Priya, watching the two of us fail to be even remotely convincing as
either presenters or a couple, eventually stops the run entirely and
puts both hands flat on the desk.

"Right," she says. "Stop. Both of you. You're doing the thing where
you try so hard to get it right that you forget how to just be two
people in a room."

"That's rather the whole problem, Priya. We're not two people in a
room. We're pretending to be a couple for a funding panel."

"No," Priya says, with the specific patience of someone repeating
something she considers self-evident, "you're two people who *like*
each other, badly hiding it behind a format, and the second you stop
trying to perform the liking and just let the actual liking show,
you'll both be fine. You don't need to act. You need to stop acting."

Neither of us has an answer to that. We run the segment again, without
trying quite so hard, and it comes out considerably better — not
perfect, not smooth, but recognisably itself, two people who like each
other doing an imperfect job of proving it on the record.

"Better," Priya announces, with the satisfaction of a director who
has just solved a problem two supposed adults couldn't solve
themselves. "Do that tomorrow. Just that."

·

The night before the first broadcast, we end up on the fire escape,
because the small meeting room has Priya's admin in it and the
overnight studio has three days of unattended cables in it, and the
fire escape has rain, moving up the Clyde in visible sheets, and
nothing else.

"Can I ask you something," I say, "off the record. Not the show's
record. Mine."

"Go on."

"If the segment wants a kiss. At the end of the hour, for the cameras
— well, for the microphones, there aren't any cameras, but you know
what I mean — is that on the table."

I say it plainly, because I have learned, in three weeks, that plainly
is the only register she trusts, and because the whole reason I'm
asking off mic, the night before, standing in the rain, is that there
is a version of this job — the fake-relationship, engagement-metric,
audience-identifiable version — that would use tonight's silence as
permission and never actually ask, and I would rather lose the show
than be the kind of person who does that to her.

Nadia thinks about it properly. I've learned to recognise the specific
stillness that means she's actually weighing something, rather than
just being quiet, and I wait through it without filling the silence,
which is, for me, its own small victory.

"Yes," she says, eventually. "On the table. Scripted. On air. At the
end of the hour, and nowhere else, and if it ever stops feeling like
that, I'll say so."

"Understood."

She looks out at the rain for a second. "You asked."

"I asked."

"Most people wouldn't have."

I don't say anything to that, because I don't entirely trust my voice
right at this moment, and because there's something else I need to
say and I want to get it right the first time.

"I need to say a thing too," I say. "And I need you to hear it as the
actual foundation this whole thing sits on, not as a nice sentence I'm
saying to make you feel better about tomorrow."

"Say it."

"What's real off that mic stays off the record," I say. "Every time.
I promise you that."

The rain does its thing on the corrugated roof above us. Nadia looks
at me for a long moment — longer than the kiss question got, longer
than anything's got so far — and something moves behind her face that
I don't have a name for yet.

"That's a strange promise to need," she says.

"Yeah," I say. "I know."

Neither of us says anything else about it. But I feel it settle into
place between us the way a fact settles once it's been said out loud —
solid, load-bearing, the kind of thing you find out later you've been
leaning your whole weight on.

We stay on the fire escape a while longer than either of us needs to,
watching the rain do its thing over the rooftops toward the river,
neither of us in any particular hurry to go back inside and be two
professionals preparing a segment instead of whatever we currently
are, standing in the wet, having just built something between us with
words instead of a contract.

"Nervous," Nadia says, eventually, not quite a question.

"Terrified," I say. "You?"

"I engineer live radio for a living. Nerves are a Tuesday." A beat.
"This is different nerves."

"How can you tell the difference?"

"The Tuesday kind, I know what I'm frightened of," she says. "This
kind, I don't. That's the tell."

I don't have an answer for that, so I don't try to give her one. We
just stand there, in the rain, letting the not-knowing be its own
kind of company, until the cold finally drives us both back in.

Priya's left a note on the whiteboard when we come back through — GOOD
LUCK TOMORROW, DON'T MESS IT UP, in her small square handwriting, with
a drawing of a microphone that is, generously, more confident than
accurate — and Nadia looks at it for a long moment before saying,
quietly, "she's put a lot of faith in something that's mostly held
together with a cue sheet and a promise."

"She's seventeen. Faith's cheap at seventeen."

"It shouldn't be cheap anywhere," Nadia says, and wipes a smear of rain
off her sleeve, and heads for the door, and I follow her out into a
Glasgow night that has, for once, nothing further to say about any of
it.

·

I don't sleep at all that night, in the end, though not for the
reasons I'd expected.

I lie awake running through the format one more time, not because it
needs it — Nadia's built it properly, better than anything I'd have
managed alone — but because running through things is the only
mechanism I have for managing a feeling too large to sit still with.
Three call slots. Thirty minutes. Music beds under the advice, silence
kept for the moments that have earned it. A kiss at fifty-four minutes,
consented to on a fire escape, in the rain, by a woman who has said
roughly eleven words to me across three years and has just, in the
space of one evening, said several hundred more, unprompted, about
things that plainly cost her to say.

I think about the phrase she used — *this is different nerves* — and
turn it over until the sky starts going grey at the edges of my
curtains, and get up, eventually, having slept perhaps forty minutes in
total, to go and do the single most nerve-inducing thing I have ever
volunteered myself for on a Thursday evening in September.

·

Priya's already at the station when I arrive, hours before she needs
to be, and she's added something to the wall of fame overnight: a
fresh corner cleared, deliberately, with a single pin waiting in it and
a note underneath in her small square handwriting that reads FOR
TONIGHT'S MESSAGES — LEAVE ROOM.

"You didn't have to do that," I say.

"I know I didn't. I wanted to." She's rearranging cue cards that don't
need rearranging, the specific busywork of someone managing her own
nerves by managing something smaller than the actual thing. "Sandra's
already texted to say she's got the whole street listening tonight.
Grant from the allotments says he's recording it, which I've told him
he's not allowed to do, and he's said he's doing it anyway, which is
very on brand for Grant."

"No pressure, then."

"Enormous pressure," Priya says, entirely cheerfully. "The good kind.
The kind where people actually care whether you turn up." She stops
rearranging the cards and looks at me properly, for a second, in a way
that's older than her seventeen years usually let her look. "You're
going to be fine, you know. Both of you. I've watched you build this
whole thing out of nothing in three weeks, and I don't think either of
you understands yet how much of it wasn't nothing at all."

I don't ask her to explain that, because I suspect I already know what
she means, and because there isn't time before the first callers start
ringing in to warm up the lines, and because some sentences are better
left exactly as generously vague as she's left this one, to be turned
over properly later, once the first broadcast is behind us and I've
got the room to actually think about what she's just said.

·

Nadia arrives at six, exactly eleven minutes before her usual mark
even though tonight isn't, technically, her shift at all, and finds me
already at the desk, rundown in hand, reading the same paragraph for
what must be the fifth time without absorbing a word of it.

"You've read that line eleven times," she says, not a question.

"Have I?"

"You moved your lips the same way each time. I counted." She takes the
rundown gently out of my hands and sets it face-down on the desk. "You
know this already. You wrote half of it. Reading it again isn't going
to make you know it any better. It's just going to make you more
frightened of it."

"That's uncomfortably accurate."

"I'm an engineer," Nadia says. "Accuracy's the whole job." She looks at
me properly, in the specific way she has, head tilted a few degrees off
centre. "For what it's worth — and I don't say this kind of thing
often, so take it as the rare event it is — I think we're going to be
alright tonight. Not perfect. Alright. That's usually enough."

"Since when are you the reassuring one? I thought that was meant to be
my job."

"Since about ten minutes ago, apparently," Nadia says, entirely
unbothered by the reversal, "because somebody has to be, and you've
clearly used up your whole supply of it on yourself already this
evening, badly, on a rundown that doesn't need reading an twelfth
time."

I laugh, properly, the tension breaking for a moment, and she almost
smiles back — the real one, the one around the eyes — before Priya
calls through that the lines are open and there's no more time left
for either of us to be frightened about anything except, now, simply
doing the thing.

·

I catch sight of the wall of fame on my way to the booth, still bare
except for Priya's cleared corner and the single waiting pin, and think,
briefly, about how strange it is that a corkboard by a kettle has
become, this week, something I check the way other people check a
horoscope — not for prediction, just for reassurance that the ordinary
furniture of the place is still exactly where it should be.

Malcolm's voice arrives in my head, unbidden, the way it does at moments
like this one: *don't let it become boring, and don't let it close.*
I've never once, in nine years, understood quite how much weight those
eleven words could carry until tonight, standing outside a studio door
about to broadcast a relationship that started as a lie and has become,
somewhere in the last three weeks, the truest thing in my life.

Nadia's talkback crackles once, testing. "Thirty seconds," she says,
flat, certain, the same voice she'll use for every cue tonight and every
Thursday after this one, and I take my seat, and settle my headphones,
and wait for the red light that is, in this particular basement, never
actually red at all, only ever the same patient, unfixed amber it's
always been.


## Chapter Seven — Sound Check

An hour to air, and Priya is running the desk like a small furious
adult who has finally, after four years of being handed jobs nobody
else wanted, been handed the one she actually wants.

"Rundown," she says, sliding a page across without looking up from her
headset. "Three pre-screened callers. Two stings. One planned—" she
glances at it, glances at me, glances away again, in the specific
manner of a seventeen-year-old declining to editorialise about her
employer's fake love life "—beat, at fifty-four minutes."

"Thank you, Priya."

"I didn't write the beat in. I found it already written in when I
opened the doc. I want that on the record."

I catch my own reflection in a dead monitor on the way to the booth —
a version of my face I do not recognise, nervous in a way that has
nothing, structurally, to do with a fake segment — and don't have time
to do anything with that information before Nadia's voice comes
through the talkback, flat and certain.

"Levels are good," she says. "So are you."

She's gone back to the fader before I can do anything at all with that
sentence, which is, I am coming to understand, precisely her method:
say the true thing once, cleanly, and get out before either of us has
to look directly at it.

·

On the Level goes out live for the first time at seven o'clock on a
Thursday, and it is, against every reasonable expectation I had walked
in with, good.

Not good like a fake thing performing competently. Good like an actual
show.

The first caller is a man called Grant from Maryhill whose partner
keeps rearranging the cutlery drawer without warning him, which sounds
like nothing until Grant starts talking and it becomes clear the
cutlery drawer is standing in, very obviously, for something much
larger about feeling consulted. Nadia takes the call, not me — she
leans into the second mic like she's done it a hundred times, which
as far as anyone at Kelvin Row knows she never has — and asks him,
flatly, "does she tell you why, or does she just do it," and the whole
studio goes quiet in the specific way a room goes quiet when somebody
has, by accident, asked exactly the right question.

Grant says she just does it.

"Ask her why once," Nadia says, "properly, not as an accusation. If
she can't tell you, that's the actual problem and it isn't the
drawer."

I have to look down at the rundown to stop myself staring at her.

The second caller is a woman called Debs who wants to know whether
it's normal to still fancy your partner of eleven years so much it's
distracting at work, which I field myself, badly, laughing so hard on
air that Priya has to cut to a sting early — an actual real laugh that
nearly costs us a full five seconds of dead air and somehow, judging
by the messages that come in afterward, makes the segment better
rather than worse. Debs, it turns out, is delighted rather than
offended, and texts in afterward to say so, which becomes, without
either of us planning it, the first entry in what Priya will later
start calling *the wall of fame* — a corkboard by the kettle, covered
in printed-out messages from listeners who liked something enough to
say so twice.

The third caller asks about anniversaries, and Nadia answers with four
words that land better than anything I'd scripted, and I catch myself,
mid-link, thinking: *this is not what I expected fake to feel like.*

At fifty-four minutes, on cue, we do the thing we agreed on the fire
escape.

It's scripted. It's consented to, off mic, the night before, in the
rain, in words. It's supposed to last about a second and a half,
because that's what the cue calls for, a neat, broadcastable, funny
little button on the hour.

It runs longer than that. Not dramatically — nobody listening would
clock it, I don't think, though I'll wonder about that for days — but
long enough that neither of us corrects it in the room, long enough
that when we come apart there's a half-second where neither of us says
anything at all, on air or off, which for two people whose entire
professional lives are built around never letting dead air sit is its
own kind of confession.

We finish the segment. We do the outro. We are, professionally, fine.

Priya, in the booth, has gone entirely still, headset around her
neck, watching the two of us through the glass with an expression I
can't fully read from where I'm standing — somewhere between
delighted and slightly alarmed, the specific look of a seventeen-year-
old who has just watched two adults do something considerably more
significant than either of them appears to have noticed themselves
doing.

She doesn't say anything over the talkback. She just presses the
button for the closing sting, and lets the segment end properly, and
saves whatever she's thinking for the corridor, which turns out to be
the correct professional call, and also, I'd guess, the kindest one.

·

Backstage — if you can call a corridor with three buckets in it
backstage — we talk very fast about everything except the thing that
just happened.

"The second caller's story really landed," I say.

"The stings were tight," Nadia says.

"Priya's timing on the cut was—"

"Excellent," Nadia agrees, and does the outro voice one more time,
entirely unnecessarily, as a joke that is also, very obviously, a way
of not saying anything else, and I let her, because I am doing the
exact same thing from the other side of the same silence.

Priya watches the two of us do this — watches us talk very seriously
about sting placement for a full ninety seconds without once
mentioning the actual final ninety seconds of the broadcast — and
says nothing at all, which from a seventeen-year-old who has said
plenty about everything else this week is, itself, a form of
commentary I choose not to examine too closely tonight.

Neither of us says the word *kiss* out loud, not once, the entire
evening. I go home and lie in the dark running it over anyway, on
repeat, the extra half-second of it, looking for the seam where the
performance ended and whatever came after started, and not finding one,
which is exactly the problem.

Priya texts the group chat at half eleven — a chat that currently
contains me, Priya, and Nadia, and which Priya has, without any
consultation whatsoever, renamed KELVIN ROW: OPERATION COUPLE — with a
single message: *that was so good I forgot to be embarrassed for both
of you.* Nadia doesn't reply. I don't either, though I look at it
four separate times before I put the phone face-down on the nightstand
and make myself, unconvincingly, go to sleep.

·

I run the whole broadcast again in my head the next morning, on the
walk in, the way you replay a good exam once it's safely behind you,
checking each answer for the mistake you're sure must be hiding
somewhere in it.

I can't find one. That's what unsettles me most, if I'm honest. Six
weeks ago I couldn't have told you what Nadia Quinn's voice sounded
like doing anything other than reading back a cue in the flattest
register available. Now I can replay, at will, the specific warmth she
put into four words about anniversaries, a warmth that wasn't in the
script and wasn't, as far as I could tell, performed for anyone's
benefit but the caller's.

I mention none of this to her when I arrive. She's at the desk, one
cup on and one off, running the overnight logs with her usual
unhurried thoroughness, and when she says good morning it sounds
exactly like every other good morning she's ever given me, which I
find, this particular Friday, almost unbearably reassuring and almost
unbearably disappointing in roughly equal measure.

"Numbers are good," she says, without looking up, sliding a printed
sheet across the desk. "Better than good."

I look at the sheet. She's right. She's always right. I am beginning
to suspect this is simply going to be a permanent feature of my life
from here on, and finding, to my own considerable surprise, that I
don't mind it in the slightest.

·

Douglas rings that same Friday, which nobody in the building
particularly wants, and asks, in the specific tone of a man reading
from a checklist he didn't write and doesn't fully understand, whether
he might "pop down and observe the format in situ" ahead of the next
funding cycle.

"He wants to watch us on air," Priya says, relaying the message with
the specific relish of someone delivering bad news she personally
finds entertaining. "Like we're a nature documentary."

"When."

"Next Thursday. He's bringing a clipboard. I know this because he
said the word *clipboard* out loud on the phone, unprompted, as though
it were a credential."

Nadia takes the news with her usual flatness, though I notice, for the
first time, something that might be the faintest trace of unease
behind it — gone again almost before I've registered it, filed away
under the growing list of things about her I'm learning to notice
rather than ask about outright.

"Does it change anything," she asks, "having him in the room?"

"It shouldn't."

"That's not an answer to whether it does."

I think about it properly, because she's asked properly, and because
by now I know better than to give her a managed version of a true
thing. "It changes how much I'll be performing rather than just doing
the job. Which is exactly the problem Priya keeps telling us not to
have."

"Then don't perform it," Nadia says, simply, as though the solution
were always this obvious and I'd simply been too busy dreading the
visit to see it. "Do the show. Let him watch the show happen. If the
show's good, it doesn't need help pretending to be good in front of
him specifically."

I hold onto that all week, the way I hold onto most of the things she
says that sound, on first hearing, like they ought to be simpler than
they turn out to be in practice — because Thursday arrives, and
Douglas does bring the clipboard, exactly as threatened, and sits in
the corner of the booth in a coat he does not remove for the entire
hour, writing things down at intervals that correspond to absolutely
nothing happening on air, in a way that makes Priya, watching him
through the glass, mouth the word *why* at me twice without sound.

We do the show anyway. We don't perform it for him. By the end of the
hour he's put the clipboard away entirely and is simply listening,
coat still on, in the specific stillness of a man who came in expecting
to tick boxes and has, instead, without quite meaning to, been drawn
into an actual broadcast.

"That was," he says afterward, in the foyer, visibly hunting for the
correct bureaucratic register and, for once, not quite finding it,
"rather good, actually. I'll say so in my notes."

"Thank you, Douglas."

"Don't let it go to your head," he adds, recovering some of his usual
stiffness on the way out the door, and I don't tell him that it isn't
his approval I'm interested in holding onto, though I suspect, from
the look Nadia gives me through the glass, that she's worked that out
already without needing to be told.

·

Priya debriefs the whole visit afterward with the specific relish of a
seventeen-year-old who has just watched an adult fail to be
intimidating and wants the moment properly documented for posterity.

"He wrote down 'clipboard' as a word," she reports, "and then, ten
minutes later, wrote down nothing at all for the rest of the hour,
which I am officially logging as a win, possibly the biggest win this
building has had against Douglas specifically in the whole four years
I've worked here."

"It's not a competition, Priya."

"Everything's a competition. That's the whole basis of local
government." She's already updating her spreadsheet with a new column,
unprompted, labelled DOUGLAS VISITS: WON. "I'm tracking it from now on.
I want a proper record for whenever the next renewal cycle comes round
and somebody claims we've never had positive engagement from a panel
member in person."

"You're remarkably invested in beating a man who's, structurally,
meant to be on our side."

"He's meant to be neutral," Priya corrects, with the specific precision
of someone who has clearly looked up the actual terms of reference for
the panel at some point out of pure spite. "Neutral isn't the same as
on our side. I intend to move him from neutral to actually on our side
by the end of the renewal cycle, and today's clipboard incident is
step one."

I don't ask what steps two through however-many are, mostly because I
suspect I'd find the answer both alarming and, in Priya's hands,
entirely likely to work.

·

Nadia and I stay late that same evening, going back over the broadcast
properly this time, without an audience or a corridor's worth of
adrenaline to rush us past the harder parts of the conversation.

"I want to ask you something," I say, "and I want you to know you
don't have to answer it if it's too much."

"Go on."

"That extra half-second. At fifty-four minutes. Was that you, or was
that both of us, or is that a question you don't actually have an
answer to either?"

Nadia considers it properly, the way she considers everything, turning
a fader idly without adjusting anything. "I don't have a clean answer,"
she says, eventually. "I know I didn't correct it. I know I could have.
I know that not correcting something is its own kind of decision, even
when it doesn't feel like one in the moment."

"That's not nothing."

"No," she agrees. "It's not nothing." She looks at me properly. "I
think we should probably talk about what it means. Not tonight. I'm not
ready tonight. But soon, before it becomes the kind of thing neither of
us can talk about because we've let too much silence build up around
it first."

"Soon," I agree, and it's enough, for tonight, to have named the thing
without yet having to solve it, the two of us sitting together in a
basement that smells of damp and old cable, letting a shared silence do
the work that neither of us is quite ready to hand to words yet.

·

I ring Fiona on the walk home, mostly to have somewhere to put the
particular restless energy of an evening that's ended in more questions
than answers.

"You sound unsettled," she says.

"I am, a bit. Good unsettled, I think. The kind where you've said a
true thing out loud and now you have to actually live with having said
it, rather than getting to take it back."

"That's usually the good kind," Fiona agrees. "The bad kind is when you
regret saying it. You don't sound like you regret it."

"I don't."

"Then you're fine," she says, with the specific certainty she reserves
for questions she considers already settled. "Go home. Sleep on it.
Whatever needs deciding will still need deciding tomorrow, and you'll
be better rested for it," and rings off, leaving me to finish the walk
home turning the evening over, more settled than I was five minutes
ago, exactly the way a good conversation with my sister generally
leaves me.


## Chapter Eight — The Numbers Come In

The email arrives at nine the next morning, from the panel's admin
address, not from Kirsty, which I notice and file away without knowing
what to do with it yet.

*Encouraging early engagement metrics*, it says, in the specific
bloodless prose of a person whose entire job is not committing to
anything in writing. In panel language, I am learning, that sentence
is roughly the temperature of a hug.

Overnight listenership is up — for a Thursday slot that used to run a
repeat of the shipping forecast to an audience of, as far as I've ever
been able to establish, one insomniac fisherman in Greenock. Priya has
already built a spreadsheet, unprompted, with the metric broken out by
quarter-hour, and there's a small, private satisfaction in watching a
seventeen-year-old take an institution's own language and turn it into
a weapon on our behalf.

I allow myself, for about ninety seconds, standing at the kettle
waiting for its own four-minute cycle, to believe this might actually
work — on every level I built it to, and on one I did not budget for
at all, and have no intention, this morning, of examining any further.

Priya finds me at the kettle and reads the email over my shoulder
without asking, which is a habit of hers I have long since given up
trying to correct.

"Encouraging," she reads aloud, in a voice that manages to make the
word sound like an accusation. "That's it? A whole show, live, unpaid
overtime for both of you, and the panel's response is *encouraging*?"

"It's better than what they usually say."

"What do they usually say?"

"Nothing. Usually there's just a longer silence before the next
review cycle."

Priya considers this. "So encouraging is good."

"Encouraging is very good."

"This building has extremely low standards for good," she says, not
unkindly, and goes to update her spreadsheet, and I stand at the
kettle a moment longer, watching the little orange light on it that
means it is, at long last, nearly done boiling, and think that she is
not wrong, and that I have spent six years making my peace with
exactly that fact.

·

I don't sleep properly that night, which is not, strictly, new — I
run a station on a leaking roof, sleep and I have an understanding —
but it's new in kind. I lie there reverse-engineering one extra
half-second of a scripted kiss the way I'd reverse-engineer a discrepancy
in the roof fund, running it for meaning that arithmetic was never
built to find.

I draft three texts to her about it. *Hey, so, about last night* — no.
*Not that it matters but* — worse. *Did you notice* — I delete that one
so fast I nearly drop the phone.

I send none of them. I lie in a flat I have arranged, very
deliberately, over several years, to be a place where being entirely
alone is comfortable — good light, one very good armchair, nothing on
the walls that requires explaining to a guest, because there are so
rarely guests to explain anything to — and find that tonight it is not
quite managing its usual trick, and I fall asleep still running the
sum, and get, as ever, no answer that holds up in daylight.

I wake at half six with the specific dread of a person who knows,
somewhere below conscious thought, that they are going to have to walk
into a basement in four hours and be normal at someone. I am not,
generally, someone who dreads seeing people. I have built an entire
professional identity around being extremely findable, extremely
available, always the one who picks up. This is new, and I don't like
it, and I get up and make tea I don't drink and go into work early
anyway, because the alternative is lying there letting the dread win,
and I have never once in my life let a feeling win a fight it hadn't
earned.

It turns out, walking down Dumbarton Road in the grey half-light,
that I needn't have dreaded it at all, because Nadia treats the
morning after exactly the way she treats every other morning: level,
unremarkable, precisely calibrated. She's at the desk when I arrive,
one cup on and one off, running through the overnight logs with the
specific unhurried thoroughness of someone who has never once in her
life let a broadcast go out without checking it twice, and she says
good morning to me the same way she's said it for three years,
without a flicker of anything I could point to and call a change.

I don't know, at the time, whether that's relief or its opposite. I
spend most of the walk home that evening deciding it's relief. I spend
most of the following morning deciding it might be the opposite. By
the time I've worked out which one it actually is, an entire week has
gone by and the question has, without my noticing, stopped mattering
nearly as much as I thought it would.

The wall of fame gets its second entry that Friday — a message from a
listener called Sandra who says she's been widowed three years and
that hearing two people argue gently about a cutlery drawer, live, at
seven o'clock on a Thursday, was the first time in a long while the
radio felt like company rather than noise. Priya reads it out loud in
the kitchen, and none of us says anything for a moment afterward,
because there isn't really anything to say to a sentence like that
except to go and make sure the next Thursday earns it.

·

I meet Sandra properly a few weeks later, entirely by accident, in the
queue at the good bakery on Byres Road, and only work out who she is
when she mentions, to the woman behind her, that she listens to "the
couple on Kelvin Row," in a tone that makes it sound like we're
practically celebrities, which we are, I suppose, to an audience of a
few hundred people who tune in on a Thursday specifically for us.

"You're the manager," she says, once she's clocked me properly, going
slightly pink. "Robyn. I recognise your voice."

"That's me."

"I hope you don't mind me saying — the show's got me through some
quite bad evenings this autumn. My husband used to fall asleep in the
chair by half seven most nights, near the end, and the flat's very
quiet without that particular kind of company. Your show fills it up
a bit."

I don't have a rehearsed response to that, which is, by now, becoming
something of a theme in my life. I just tell her, honestly, that it
means more to me than she probably realises, and mean it, and watch
her go off with her bread looking, I'd like to think, a fraction
lighter than she came in. It occurs to me, walking home, that this —
not the panel, not the renewal, not even Nadia, though certainly also
Nadia — is actually the whole point of the building I've spent nine
years keeping the lights on in.

·

I tell Nadia about Sandra that evening, expecting a small nod of
acknowledgement and nothing more, and instead she goes quiet for a
long moment, the specific quiet that means she's turning something
over properly.

"That's the thing nobody tells you about doing this job well," she
says, eventually. "You don't get to see most of it land. You put a
segment out into the dark and you hope it finds somebody, and
occasionally, if you're lucky, you meet them at a bakery and find out
it did. But most of the time you never know. You just keep doing the
work and trust that it's finding somebody, somewhere, even when you
can't see it."

"Is that hard? Not knowing?"

"It used to be," Nadia says. "I think I've made my peace with it,
mostly, by deciding that the not-knowing is actually the point. If I
needed to see the effect every time, I'd be doing it for the wrong
reason." She looks at me properly. "You met one Sandra today. There
are probably forty more out there we'll never meet, and that has to
be alright, or none of this is sustainable."

I think about that for the rest of the evening — about work you do
without ever seeing where it lands, and what it takes to keep doing it
anyway, faithfully, week after week, on the strength of forty
Sandras you'll never actually meet.

·

Kirsty comes down to the station in person that same week, rather than
sending an email or a text with a smiling emoji doing more work than
it should, and the difference in register is not lost on me — nor, I
suspect, on her.

"I wanted to see the numbers properly," she says, standing in the
foyer in a coat instead of her panel blazer, which I notice
immediately, the way I noticed the coat instead of the blazer once
before, at a coffee shop, eight years' worth of context attached to
the smallest sartorial choice. "Not the summary Douglas'll circulate.
The actual shape of it."

I show her Priya's spreadsheet, which has, by now, four tabs and a
colour-coding system Priya refuses to explain to anyone outside the
building. Kirsty studies it for longer than the numbers strictly
warrant, and I understand, watching her, that she isn't really reading
the data. She's using it as a reason to stand in this basement a
little longer than a five-minute professional visit requires.

"You built something real here," she says, eventually, not looking up
from the spreadsheet. "I mean that as a genuine observation, not panel
language. I sat through your first meeting fully expecting a fake
segment to look like a fake segment. It doesn't. Whatever's actually
happening on that mic, it's not what I expected to find when I took
this job."

"What did you expect to find?"

"Honestly? A station running on fumes and a manager too proud to say
so." She looks up properly now. "I've watched a lot of applicants over
the last year say the right things in the right order because they've
learned what the panel wants to hear. You've never once, in four
years, said the right thing in the right order. You just say the true
thing, badly timed, and somehow it keeps working anyway."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's an observation," Kirsty says, which I recognise, with some
amusement, as almost exactly Nadia's own preferred register, delivered
by a woman who couldn't be more different from her in every other
respect. "Take it as whichever one helps."

She leaves without asking to meet Nadia properly, which I notice
again, and don't yet know what to do with, filing it alongside the
other small facts I'm collecting about a woman who left this building
eight years ago and has, apparently, never quite stopped watching it
from wherever she landed.

·

I mention the visit to Nadia afterward, expecting the usual flat
non-reaction. Instead she's quiet for a moment, turning a fader idly.

"She still cares what happens to this place," Nadia says. "You know
that, don't you. Whatever else is going on with her. That wasn't a
panel visit. That was someone checking on something she still thinks
of as partly hers."

"I hadn't thought of it that way."

"You don't, generally, think of things that way until someone else
says them out loud," Nadia says, without any particular edge to it,
just an observation, the kind she's started making more often lately
about me specifically, and I let it sit rather than defending myself
against it, because by now I've learned that's usually the correct
response to a true thing said plainly.

·

I ring Fiona that evening to tell her about the Kirsty visit, mostly
because I've noticed, this month, that not telling her things has
started to feel like its own small dishonesty, a habit I'm trying,
slowly, to unlearn.

"She came down herself? Not an email?"

"In person. In a coat, not the blazer."

There's a pause on the line, the specific weighing pause Fiona does
when she's deciding how much to say. "That's not a professional visit,
Robyn. You know that, don't you. People don't drive across town in
their own coat to check a spreadsheet that could've been emailed."

"I know."

"Does it bother you? Nadia, I mean. Knowing Kirsty's still — whatever
she is. Orbiting."

I think about the question properly before I answer it, because it
deserves more than a reflexive no. "I don't think it does. Nadia
noticed before I said anything, and she wasn't bothered either, or if
she was, she didn't let it look like it. I think we've both got old
files we're carrying. Hers isn't mine to open. Mine isn't hers to
manage. We're just letting them both exist without either of us
needing the other one to solve it."

"That's remarkably mature," Fiona says, "for two people who started
this whole thing as a lie to a funding panel."

"I'm aware of the irony."

"Good," Fiona says. "Hold onto the awareness. It'll serve you well,"
and rings off with her usual efficiency, leaving me to sit with the
observation a while longer, turning it over the way I turn over most
of the things she says that turn out, on reflection, to be more useful
than they sounded in the moment.

·

Grant from Maryhill, our cutlery-drawer caller, rings in again a few
weeks later, unprompted, just to give an update nobody asked for, which
turns out to be exactly the kind of listener behaviour Priya's second
spreadsheet tab was built to celebrate.

"Took your engineer's advice," he tells me, live, delighted with
himself. "Asked her why, properly, like you said. Turns out she
rearranges the drawer when she's stressed about something else
entirely and doesn't want to talk about the something else. The drawer
was never the actual problem. It was just the only door she knew how
to knock on."

"How'd that conversation go?"

"Better than I expected. Worse than I hoped. Which I'm told," Grant
says, with the specific pride of a man repeating wisdom he's clearly
been turning over for weeks, "is usually about right, for the
conversations that actually matter."

I catch Nadia's eye through the glass while he's talking, and she
raises an eyebrow slightly — not quite a smile, but close enough that
I recognise it as one — because *the conversations that actually
matter usually land better than expected and worse than hoped* is,
almost word for word, something she said to a different caller weeks
ago, now travelling back to us secondhand, refracted through a
stranger's marriage, proof of exactly the kind of thing she once told
me you never get to see land, except this once, against the odds, we
did.

·

I tell Fiona about Grant's call that evening, mostly because it's the
kind of small, ordinary good news that doesn't survive being kept to
myself for very long these days.

"That's rather lovely," Fiona says. "A whole marriage, quietly
improved, because of a radio segment about a cutlery drawer."

"It wasn't really about the cutlery drawer."

"It never is, is it," Fiona says, with the specific warmth of someone
who's watched me build an entire career out of exactly that
principle. "I think that's the actual thing you've always been good
at, underneath the roofs and the rotas. Finding the real question
hiding inside the small one, and answering that instead."

I don't have a ready response to that, so I let her be right about it,
the way I'm slowly learning to let the people who know me best simply
be right, without needing to deflect it into something smaller first.


## Chapter Nine — Levels

I bring tea down to the overnight desk on the Tuesday, unasked, which
I have never once done for anyone in this building I wasn't
technically managing at the time.

It isn't a large gesture. I want to be accurate about the scale of it,
because in the retelling it's the kind of thing that gets inflated —
Priya, later, will tell this story as though I'd walked down there
with a violin section. It's a mug of tea, milk, no sugar, because I've
watched her drink it that way for three years without once asking if
that was actually how she took it, which turns out, happily, to have
been a correct guess rather than an intrusive one.

Nadia looks at the mug, then at me, in the manner of a woman
recalculating a fact she thought was settled.

"You don't have to do that," she says.

"I know."

"I didn't ask you to."

"I know that too." I put the mug down on the one clear patch of desk
that isn't covered in cue sheets or the specific brand of energy drink
she pretends not to have a preference for. "It's tea. It's not a
grand gesture. Please don't make it into one."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were about to say something about it being unnecessary."

"I was," Nadia admits, "and now I'm not going to, because you've
pre-empted it, which is its own kind of irritating."

I sit on the spare stool — there's always a spare stool at the
overnight desk, for reasons nobody's ever explained to me, possibly
just institutional memory of a producer role that hasn't existed since
before I started here — and watch her work for a minute, the way I've
started doing more evenings than I'd admit to anyone directly.

"For the record," she says, eventually, not looking up from the desk,
"that ran fifty-five seconds over a fifty-four-second cue."

I know exactly what she means, and she knows I know, and there is a
whole conversation compressed into that one flat sentence about a
kiss that happened four days ago that neither of us has mentioned
since.

"For the record," I say back, and put the mug down properly this
time, and don't say anything else, because I have learned, in four
weeks, that with her the silence after a true sentence is the part
that actually counts. Filling it would be, I am starting to
understand, its own kind of lie.

She goes back to the fader. I stay a minute longer than I strictly
need to, ostensibly checking a rundown that does not, in fact,
require checking, mostly because leaving now would mean admitting that
I came down here for more than the tea and I'm not ready to admit
that, not even to myself, not with the words fully formed.

Somewhere in that minute, a level she's riding on the desk holds a
beat longer than the meter strictly needs — a half-second where her
hand could have moved and simply doesn't — and she looks up at exactly
the same moment I do, and for a second neither of us is doing anything
that could be described, in any professional sense, as work.

Neither of us says one further word about it. But something between
us has moved — I'd swear to it in any register she'd accept — and both
of us know it moved, flat-footed and entirely unwilling, yet, to call
it by its name. I finish the rundown check I didn't need to do, and
say goodnight, and walk home in the specific quiet that comes after a
thing has shifted and neither party has said so out loud, which is, I
am coming to learn, the exact temperature at which this entire
relationship seems determined to run.

Priya clocks the spare stool the following week — notices, with the
specific forensic attention of a teenager who has appointed herself
the building's unofficial intelligence service, that it has moved
six inches closer to the main desk than its official position and has
stayed there for four consecutive nights.

"I'm not going to say anything," she announces, apropos of nothing, in
the kitchen, holding a mug she has not yet filled with anything.

"Good."

"I'm just going to note, for the record, that the stool has moved."

"Noted."

"And that it keeps not moving back."

"Priya."

"I'm not saying anything," she says again, with enormous satisfaction,
and fills her mug, and leaves me standing there wondering exactly how
much of what I think is private is actually, in this building, a
matter of public record.

·

I try, that same week, to have an honest conversation with myself
about what exactly I think I'm doing, and manage about four minutes
of it before I give up and go and check on the roof instead.

Four minutes is enough, though, to establish the basic shape of the
problem. I am supposed to be running a fake relationship for the
benefit of a licensing panel. I have, somewhere in the last two weeks,
started bringing a woman tea she didn't ask for, noticing which stool
she prefers, and lying awake reverse-engineering the exact temperature
of a look that lasted less than a second. None of that is in the
format. None of that is, strictly, my job. And yet I find, standing in
the corridor with BIG LEAK dripping steadily into its bucket, that I
have absolutely no interest in stopping any of it, which is either the
best or the worst piece of information I've gathered about myself all
year, and I genuinely can't yet tell which.

·

I ring my sister that evening, mostly out of the specific unease that
comes from having noticed something about yourself you don't quite
know what to do with. Fiona — the real one, my actual sister, not the
one on my midnight candidate list and not the caller from broadcast
three either, a coincidence of names I've stopped finding funny by now
— lives in Aberdeen with two children and a husband who does something
with oil rigs that neither of us has ever fully understood, and rings
me back within the hour, which is unusual for her, and immediately
suspicious.

"You never ring on a Tuesday," she says. "What's happened."

"Nothing's happened."

"Robyn."

"I think I might be developing feelings," I say, in the flattest
voice I can manage, "for the woman I'm pretending to date for a
funding panel."

There's a pause on the line long enough that I can hear her muting
the television.

"That's the best thing you've said to me in about four years," Fiona
says, delighted, entirely unhelpful. "Tell me everything."

I don't tell her everything. I tell her enough — the format, the fire
escape, the tea, the stool — and she laughs at exactly the right
points and goes quiet at exactly the right points too, and by the end
of the call I feel, if not resolved, at least considerably less alone
with the specific unease of the thing, which turns out to be most of
what I actually needed.

"Can I say one thing before you go," Fiona says, "as your older
sister, with all the authority that comes with three whole years of
seniority?"

"Go on."

"You've spent your whole adult life being the reliable one. The one
who stays, who covers, who doesn't ask for anything because asking
means someone might say no. I've watched you do it since you were
nineteen, and I've never once said anything, because it seemed to be
working for you, in its own grim way." A pause, the specific one that
means she's about to say something she's been saving up. "This is the
first time in years you've rung me sounding like you actually wanted
something for yourself, rather than reporting on how well you were
managing everyone else's. I just wanted to say I noticed, and I'm
glad, and you don't have to manage this one, Robyn. You're allowed to
just want it."

I don't have a response to that which doesn't risk my voice giving out
entirely, so I just say, "thank you," and mean it more than the two
words can hold, and we say goodnight, and I sit for a while afterward
in a flat that feels, for the first time in longer than I can
remember, less like a place I've arranged to survive being alone in
and more like somewhere I might actually want to be found.

·

Priya's having a rough week at school, it turns out, though it takes
me three days of noticing she's shorter-tempered than usual before I
actually ask about it rather than simply working around it.

"Highers," she says, when I finally ask, in the specific clipped tone
of someone who would rather not be discussing this at all. "Everyone's
decided this is the year that determines the rest of my life, which is
an enormous amount of pressure to put on a set of exams I'm sitting in
a building that smells permanently of the chip fryer next door."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It is exhausting. Mum's doing nights at the QE, so I'm on my own for
the actual revising, and school keeps sending letters home about
*application strategy* that Mum doesn't have time to read before her
shift, so I end up reading them myself and pretending to her that I've
got it handled, because she's got enough on without also worrying about
university admissions timetables."

I don't say anything straight away, because I recognise, uncomfortably,
a version of my own nineteen-year-old self in that sentence — a
teenager quietly managing the adult-shaped gaps in her own life so
efficiently that nobody around her ever has to notice there were gaps
at all.

"You don't have to handle it alone," I say, eventually. "I mean that
practically, not just kindly. I can read a UCAS form. I've read
considerably worse paperwork for this station over the years."

Priya looks at me for a long moment, the specific wary look of someone
who isn't used to being offered help without having to ask for it
first, loudly, several times.

"You'd actually do that?"

"I'd actually do that."

"Okay," she says, in a smaller voice than her usual one, and pulls a
crumpled letter out of her bag that has clearly been read and reread
and folded back up several times without ever quite being dealt with,
and slides it across the desk toward me, and we spend the next forty
minutes going through it properly, line by line, until the whole thing
looks considerably less like a mountain and considerably more like a
form, which is, in the end, all it ever actually was.

"Thanks," Priya says, at the end of it, not looking up from the now
much less crumpled letter. "For not making a whole thing of it."

"I'm not going to make a whole thing of it. I'm just going to keep
asking, from now on, when you're having a rough week, instead of
waiting for you to tell me. That's not a whole thing. That's just
paying attention properly, which I should have been doing already."

She doesn't say anything else about it, but she leaves the letter on
the desk rather than folding it back into her bag, which I understand,
without either of us naming it, to be its own kind of thank you.

·

Nadia catches the tail end of the UCAS conversation from the overnight
desk, close enough to have heard most of it, and doesn't say anything
until Priya's gone home for the night.

"You're good with her," she says, simply, cleaning down the desk for
the evening.

"I try to be."

"It's not trying. I've watched people try to be good with teenagers.
It usually looks like performing patience at them. What you just did
was actually listen to what she needed and give her exactly that,
without making it about how generous you were being for offering."

"She reminds me of myself, a bit. At that age."

"I know," Nadia says. "I've noticed. I think it's why you're good at
it — you're not guessing what a seventeen-year-old in that situation
needs, you're remembering." She pauses, considering something. "Were
you close with anyone, at her age? An adult who did for you what
you're trying to do for her?"

I think about the question properly before answering, because it's a
fair one and deserves more than a reflexive deflection.

"Malcolm, a bit," I say, eventually. "Not in the way you mean — he
wasn't warm, exactly, he's never been warm. But he never once treated
me like I was too young for the job, which turned out to matter more
than warmth would have. Nobody else really stepped in. My mum was
doing her best with what she had, which wasn't much spare capacity by
the time I was Priya's age. I mostly worked it out myself, which I
suppose is exactly why I don't want Priya to have to."

"That's not a small thing you're doing for her."

"It doesn't feel like much. It's just paying attention."

"That's the whole of what most people never get," Nadia says, "so I
wouldn't undersell it," and goes back to the desk, and I let the
compliment sit rather than deflecting it, which is, this month,
becoming something of a small personal project of its own.

·

Priya finishes the UCAS form properly a few days later, and brings it
to me for a final look before she submits it, which I understand,
watching her hover in the office doorway rather than simply handing it
over, to be its own small act of trust rather than a mere formality.

"It's good," I tell her, reading it through properly. "Better than
good, actually. Your personal statement's the best piece of writing
I've seen out of this building in years, and I include several grant
applications I've personally laboured over in that assessment."

"You're just saying that."

"I'm not just saying that. I don't just say things, Priya, you've told
me so yourself, repeatedly, usually while criticising my handwriting."

She almost smiles at that, ducking her head to hide it, and takes the
form back with visibly more confidence than she brought it in with.

"I'm putting media studies first choice," she says, "but I've put a
business course second, in case anyone official ever asks me to prove
I've thought about *contingencies*." She says the word with the exact
weary distaste Kirsty reserves for panel jargon, and I laugh, properly,
recognising immediately where she's picked up the inflection.

"You sound like Kirsty."

"I sound like someone who's spent four years listening to every adult
in this building complain about bureaucratic language," Priya
corrects. "I'm allowed to have opinions about contingencies. I've
earned them."

She submits the form that evening, from the good chair, with the
specific ceremony of someone marking a genuine occasion, and texts the
group chat a single word — *DONE* — that gets more replies, faster,
than almost anything else posted there all autumn.

·

Nadia buys her a small bar of chocolate the next day, unprompted,
leaving it on the desk with no note, which Priya finds and holds up,
baffled.

"Is this from you?"

"It's from whoever left it there," Nadia says, entirely unbothered,
not looking up from the fader, and Priya, recognising immediately that
this is as much acknowledgement as she's going to get, eats the
chocolate at the desk with the specific quiet satisfaction of someone
who understands, without needing it spelled out further, exactly what
kind of currency this building actually trades in.

"You're both terrible at expressing things directly," she informs us,
"and I find it extremely charming, which I want on the record as a
begrudging compliment rather than an invitation to keep doing it."

"Noted," I say, and Nadia, for her part, says nothing at all, simply
goes back to the desk with the specific satisfied stillness of a woman
who has said everything she needed to say in the form of one small bar
of chocolate, left without ceremony, exactly where it would be found.


## Chapter Ten — The Brief for Broadcast Two

We're prepping broadcast two off a caller's letter that's actually
tricky — a couple arguing about a shared bank account, one of them
wanting to keep saving separately, the other reading that as a door
half-shut — and for the first time neither of us has to fake having an
opinion about it.

"You're wrong about the joke on page two," Nadia says, not looking up
from the rundown.

"I am not."

"It's a good joke. It's wrong for the caller. She's not going to want
to laugh at the account thing, she's going to want someone to say the
account thing is reasonable and the door thing is also reasonable and
that they're not the same argument." She crosses it out with the
station biro, the one on the string. "Cut it."

I cut it. She's right, which is becoming a genuinely inconvenient
pattern, one I've started privately cataloguing: Nadia's right about
the joke on page two. Nadia's right about which caller to screen out.
Nadia was right, three weeks ago, about the thermostat, the one thing
in this building I'd been prepared to argue about to the death, and
she settled it in one sentence by pointing out that the radiator in
the corridor runs off the same circuit and nobody had ever checked.

"You could have told me that a year ago," I say, apropos of the
thermostat, apropos of nothing, apropos of the general pattern.

"You never asked."

"I've been managing this building for six years. I don't get to ask
the engineer basic questions about wiring, I'm meant to already know."

Nadia looks at me properly for the first time all afternoon. "That's
possibly the most Robyn sentence you've said to me yet," she says,
"and I want you to notice that I mean it as a diagnosis, not a
compliment."

Somewhere in the second hour of prep, a bit forms that neither of us
builds on purpose: Nadia mutters the actual, technically correct answer
into the talkback under her breath, and I repeat it on air two minutes
later as though it were my own wisdom, and it works, every single
time, and by the third instance we're both laughing about it in a way
that has stopped feeling like homework and started feeling like
something closer to a language — one with its own grammar, its own
private jokes, built entirely out of six weeks of forced proximity and
neither of us noticing, until now, quite how fluent we've become in
it.

·

Second broadcast, kept mercifully brief on the page, because there are
only so many times you can describe a red bulb and a level meter
before the reader starts wondering if anything else happens in this
basement: the account caller gets a real, useful answer — Nadia's,
mostly, delivered in my voice — and there's no stunt beat this week,
deliberately. We agreed, off mic, that a kiss-of-the-week format would
be worse for everyone, us very much included.

There is one moment, though, that I want on the record properly,
because it's the first time a caller has made either of us go quiet
rather than clever. A man rings in — older, careful with his words,
the kind of caller who's clearly rehearsed the call three times on his
own landing before dialling — and asks, simply, whether it's normal to
still be a little frightened of your own wife after thirty years of
marriage, not frightened of her exactly, frightened of getting it
wrong, of the account, the door, the whole architecture of a life you
built together starting to show a crack you can't fix on your own.

Nadia answers that one. Not with a level, not with a timing. She says,
"It's not about being frightened of her. It's about being frightened
that you'll stop being someone worth staying with. Those are
different fears, and only one of them is actually about your wife."

There's a silence on the line, and then the caller says, quietly,
"aye, that's it exactly," and hangs up before either of us can say
anything else, and Priya has to fade the next sting in eleven seconds
early because none of us, for a moment, trusts our own voice.

A small crowd of regulars has started phoning in just to hear the two
of us argue about the studio thermostat, which nobody scripted and
nobody can explain, and Priya's spreadsheet gets a second tab, titled,
with the specific bluntness of a seventeen-year-old who has stopped
pretending she doesn't have opinions about our fictional relationship,
REGULARS WHO ARE ACTUALLY HERE FOR THEM.

I ask her, once, whether she thinks the audience can tell the
difference — between the segment and whatever's actually happening
underneath it. Priya looks at me the way you look at someone who has
asked a question they already know the answer to and are hoping,
against the evidence, that you'll disagree with them.

"Robyn," she says, "I don't think there's a difference left to tell."

I don't have an answer for that, so I don't try to give her one, and
go back to the rundown, and don't think about it again for at least
several hours, which — for a woman who thinks about everything, at
length, usually at four in the morning — counts, this week, as
enormous restraint.

·

Priya's own life, I am slowly starting to learn, is considerably more
complicated than the version of her that runs a desk with such
frightening competence four nights a week would suggest.

She lives with her mum in a flat off Maryhill Road, one of three
children, the only one still at home, and does two Highers alongside
the station work, which is more than most adults I know manage to
balance without something giving. Her mum works nights at the Queen
Elizabeth, which is, Priya mentioned once, in passing, entirely without
self-pity, the actual reason she ended up at Kelvin Row in the first
place — an empty flat on a Tuesday evening and a radio advert for
volunteers that happened to be on the one time she was listening.

"I like it here better than at school," she told me once, months ago,
apropos of nothing, stacking cue sheets. "At school I'm seventeen. Here
I'm just good at something. Nobody checks my age before they let me
run a desk. They just check whether the desk's been run properly."

I think about that sentence more than I probably should, given
everything else currently occupying my attention. There's a version
of this whole strange autumn where Priya is the actual heart of it —
not the fake couple, not the panel, not the roof, but a seventeen-year-
old who found the one room in the city where being good at something
mattered more than being a particular age, and who has, without ever
being asked to, made it her whole personality.

I don't tell her any of this. She would, correctly, find it
mortifying. I just make sure, more carefully than I used to, that the
rota never runs her past what a seventeen-year-old should reasonably
be asked to do, even when she insists, loudly and often, that she's
fine.

·

Nadia clocks the pattern before I've fully admitted it to myself —
that I've started, without deciding to, protecting Priya the way
nobody protected me at that age, filling in the gaps I fell through
myself at nineteen with nobody standing close enough to catch me.

"You know you can't fix your own nineteen through her, don't you," she
says, one evening, gently, in the specific tone she reserves for
observations she's fairly sure will land badly and has decided to make
anyway because she thinks they matter more than the discomfort of
saying them.

"I'm not doing that."

"You're doing a version of that. It's not a bad version. She's lucky
to have it. I just want you to notice you're doing it, because the
version of care that comes from an old wound and the version that
comes from actually looking at the person in front of you aren't
always the same thing, even when they look identical from the
outside."

I sit with that for longer than I'd like to admit. She's right, of
course — she generally is — but there's something in the noticing
itself that feels, for once, less like an accusation and more like
being handed a tool I didn't know I needed. I start trying, after
that, to ask Priya what *she* actually wants from this place, rather
than simply assuming I already know, having once been seventeen and
unasked myself.

I ask her properly, one quiet evening, sitting on the good chair while
she runs the desk from the spare stool, a reversal of the usual
arrangement that she clocks immediately and finds, I think, more
significant than I'd intended it to be.

"What do you actually want out of this place," I say. "Not what you
think I want to hear. The real answer."

Priya doesn't answer straight away, which is unusual for her, and I
let the silence run rather than filling it, the way I've been slowly
learning to.

"I want to run something," she says, eventually. "Not this exact
thing, necessarily. But something like it. Something where nobody
checks whether I'm old enough before they let me be good at it." She
turns a fader idly, not really adjusting anything, just needing
somewhere to put her hands while she says something true. "Everyone at
school talks about university like it's the whole of what happens
next. Nobody talks about the version where you just get very good at
something real, early, and build outward from there."

"You could do both."

"I could. I probably will, if I'm honest — Mum would never forgive me
if I didn't at least try for it properly. But I wanted you to know
that this isn't a stopgap for me. It's not something I'm doing until
my real life starts. This is my real life. I'd like that taken
seriously, rather than treated like something charming a teenager does
on Saturdays."

"It's taken seriously," I say. "It has been for a while. I don't
think I've said that to you properly, though, so: it is. You're not
a volunteer who happens to be good. You're one of the best producers
this station's ever had, and I include several decades of paid adults
in that assessment."

Priya doesn't say anything for a moment, and when she does, her voice
has lost some of its usual armour. "Nobody's ever said that to me
using the word *producer* before. Everyone says *helper.* Or *the
young one.*"

"You're not the young one here. You're Priya, and you run a desk
better than most people twice your age, and I should have told you
that a long time before this week made me actually stop and look at
you properly."

She goes back to the fader, visibly working to compose herself, and I
let her have the moment without commentary, because some things, once
said, don't need to be said again to be believed — they just need
time to actually land.

·

I catch Nadia, later that same evening, looking at an envelope on the
side table by the overnight desk that I've never seen before and don't
recognise — heavy paper, a return address I can't read from where I'm
standing, postmarked somewhere that isn't Glasgow.

She sees me clock it and moves it, unhurriedly, into her bag, without
comment, in the specific unhurried way that is somehow louder than if
she'd snatched it away.

"You don't have to tell me what that is," I say.

"I know I don't."

"I'm not asking."

"I know you're not." She zips the bag, finally, and looks at me
properly. "It's from a theatre company. The touring one. They want an
answer about something, and I haven't given them one, and I'm not
ready to talk about why, and I'd like you to let that be enough for
now, the way I let the London thing be enough for you, without
either of us actually explaining it to the other."

I go very still at that, because I hadn't told her about London — not
directly, not in so many words — and yet here she is, naming it
plainly, in a tone that makes it clear she's known for a while and
has simply been waiting, the way she waits for most things, for me to
either bring it to her myself or not.

"How long have you known?"

"Since the reminder went off on your phone in the kitchen and you
turned it face-down so fast you nearly knocked over your tea," Nadia
says. "I'm an engineer, Robyn. I notice things people try to hide.
It's the whole job."

"I should have told you."

"You should have," she agrees, without any particular heat in it,
"but I understand why you didn't, because I'm currently doing the
exact same thing to you with an envelope, so I'm not in a strong
position to be angry about it." A pause. "Same rule as the podcast.
I'm not owed your London decision on my timeline. You're not owed my
envelope on yours. We tell each other when we're ready, and until
then, we don't pretend the not-telling isn't happening. That's the
part that actually matters — not the secret itself, just being honest
about the fact that there's one."

I think about that distinction for a long time afterward — the
difference between a secret kept and a secret denied — and find,
turning it over on the walk home, that it's possibly the most useful
piece of relationship architecture anyone's ever handed me, real or
fake, and that I intend to hold onto it considerably longer than the
six weeks currently on the contract.

·

I tell Fiona about the envelope rule that same week, in broad strokes
rather than specifics, mostly because I want to say it out loud to
someone and hear whether it sounds as wise repeated back as it did in
the moment, or whether it was simply Nadia's particular gift for
sounding wise about anything she says plainly enough.

"That's actually rather good," Fiona says, after a pause, sounding
faintly begrudging about it, the specific tone of an older sister
conceding a point she'd rather have made herself. "I wish someone had
told me that fifteen years ago. Your brother-in-law and I spent the
first two years of our marriage assuming secrets and honesty were
opposites, when actually the opposite of honesty is just pretending
there's nothing there at all."

"You have secrets from each other?"

"Everyone has secrets from each other, Robyn. The healthy version
isn't no secrets. It's not pretending the gaps don't exist." A pause.
"I still haven't told him about the money I lost on that terrible
investment his cousin talked me into, for instance. He knows there's
something. He's chosen not to push. I'll tell him eventually, when I'm
ready, and he'll be annoyed, briefly, and then he'll get over it,
because the not-telling was never actually about him."

"That's a very specific example to have ready."

"I've had a while to think about it," Fiona says, dryly, "same as you
apparently have now, with your envelope woman and her mysterious
theatre contract. Sounds like you've found someone who's already
worked out something it took me a decade of marriage to learn. Hold
onto her."

"I intend to," I say, and mean it more than the three words let on,
and Fiona, for once, doesn't push for anything further, just lets the
sentence sit there between us, satisfied, apparently, that she's said
what needed saying.

·

I think about the envelope rule for days afterward, turning it over at
odd moments — waiting for the kettle, walking down Dumbarton Road,
lying awake doing the arithmetic I've stopped being able to help doing
about anything that actually matters to me. It occurs to me, somewhere
around the third day of turning it over, that I've spent six years
running this station on a version of the opposite principle: manage
every gap before anyone notices it's there, close every silence before
it has the chance to become a question. Nadia's rule is the first
piece of relationship architecture I've ever encountered that assumes
the gaps are allowed to simply exist, unmanaged, as long as everyone's
honest that they're there.

I mention as much to her, eventually, once I've had proper time to sit
with it. "I think I've spent my whole adult life trying to eliminate
every gap instead of just naming them."

"I know," Nadia says. "I noticed that about you roughly a week in."

"You could have said something."

"I did say something. I gave you the envelope rule." She almost
smiles. "I don't tend to explain things twice, Robyn. I say them once,
properly, and trust you to do the rest of the work yourself, in your
own time. It generally lands better that way."

I don't have an answer for that except to admit, quietly, that she's
right, which by now has become something of a running theme in this
particular education.


## Chapter Eleven — Not For the Show

We end up on a wall outside a chip shop on Byres Road on a night with
no rundown, no contract requiring it, no reason at all except that
Nadia said she was hungry and I, for once, didn't turn it into a task.

Before I see her face I hear the street: the fryer roaring through the
open door behind us, a bus somewhere up the hill changing gear with a
sound like a held breath let go, rain starting three closes away and
taking its time getting to us, the specific Glasgow weather that
announces itself before it arrives. Only then, turning, do I actually
look at her — properly, off duty, a chip halfway to her mouth, laughing
at something small I've said that wasn't even trying to be funny.

We talk about nothing, in the specific way that ends up meaning
everything. She tells me about a tour she did once through the Baltic
states with a company doing a two-hander that ran ninety minutes with
no interval, and how she used to time the whole thing from a booth at
the back and could tell, by minute forty, exactly how the audience
was going to feel about the ending. I tell her about the first thing I
ever presented, aged twenty-two, a graveyard-shift music show with an
audience she assures me, kindly, must have been at least four people,
and she laughs at the right places, and asks a follow-up question,
which — for a woman who has spent three years failing to make small
talk to save her life — I clock immediately and don't mention, because
mentioning it would be exactly the kind of thing that makes a person
stop doing it.

Nothing about the show comes up for forty minutes. I check, afterward,
lying awake, and I'm fairly sure it's a record.

Walking back, our hands are near enough that the back of hers brushes
the back of mine twice, and neither of us closes the gap, on purpose,
by an agreement neither of us has said out loud, and somehow that not-
touching does more work than an actual touch would have.

·

She's quiet back at the studio, in a way I've learned to read as
different from her ordinary quiet, and when I ask if she's alright she
doesn't deflect it into a level or a timing, which is itself the tell.

There's an envelope on the overnight desk I haven't noticed before —
a fringe theatre company's letterhead, SOUND DESIGNER, six months,
touring, unopened, with three weeks of dust settling into the crease
where she's clearly folded it and refolded it without ever getting as
far as the seal.

"I keep telling everyone I don't want it," she says. "I've told you
that. It isn't true. I don't want to *want* it, which isn't the same
thing, and I know it isn't."

"Why haven't you opened it?"

"Because touring puts me in front of people," she says, "and being in
front of people is what the podcast turned into something I still
can't talk about all the way through." She says it at the envelope, not
at me, which I understand, this once, not to push against. "There's a
reason to stay that isn't the roof."

She doesn't finish the sentence. She doesn't have to; I hear the shape
of the rest of it anyway, sitting in the room with us, unspoken and
entirely unmissable.

I don't ask her to say the rest. I've learned that much, at least. I
sit with her, in the quiet, while the level meter does its small green
work in front of us both, and let the unopened envelope stay exactly
where it is.

"Do you want it," I ask, eventually, meaning the job, not the reason.

"I don't know," Nadia says, which from her — a woman who answers
almost every question with a fact, a timing, a number — is nearly a
whole confession on its own. "I know what I'm supposed to want. Six
months, good money, a company I respect, work I'm good at. I know
what a sensible person does with that envelope."

"What does the sensible person do?"

"Opens it. Says yes. Goes back to being someone who's good at a job
and invisible while she does it, in a different city, where nobody's
heard of the podcast and nobody's going to ask."

"And what do you actually want?"

She doesn't answer that one. She looks at the envelope instead, for a
long moment, and then puts it in the desk drawer, not the bin,
which I notice, and don't say anything about, because there's a
version of this conversation where I push and a version where I let
her carry it at her own pace, and I am trying, this once, very hard,
to be the second kind of person.

We sit a while longer without talking, the level meter's small green
work the only thing moving in the room, and I find myself doing the
arithmetic anyway, quietly, the way I do everything: six weeks left on
the show, an unopened envelope with a six-month contract in it, a
woman who has just told me, as plainly as she's told me anything, that
there's a reason to stay that isn't the roof. I don't say any of that
out loud. I just sit with her, and let the desk drawer stay shut, and
try to be someone worth staying for rather than someone who simply
asks her to.

·

Priya, entirely without meaning to, hands me the exact framing I need
for it a few days later, in the kitchen, apropos of a school essay
she's meant to be writing about, of all things, market forces.

"The thing about touring work," she says, not looking up from her
laptop, in the specific tone of someone thinking out loud rather than
making conversation, "is that it always pays better than staying
somewhere does. That's just supply and demand. Staying's cheap because
everyone assumes you'll do it for free, out of loyalty, and loyalty
doesn't show up on anyone's invoice."

"That's very cynical for an essay about market forces."

"It's not cynical, it's economics." She finally looks up. "Why, is
this relevant to something?"

"No," I say, far too quickly, and Priya goes back to her essay with
the specific, satisfied silence of a teenager who knows exactly when
she's landed something and has decided, generously, not to make me say
so out loud.

I think about her sentence for days afterward. *Loyalty doesn't show
up on anyone's invoice.* It's the truest thing anyone's said to me all
month, and it isn't even about the two of us, not directly — and yet I
can't stop turning it over, wondering what it would actually take to
make staying somewhere worth as much, on paper and off it, as leaving
would be.

·

I finally ask Nadia properly about the touring years the following
weekend, over a pot of tea that's gone cold twice while we talk,
because I've decided that understanding the shape of what she's
weighing matters more than my own discomfort at asking.

"What was it like," I say, "before all this. The company you toured
with."

"Good," she says, without hesitation, which surprises me. "Properly
good. Small houses, mostly — village halls, community centres, the
odd proper theatre if we were lucky. I did the sound for a two-hander
about a lighthouse keeper that toured for three years and I could
still, today, tell you every cue in it from memory. There's a
particular kind of satisfaction in a small room that's completely
silent because you've built the silence properly, on purpose, out of
speakers and a mixing desk and about four years of learning exactly
how a room breathes."

"You miss it."

"I miss the work. I don't miss the life around the work." She turns
her mug slowly on the table. "Digs in other people's spare rooms.
Never being anywhere long enough to know which bench is dry after
rain. Being, for six months of every year, entirely and only defined
by the job, because there wasn't time to be anything else." She looks
up. "The overnight desk gave me somewhere to actually live. That's
worth more to me now than any lighthouse keeper's cue."

"So the contract—"

"The contract's good work, properly paid, genuinely tempting on every
professional measure I've got." She says it plainly, no drama in it.
"And I keep not opening it, and I think, if I'm honest with both of
us, it's because for the first time in about six years I've got a
reason to stay that has nothing to do with being too tired to leave."

I don't say anything to that. I just reach across the table and take
her hand, briefly, and she lets me, and neither of us needs to say
anything further about an envelope that's still, for now, sitting
unopened in a drawer.

·

The theatre company itself rings the following week, chasing an
answer, and I happen to be in the room when the call comes through,
which means I get to watch Nadia handle it in real time rather than
hear about it afterward secondhand.

She's kind about it. Genuinely kind, not performing kindness for my
benefit — she thanks them properly, tells them the work sounds
exactly like the sort of thing she'd have leapt at three years ago,
and asks, without any drama in it at all, for another fortnight to
decide, which they grant, sounding faintly puzzled that anyone would
need that long to say yes to what is, by any reasonable measure, an
excellent offer.

"You could have just said no," I say, once she's hung up.

"I could have. I didn't want to, not yet, because saying no felt like
deciding something before I'd actually let myself feel the whole
weight of it." She sets the phone down. "I spent two years assuming
the only two options available to me were touring, in front of
people, or hiding, behind a desk. I hadn't considered there might be
a third one, until recently, where I get to be visible on my own
terms, in one place, with people who already know me. I want to sit
with that a bit longer before I close a door on the other version."

"Take as long as you need."

"I will," Nadia says, and looks, for a moment, less like a woman
deciding between two jobs and more like a woman finally being allowed
to choose something for herself, properly, without anybody's deadline
attached to it but her own.

·

I mention the fortnight to Priya, in passing, not the details, just
that Nadia's got a decision to make and has bought herself some room
to make it properly, and Priya, for once, doesn't push for the whole
story, just nods slowly in the specific way she does when she's
filing something important away rather than gossip.

"Good," she says. "People should get fortnights. Everyone always wants
answers immediately, like a decision that takes two weeks instead of
two minutes is somehow a worse decision, when it's usually just a more
honest one."

"That's very wise for someone who complained about the wait for her
own exam results for a solid month."

"That's different. Exam results are outside my control. This is
inside hers. You should always take longer over things that are
actually yours to decide, and less time over things that are just
going to happen to you regardless." She says it with the specific
flat certainty of a teenager who has clearly thought about this more
than her age would suggest, and goes back to whatever she was doing
before, leaving me, again, faintly astonished at how much wisdom keeps
turning up in this building from a person who isn't legally old enough
to buy a lottery ticket.

I think about her distinction for the rest of the week — things that
are yours to decide, versus things that simply happen to you — and
find myself sorting my own recent choices into the two piles more
carefully than I have in years. The London job: mine to decide, and I
took my time over it, eventually. The panel's renewal: not mine, not
really, however much energy I spend managing it. Nadia: entirely,
frighteningly, mine to decide, every single day, in small ways, and I
notice, doing the sorting, that I've started taking my time over that
one too, rather than rushing to secure an answer the way I used to
rush to secure everything else.

·

Fiona rings partway through that same week, and I tell her about the
envelope, in outline, the way I've started telling her most things
about Nadia now — not the full story, because most of it isn't mine to
share, but enough that she understands the shape of what's happening.

"She sounds like someone who's been burned by moving too fast before,"
Fiona says, once I've explained the fortnight.

"I think she has."

"Then let her have the fortnight, properly, without checking in on it
every day to see how she's getting on. I know that's not your natural
setting. Do it anyway."

"I am doing it."

"Good," Fiona says, sounding genuinely pleased, in the specific tone
she reserves for moments where she's watching me manage to do
something she once doubted I could. "You know, this whole thing started
as a favour to a funding panel, and I remember thinking, when you first
told me, that it sounded like the single worst idea you'd ever had.
I want that on the record too, since we're keeping records of
everything else this autumn."

"Duly noted."

"I'm allowed to have been wrong," Fiona says, "loudly, and often, if
it means being right about something this good in the end," and rings
off, leaving me smiling at the phone in the specific way that's become,
this month, considerably more common than it used to be.


## Chapter Twelve — London, Again

There's a calendar reminder on my phone that says, simply, *answer
London*, and it is five weeks old, and I set it myself, and I have
been walking past it the way you walk past a bill you've decided to
open later.

The job is real. Senior producer, national reach, a salary that would
make the roof fund look like a rounding error, at a station I used to
believe, at twenty-six, was the entire point of doing any of this. I
read the email again for the fourth or fifth time — I've stopped
counting — standing in the office with the door shut, and I notice,
this time, a detail I'd skated past before: the sign-off is from a
name I half-recognise, someone who used to be junior when Kirsty and
I did breakfast together, who is apparently now senior enough to be
courting me on somebody else's behalf.

Eight years. Everyone moves except the people who stay.

Kirsty texts, unprompted, *just checking in about the format, no
pressure :)*, which manages to be, somehow, the most pressure available
in the English language, four words and an emoji doing the work of an
entire committee meeting.

I don't tell Nadia it's resurfaced. I notice myself not telling her,
which is new, and which I don't examine too closely, because examining
it would mean admitting there's now a category of thing I keep from
her on purpose, and I am not ready for that category to have a name
yet. I tell myself it's not relevant to the show. I tell myself there's
nothing to tell until I've actually decided something. Both of those
are true and neither of them is the reason.

·

Broadcast three, and the rundown has a slot for a pre-screened caller
with a nice, safe question about anniversaries.

What we get instead, patched through by an error nobody will ever fully
explain, is a real one — unscreened, live, a woman's voice slightly
shaking, asking: "How did you know you could trust each other
completely?"

She means it. Her name's Fiona — a different Fiona from the one on my
midnight list of candidates, a coincidence I clock and immediately
discard, because there's no room in this moment for anything except
the actual question. She's waiting for an answer, the way people wait
when the question actually matters to them, breath audible even
through a phone line, and the scripted line is right there on the
rundown, four words away from my mouth — *communication is a muscle*,
something Priya and I workshopped over a whiteboard three weeks ago —
and neither of us reaches for it.

What comes out instead is true and entirely un-cued: something about
an overnight shift, a fader slid across without being asked, a mug of
tea that arrived unrequested and meant, without either of us saying
so, *I noticed*. None of it makes sense to a stranger listening in.
All of it is real, and for about four seconds — half a beat, less than
it takes Priya to reach for the next sting — the fake thing and the
true thing touch, live, in front of an audience, before either of us
catches it and I cue the next segment on pure instinct.

Off air, in the corridor, both of us are shaking slightly, and neither
says why.

Priya, mercifully, does not comment on it directly, though she watches
us both with the specific narrowed attention of someone doing sums she
isn't ready to say out loud yet either. She busies herself instead
with the automation queue for the next segment, giving us both, I
realise afterward, exactly the kind of privacy a seventeen-year-old
has no obligation whatsoever to extend to her employer and extends
anyway, because she is, under the gossip and the spreadsheets, kinder
than she generally lets on.

I go home that night and don't draft any texts at all. There doesn't
seem to be a sentence small enough to hold what just happened, and for
once I don't try to build one anyway.

·

The London decision doesn't get any easier for being deferred. If
anything, deferring it makes the deferring itself into its own small
daily task — the calendar reminder, snoozed rather than answered, the
recruiter's second email arriving exactly a week after the first with
the specific patient persistence of someone who's been told, somewhere
up the chain, that I'm worth waiting for.

I read the second email standing at the kettle, the same kettle, the
same four minutes, and notice, this time, that my first reaction isn't
excitement or dread but something closer to irritation — the
particular irritation of being interrupted mid-thought by a decision
I've already, somewhere below the level I'm willing to admit to, more
or less made.

I don't answer it that day either. But I notice the irritation, and
file it, alongside everything else this month, for later examination,
in the growing queue of things I am, however slowly, finally starting
to actually look at rather than simply carry.

·

The caller from broadcast three — Fiona, the one whose voice shook,
the one who asked the question neither of us could script an answer
to — writes in a few days later, care of the station's general
inbox, a short message thanking us for the answer and saying, almost
in passing, that she'd gone home that night and had the actual
conversation with her own partner that the call had been circling
around for weeks.

I read it out to Nadia at the desk. She doesn't say anything for a
moment, just looks at the screen for a long time, and then says,
quietly, "that's the whole job, isn't it. Not the panel. Not the
metric. Just — that."

"Just that," I agree.

Neither of us says anything else about London, or the panel, or the
particular category of thing I've started keeping from her. But I
notice, watching her read Fiona's message a second time, that whatever
I decide about that job, it's going to have to survive being weighed
against a moment like this one, and that the maths, when I finally do
it properly, is not going to be close.

·

Kirsty texts again that same evening, a short message this time,
about the broadcast three clip rather than about London: *heard about
the four seconds. People are talking about it in a good way, for
once. Well done, both of you.*

It's a small, ordinary kindness, and I let it be exactly that — a
colleague passing on some cheering news — without reading anything
further into it, because there isn't, as far as I can tell, anything
further to read.

·

Douglas, for his part, is considerably less charmed by broadcast
three than the general mood in the building would suggest he ought to
be. His email arrives two days later, addressed to me rather than the
whole panel distribution list, which I've learned by now to read as
its own small warning sign.

*While the content continues to perform well on engagement metrics,*
it reads, in the specific bloodless register I'm coming to recognise
as his native tongue, *the panel would want assurance that the format
maintains appropriate professional boundaries, particularly regarding
unscreened caller content going forward. Recommend tightening screening
protocols ahead of the next review point.*

"He's worried about the four seconds," Priya says, reading over my
shoulder, uninvited, as ever. "The bit everyone else loved. He's
worried about the exact bit that made the show good."

"He's worried about liability, not quality. Those aren't the same
thing to him."

"They should be the same thing to everyone," Priya says, with the
specific moral clarity available only to a seventeen-year-old who
hasn't yet had to sit across a table from a funding panel and learn to
speak their particular dialect of caution. "That was the best four
seconds of radio this station's ever produced and he wants to make
sure it never happens again."

I don't disagree with her, not really, but I also recognise, reading
the email a second time, the specific shape of a warning that's going
to matter later, in ways I can't yet fully see the edges of — a
funding review that has decided, somewhere behind its bloodless prose,
that the show's real strength is also its real risk, and that those
two facts are going to keep sitting uncomfortably close together for
as long as the renewal remains undecided.

I don't reply to Douglas that day. I sit with the email a while
instead, the way I've started sitting with things rather than
immediately managing them, and notice, with some discomfort, that the
version of me who'd have fired back a reassuring response within the
hour is, these days, considerably harder to locate than she used to
be.

·

I do eventually reply to Douglas, two days later, once I've had time
to draft something that doesn't read as either capitulation or
confrontation, and I show it to Nadia before I send it, the way I've
started showing her most things that touch the show now, regardless of
whether they technically fall under her job description.

"You've been reasonable," she says, reading it over. "Possibly too
reasonable. You've agreed to review the screening protocol without
actually agreeing to change anything about how the segment runs."

"That's deliberate."

"I know it's deliberate. I'm just checking you know it's deliberate,
rather than it being an accident of you being too tired to hold a
firmer line." She hands the laptop back. "It's good. Send it."

I send it, and the reply that comes back from Douglas a day later is,
by his standards, almost warm — a single line acknowledging the
response, no further demands, which I've learned by now to read as
roughly the temperature of an actual thaw rather than merely an absence
of complaint.

"He's not a villain," Nadia says, later, when I mention the reply.
"I know it's easy to make him one. He's just a man whose entire job is
covering the panel's exposure, and covering exposure and caring about
the actual work aren't always the same instinct, even in someone who
isn't secretly malicious about it."

"When did you get so generous about Douglas?"

"I'm not being generous. I'm being accurate," Nadia says, which I've
come to understand, by now, is simply how she experiences most acts of
fairness — not as generosity extended, but as a fact correctly stated,
regardless of how much warmer it might look from the outside.

·

Priya, who has clearly been keeping her own private ledger of Douglas
interactions since the clipboard incident, greets the news of the
thaw with visible disappointment.

"That's it? One reasonable email and he's just — nice now?"

"People are allowed to be won round, Priya."

"I had a whole campaign planned. Months of strategic clipboard
sabotage." She sighs, the specific put-upon sigh of a general robbed
of her war by an early peace treaty. "Fine. I suppose grudging thaw is
still a victory. I'll update the spreadsheet to reflect a diplomatic
resolution rather than the glorious rout I was hoping for."

"You wanted a rout against a funding panel member."

"I wanted *justice*," Priya says, with enormous dignity, "and I'm
choosing to interpret a one-line acknowledgement email as a partial
delivery of it." She closes her laptop with the specific finality of
someone declaring a subject closed, at least for today, and goes to
put the kettle on, leaving me to reflect, not for the first time, on
how much of this building's actual morale runs on a seventeen-year-
old's capacity to turn even a bureaucratic thaw into a small personal
triumph worth celebrating properly.

·

The caller from broadcast three — the other Fiona, the one whose
question neither of us could script an answer to — writes in again a
few weeks later, a longer message this time, thanking us properly and
mentioning, almost in passing, that she and her partner have decided to
get married in the spring.

I read it aloud to Nadia at the desk, and she goes quiet in the
particular way that means she's genuinely moved rather than merely
processing information.

"We didn't do that," she says, eventually. "Whatever got them there.
We just happened to be on air at the right four seconds. They did the
actual work themselves."

"I know. But we were the four seconds."

"That's a strange thing to have been," Nadia says, "for two people who
started this whole show as a favour to a funding panel." She looks at
the message again. "I keep thinking about what Priya said. That there's
no difference left to tell, between the segment and whatever's actually
happening underneath it. I think that's exactly why this landed the way
it did. If we'd been faking it, that caller would have heard four
seconds of performance. She heard something real, by accident, and it
apparently helped her decide something enormous about her own life."

"Does that frighten you? How much weight a few unscripted seconds can
carry?"

"A bit," Nadia admits. "Mostly it makes me want to be more careful with
the unscripted seconds, rather than less generous with them. There's a
difference between being frightened of something and deciding it
deserves more care because it matters. I think I've spent most of my
life doing the first one when I should have been doing the second."

I don't have anything to add to that, so I let it sit between us,
the two of us reading and rereading a stranger's happy news at a desk
in a basement that started this whole autumn as a funding-panel
gambit and has become, somewhere along the way, considerably more.

·

I tell Fiona about the wedding message that same night, and she goes
quiet on the line for a moment, the specific quiet she reserves for
news that means more to her than she immediately lets on.

"That's the kind of thing you don't get to plan for," she says,
eventually. "You set out to save a roof. You end up changing the
actual course of a stranger's life, by accident, because you happened
to say the true thing on air instead of the scripted one. I hope you
let yourself feel how enormous that is, rather than just filing it
under good outcomes and moving on to the next problem."

"I'm trying to let myself feel it properly."

"Good," Fiona says. "That's new for you, and I like it," and we talk a
while longer, about nothing much, before ringing off, leaving me
sitting at the kitchen table with a stranger's happy news and a
growing sense that this whole strange autumn has turned out to matter
in ways considerably larger than a funding renewal ever could.


## Chapter Thirteen — The Voicemail

"That wasn't in the rundown," Nadia says, in the corridor, which from
her is practically a confession.

"No," I agree, and neither of us elaborates for a while. We stand
there not quite looking at each other, both aware that something has
shifted that a rundown can't route around anymore, however carefully
either of us tries.

Priya walks past with a mic stand under one arm. "You two are
insufferable," she says, with real, unmistakable affection, and keeps
walking, and neither of us corrects her.

"She's not wrong," Nadia says, once Priya's out of earshot.

"No," I agree.

"I keep waiting for one of us to say something about it. The four
seconds."

"Me too."

"Neither of us is going to, are we."

"Not tonight," I say, which isn't a no, and we both hear that it
isn't a no, and stand there in the corridor a moment longer before the
next thing on the rota calls one of us away, as it always, eventually,
does.

·

I find the box in the loft on the Sunday, looking for an old contract
of my own that turns out not to be in there at all — a stipend
agreement from my first year here, if you're curious, entirely
irrelevant, the kind of thing you decide you urgently need at eleven
on a Sunday morning specifically so you have a reason to be up a
loft ladder instead of doing anything that requires actual feeling —
and instead there's an answering machine tape with my mother's
handwriting on the label, unplayed since the funeral.

I sit up there for a while, cross-legged on loft insulation that
itches through my jeans, holding a cassette the size of a bar of soap
that contains, as far as I can work out, the last new recording of my
mother's voice that exists anywhere. She left it on the station's old
answerphone the week before she died, a message about nothing —
whether I'd remembered to renew the transmitter insurance, something
about the weather, ordinary Tuesday business — and somebody, at some
point, in some act of quiet kindness I've never properly thanked
anyone for, transferred it off the system and onto a cassette and
labelled it, in handwriting I don't recognise, MUM — DON'T DELETE.

My whole adult architecture, laid bare on one shelf: I became the
reliable one at nineteen because somebody had to be, and I have not
once, since, stopped long enough to grieve on anything resembling a
schedule. There was always a roof. There was always a rota. Grief, it
turns out, is remarkably easy to postpone indefinitely if you're
sufficiently good at being needed elsewhere.

I remember the week itself in fragments rather than a story — the
hospital corridor with the vending machine that only took exact
change, the specific way Priya's predecessor at the desk kept the
station running for nine days without once asking me to come in, the
funeral itself mostly a blur except for the very last thing, which was
walking back into the basement four days later because there was a
Thursday show that needed presenting and nobody else who could do it,
and discovering that doing the show was, in fact, easier than sitting
at home with the quiet. I have been discovering some version of that
same fact, on a loop, for six years. Work first. Feeling later, if at
all, on a schedule I control.

It has never once occurred to me, until this exact moment on a loft
ladder, cassette in hand, that *later* has simply never arrived, and
that I have built an entire life, a whole capable and well-regarded
life, around a debt to my own grief that I have quietly, competently,
never once paid.

I don't play the tape. I hold it for a while, and then I put it back
in the box, and I let myself cry about it for exactly four minutes,
with the timer on my phone running, because a timer is the only
permission structure I currently have access to.

I tell nobody. Not yet. I put the box back where it was and go back
downstairs to a rota that, as ever, needs me.

·

I think about the tape for the rest of the week, in the particular way
grief resurfaces once you've finally cracked the seal on it — not
constantly, not dramatically, just present, at odd moments, in the
gap between one task and the next. I catch myself, twice, reaching for
my phone to call her about something entirely ordinary — a broken
fader, a caller who reminded me of something she'd have laughed at —
before remembering, both times, with the same small fresh shock, that
there's nobody on the other end of that particular number any more.

I don't mention any of this to Nadia yet. It feels, somehow, too large
and too recent to hand over properly, and I've learned enough from her,
this month, to know the difference between sharing something and
simply offloading it onto the nearest available person. When I do tell
her, eventually, I want it to be because I've chosen to, not because
the grief got too heavy to carry alone for one more day. There's a
difference, and it's a difference I'm only just learning to
recognise, four days at a time, one avoided phone call at a time.

·

I do, eventually, ring Fiona in Aberdeen and tell her about the tape,
because there's a difference between offloading grief onto whoever's
nearest and choosing, deliberately, to hand a piece of it to the one
other person who actually knew our mother the way I did.

"I remember that message," Fiona says, quietly, once I've explained
which recording it is. "She left one for me too, the same week.
Different message. Same voice."

"You never told me that."

"You never told me about yours either," Fiona says, not unkindly, "and
I think that's rather the whole family tradition, isn't it. Everybody
carrying their own bit of her, separately, because nobody wanted to be
the one who brought it up first and made the other one sad."

We sit on the phone for a long time after that, not saying very much,
both of us listening to the specific quality of shared silence that
only exists between people who've lost the same person and are, for
once, allowing themselves to sit in that loss together rather than
apart. It doesn't fix anything. But it does, for the first time in six
years, feel like carrying something in company rather than alone,
which turns out to be most of what either of us actually needed.

·

I take the tape out of the box again the following Sunday, alone, in
the quiet of my own flat, and get as far as holding it in both hands
before I put it back, unplayed, for a second time.

I'm not ready for the whole of it yet. Four seconds cost me more than
I expected, and I've learned enough about myself this month to know
the difference between pushing through a feeling because it's brave
and pushing through it because stopping would mean admitting I'm not
ready, which isn't bravery at all, just another version of managing
something rather than actually sitting with it.

So I wait. I put the tape back in the box, and the box back on the
shelf, and tell myself, without much conviction, that there'll be a
better moment for the rest of it, sometime, though I have no idea yet
what that moment is going to look like, or who might be standing next
to me when it finally arrives.

·

I mention the loft, though not the tape itself, to Nadia a few days
later, mostly because the silence around it has started to feel like
its own small dishonesty, and I've been trying, since the envelope
conversation, to get better at naming the gaps rather than just
managing around them.

"There's a thing in a box in my loft I haven't told you about," I say.
"I'm not ready to tell you what it is yet. I wanted you to know there's
a thing, rather than just letting you notice I've gone quiet some
evenings and wondering why."

Nadia looks at me for a long moment, and I can see her deciding,
visibly, not to push, the same restraint she's shown me a dozen times
already this month.

"Okay," she says. "Thank you for telling me there's a thing. That's
different from telling me the thing, and I know the difference costs
you something to hold onto."

"It does."

"Take whatever time it needs," she says. "I'm not going anywhere while
you work out what to do with a box in a loft."

I don't say anything else about it that evening, but I notice, walking
home, that having said even that much — a thing exists, I'm not ready,
please don't ask — has lightened something in my chest that I hadn't
fully registered as heavy until it was carrying slightly less.

Priya, separately, catches me looking distracted at the desk that same
week and, with her usual bluntness, asks outright whether I'm alright.

"I'm fine," I say, automatically, and then, catching myself, the way
I've been trying to catch myself lately: "Actually. Not entirely. I
found something of my mum's. I'm still working out what to do with
it."

Priya doesn't say anything clever or comforting. She just says, "that's
allowed to take as long as it takes," with a gravity that surprises me
coming from a seventeen-year-old, and goes back to the desk, and lets
me have the rest of the shift in a version of quiet that doesn't feel
like being managed, which is, I am learning, an entirely different
thing from being left alone.

·

I find myself, in the days after, noticing my mother in small,
unexpected places around the building — a turn of phrase Sandra uses on
a Thursday call that sounds, for a second, exactly like something my
mother used to say about the weather; the specific way the kettle's
handle has worn smooth in one particular spot, the same spot worn
smooth on the kettle in our childhood kitchen, as though some habits of
hand simply pass themselves down regardless of who's holding the
handle.

I mention this to nobody, at first, because it feels too small and too
strange to hand over as a proper sentence. But I write it down, in the
same notebook I've started using for everything I can't quite say out
loud yet, alongside the four minutes I allowed myself on the loft
ladder and the fact of a cassette that says, on its label, in
handwriting I don't recognise, MUM — DON'T DELETE.

It occurs to me, writing it down, that grief postponed doesn't
actually go anywhere in the meantime. It just waits, patiently, in the
worn handle of a kettle and the cadence of a stranger's phone call,
for the one moment you're finally ready to let it back into the room
properly, and that moment, I am slowly starting to suspect, might not
be very far away at all.

·

Nadia notices the notebook before I've said anything about it — catches
me writing in it one evening at the overnight desk, in the specific
unhurried way she notices most things, and doesn't ask what's in it,
just asks, gently, whether writing it down is helping.

"I think so," I say. "It's not fixing anything. It's just making the
carrying a bit less shapeless."

"That's usually enough, to start with." She doesn't push for more, the
way she's learned not to, the way I'm learning not to with her in
return. "For what it's worth — and I don't offer unsolicited comparisons
often — grief and the podcast thing aren't the same shape, but they
rhyme, a bit. Both of them are about something private getting handled
in public before you're ready, except yours is entirely self-inflicted,
which is its own particular kind of hard."

"How do you mean, self-inflicted?"

"You're the one who won't let yourself grieve on your own schedule,"
Nadia says. "Nobody's doing that to you. You're doing it to yourself,
the same reflex that had you covering a three a.m. shift instead of
waking anyone up. It's not a criticism. I think it's just useful to say
out loud, the way it was useful when you said the true thing about
London instead of managing it."

I sit with that for a long while after she's gone back to the desk,
turning it over the way I turn over most of what she says that lands
harder than she probably intends it to, and find, walking home later, that
she's right, again, in the specific way she generally is — not unkindly,
just accurately, which somehow always lands more gently than kindness
alone ever quite manages to.

·

I ring Fiona again, later that same week, to tell her I've decided,
finally, on a day for the tape — not tomorrow, not a specific date I've
picked out of pressure, just a decision that it's coming, soon, on my
own terms rather than avoided indefinitely.

"Do you want me there," Fiona asks, "when you play it properly?"

I think about the question longer than it probably needs, because it's
a generous offer and deserves a considered answer rather than a
reflexive one.

"I don't think so," I say, eventually. "Not because I don't want you
there. Because I think this particular one, I need to do with Nadia,
and I think you'd understand why, even though I can't fully explain it
yet myself."

"I do understand," Fiona says, gently. "I'm glad you've found someone
you trust with it. That's not nothing, Robyn, after six years of doing
every hard thing entirely on your own out of some misplaced idea that
needing help made you less reliable."

"I'm working on that idea too."

"I know you are," Fiona says. "I can hear it, actually, in how you talk
now. You sound like someone who's finally decided being reliable and
being alone were never the same requirement in the first place," and
rings off before I can find a response to that which doesn't risk
undoing me slightly, leaving me to sit with the observation the rest
of the evening, turning it over the way I turn over most of what she
says that lands harder than she probably intends.


## Chapter Fourteen — Answering London

I write the email after hours, alone in the office, with the amber
bulb doing its unconvincing impression of red through the wall behind
me.

I write four drafts before I settle on one. The first is all
gratitude and hedging, the kind of email I could have sent five weeks
ago without changing a single fact about my actual life. The second is
too short, a single line, the sort of thing you send when you're
angry at the question rather than answering it. The third is,
horrifyingly, three paragraphs about the roof, as though the panel's
own admin had written it, which tells me everything about how much of
my personality is currently on loan to that building.

The fourth one I write properly — not out of the inertia that's kept
it sitting unopened for five weeks, but for real reasons, typed out in
full: the station. The roof, which is somehow both the smallest and
the largest reason available. Priya, who is seventeen and has nowhere
else that would let her run a Saturday shift like she owns it. Six
years of Thursdays that mean something to more people than a
spreadsheet can capture. And then, because I make myself, because the
sentence sits there on the screen and I let it, a reason I am not
ready to say out loud yet but am, apparently, ready to act on anyway.

I send it before I can revise it into something safer. My hand is
actually shaking a little when I close the laptop, which strikes me,
afterward, as an absurd amount of physiology for a job I'm turning
down rather than one I'm accepting.

Then I go and find Nadia, and I tell her the whole of it — the offer,
the five weeks it sat unanswered, the reminder I set myself and then
studiously ignored, the fact that answering it felt, the entire time,
like asking for something and risking being told no in a way I could
count, on a form, with a date on it.

"Five weeks," Nadia says, when I've finished. Not an accusation. Just
checking the number.

"Five weeks."

"That's a long time to carry a decision around without letting anyone
help you carry it."

"I know."

"Did you decide because of the station," she asks, "or because of
something else, and the station's just the part you're comfortable
saying out loud first?"

I don't answer that immediately, because it's the exact question I've
been avoiding asking myself, and hearing it said back to me, plainly,
in her voice, makes it considerably harder to keep avoiding.

"Both," I say, eventually. "The station's real. It's just not the
whole of it."

"Okay," Nadia says, and doesn't push for the rest, which — I'm coming
to understand — is the single most generous thing she knows how to
do.

She doesn't perform relief. She doesn't manage the moment for me, the
way I have spent my whole life managing moments for other people. She
just listens, all the way through, without once looking away, which
turns out to be its own kind of answer — better, if I'm honest, than
anything she could have said.

It's the first time in this whole strange arrangement that I've stated
a want out loud, plainly, to another person, without it being
necessary for a show, a panel, or anybody's engagement metric.

It does not feel like relief exactly. It feels like standing somewhere
higher up than I'm used to, with rather less to hold onto than I'd
like, and rather more room to actually see.

Kirsty texts two days later — a different register from the *just
checking in* of before, shorter, more careful. *Heard you turned down
London. Are you alright?* I stare at it for a while before I answer,
because it's the first time in eight years she's asked me a question
that isn't, in some fashion, about the panel or the format or the
renewal, and I don't quite know what to do with a Kirsty who's asking
after me rather than after the show.

*I'm good*, I write back, eventually. *Better than I've been in a
while, actually.* I don't say why. I'm not ready to, not with her, not
yet. But I mean every word of it, which is itself new enough to notice.

·

Priya finds out about the decision from the group chat, three hours
after Nadia does, and responds with the specific outrage of a
seventeen-year-old who considers herself entitled to first billing on
all major station news.

"You told *Nadia* before you told me?"

"I told Nadia in person. I told the group chat because I had to tell
someone before I lost my nerve entirely."

"That's not the same as telling me first."

"Priya, I run this building. I don't have to tell you things in a
particular order."

"You do, actually," she says, with the weary certainty of someone
citing an established statute. "I do the gossip *and* the desk. There's
a protocol."

I don't have an answer to that, and I don't try to construct one,
because there isn't a version of this conversation where I win it, and
because, if I'm honest, I don't especially want to. Being the subject
of Priya's outrage, this particular week, feels considerably better
than being the subject of nobody's attention at all, which is, more or
less, how most of my adult life has gone until now.

·

The recruiter from London rings, once, properly, rather than emailing,
a week after my answer's gone in, which I understand to be a genuine
attempt to talk me out of it rather than mere procedure.

"I want to be honest with you," she says, in the specific measured
tone of someone who has clearly rehearsed this call on her own bus.
"We don't usually follow up like this. But your reputation precedes
you, and I think you might be underestimating what you're turning
down."

"I don't think I am."

"The salary alone—"

"I know what the salary is," I say, gently, because she's only doing
her job and doesn't deserve the sharper version of this conversation.
"I've thought about it properly. It's not that I don't understand what
I'm turning down. It's that I've worked out, this year, what I'd be
turning down to take it, and the second column's heavier than the
first."

She doesn't have a counter for that, because it isn't the kind of
objection her script has an answer prepared for, and we end the call
politely, and I put the phone down feeling, for the first time since
the offer arrived, entirely and uncomplicatedly certain.

·

Priya asks me, a few days later, whether I regret it — the version of
the question a seventeen-year-old asks when she's genuinely curious
rather than fishing for reassurance, which is its own kind of
compliment.

"Not for a second," I say.

"Not even a little bit? It was a lot of money."

"It was a lot of money," I agree, "for a job that would have made me
someone else's version of myself, in a city where nobody knows this
building exists, running a schedule I didn't build for an audience I
didn't choose. I've spent nine years making this place mine. I wasn't
ready to trade that for a bigger office and a worse Tuesday."

"That's very poetic for someone who spends most of her time worrying
about buckets."

"I contain multitudes, Priya."

"You contain a leaking roof," Priya says, and goes back to her
laptop, and I let her have the last word, because it's a good one,
and because I've stopped needing to win every exchange with a
seventeen-year-old to feel secure in my own decisions.

·

Fiona rings that weekend to hear the whole story properly, having only
had the group chat's abbreviated version to work from, and makes me
tell it twice — once for the facts, once, in her words, "for how it
actually felt," which turns out to be the harder of the two tellings
by some distance.

"I'm proud of you," she says, once I've finished the second version,
in a tone she doesn't often use, because we're not, either of us, a
family that says things like that lightly. "Not for turning down the
money. For knowing what you actually wanted quickly enough to say no
before it had time to talk you round."

"It didn't feel quick. It felt like five weeks of staring at a
calendar reminder."

"Five weeks is quick, for you," Fiona says. "You've been not-deciding
things for considerably longer than that, historically. I still
remember you taking the better part of a year to admit you were
unhappy at the old flat before you finally moved."

"That's different."

"It's the same shape of thing, just a smaller decision." A pause,
gentler. "I think this Nadia's good for you, from what little I've
heard. Not because of the deciding-faster thing, though that's
something. Because you sound, on the phone, like someone who's
actually living her own life at the moment, rather than narrating it
to me after the fact once it's already safely over."

I don't have a response to that which doesn't risk giving away more
than I've fully worked out myself yet, so I just say, "I think she
might be," and let Fiona hear, in the way I say it, everything I'm not
yet ready to say in full sentences, and she, mercifully, lets that be
enough for one phone call.

·

Kirsty rings again, a few days after the London decision's settled
properly, this time about something else entirely — a genuinely
practical question about the transmitter contract, the kind of call
she'd have made to me routinely for years if either of us had ever let
our professional relationship stay merely professional — and at the end
of it, almost as an afterthought, she says, "for what it's worth, I
think you made the right call. On London. I nearly went, myself, when
they made me the same kind of offer, three years after I actually left
for the national job."

"You never told me that."

"There wasn't a version of telling you that didn't sound like I was
asking you to validate my own choices," Kirsty says. "I'm telling you
now because I think you've actually worked out something I still
haven't, eight years on, which is how to want the smaller, harder,
more local thing without treating it as a consolation prize."

"Is that what London would have been? A consolation prize?"

"No," Kirsty says, after a pause. "That's rather the problem. It would
have been genuinely good. That's what makes turning it down actually
brave, rather than merely comfortable. Anyone can turn down a bad
offer. It takes something else entirely to turn down a good one because
you've decided something smaller suits you better."

I don't have a ready response to that, and Kirsty, characteristically,
doesn't wait for one, moving briskly on to a genuinely dull point about
insurance clauses, and I let the conversation carry on in its ordinary,
practical register, grateful, in a way I don't examine too closely, for
the particular kindness of being complimented and then immediately
allowed to move past it without having to sit in the glow of it for
longer than is comfortable.

·

Nadia asks me, a few evenings later, what I'd have done if the answer
had been yes instead — if I'd taken the London job, packed up nine
years of buckets and rotas, and left the building to whoever came
after me.

"I don't know," I admit. "I hadn't let myself think that far into it,
because thinking that far into it would have meant admitting I was
genuinely considering it, rather than just going through the motions
of considering it before saying no."

"That's very you," Nadia says, without any particular edge to it. "Not
looking directly at the version of the decision that frightens you
most, in case looking at it makes it real."

"Is that a criticism?"

"It's an observation. I don't think you can help it yet. I think it's
something you're working on, the same way I'm working on not assuming
every door needs three conditions written on the back of a cue sheet
before I'll walk through it." She looks at me properly. "I just wanted
to say, since we're being honest about hypotheticals — if you had taken
it, I think I'd have understood. I wouldn't have liked it. But I'd have
understood, because I know what it is to almost take the safer,
bigger, further-away version of a life, and I'd never have held it
against you for choosing it."

"I didn't choose it."

"I know," Nadia says. "I'm glad. I just wanted you to know the door
wasn't closed on my end either way, because I don't want you thinking
you owe me the smaller life, as if staying were a debt you're paying
off rather than something you actually wanted for its own sake."

I think about that distinction for a long time afterward — staying as
a debt versus staying as a want — and find, turning it over, that she's
identified something I hadn't quite named for myself yet, the exact
line between the two that I've spent most of my adult life blurring
without ever noticing I was doing it.

·

I mention the conversation to Priya a few days later, in outline, not
the specifics, and she considers it with the specific gravity she
reserves for questions she's decided are genuinely worth her full
attention rather than the ones she just enjoys being clever about.

"I think," she says, eventually, "that most people who grow up having
to be reliable early — and I'd count myself in that category, for the
record, given my mum's shifts — end up thinking love and usefulness are
the same thing. Like the only way to deserve being kept around is to
keep being useful enough that nobody ever has a reason to reconsider."

"That's rather exactly it."

"I worked that out about myself a while ago," Priya says, with the
specific matter-of-factness of someone who has clearly given the
subject considerable private thought. "It's why I keep offering to
resign every time something goes wrong that isn't even my fault. I
think some part of me still believes I have to earn my place here
every single week, rather than just — having it, because people
actually want me around, not because I'm useful."

"You don't have to earn it."

"I know that now," Priya says. "You've told me that a few times this
autumn, and I'm choosing, slowly, to actually believe it rather than
just hear it. I think that's probably what you need to do too, with
whoever it is you're not naming just now. Believe you're wanted rather
than just useful. It's a slower thing to learn than it sounds."

I don't have a response that doesn't risk giving away more than I've
told her, so I just say, "that's very wise," and let her have the
moment, and think, walking home that evening, that I've apparently
built an entire building full of people quietly working out the same
lesson at their own pace, in their own time, together.


## Chapter Fifteen — Off the Air, For Real

Broadcast four is, by now, brief enough on the page that I could tell
you the whole of it in one sentence: a chores argument, mediated with
real warmth, no stunt beat, no near miss, both of us genuinely good at
this in a way that has stopped requiring engineering at all.

That, if I'm honest, is what frightens me about it. The show has
started running itself. A listener writes in asking where we got
engaged, and neither of us has an answer prepared, and we improvise
past it, badly, live, laughing so hard Priya has to fade the sting in
over the top of us.

"You realise," Priya says afterward, headset around her neck, "that
you don't have an engagement story because you're not actually
engaged, and also that you both completely forgot that mid-broadcast,
which is either extremely good acting or something I'm not going to
say out loud because I value my job."

"Say it," Nadia says.

"I'm not saying it."

"Priya."

"I have chosen," Priya says, with enormous dignity, "not to editorialise
about my employer's love life a second time this month," and picks up
a mic stand and goes, leaving the two of us to sit with the fact that
neither of us has corrected her.

·

Nadia's flat, later, on an evening neither the panel nor the rota has
any claim on: a record player going quietly in the corner, a kettle
that takes even longer than the station's, no cameras, no cue sheet,
nothing between us but the actual room.

"Can I ask you something," I say.

"You're always allowed to ask."

"If I wanted to do that again — the thing from the first broadcast.
Not for the show. Not at fifty-four minutes. Just because I wanted to
— would that be alright?"

I ask it plainly, because the terms she gave me on a cue sheet in week
one were scoped to the segment, and I am not going to assume for one
second that they cover this, whatever this is turning into.

Nadia takes the question exactly as seriously as she's taken every
question I've ever asked her, which is to say, entirely. She looks at
me for a long moment, thinking it through properly, the way she thinks
through everything, and when she answers it's with a new kind of yes —
private, hers to give, belonging to nobody's rundown but ours.

"Yes," she says. "Not for the show."

"Not for the show," I agree.

The kiss that follows doesn't hit a cue. Nobody's counting the seconds.
It isn't over in fifty-five, because there's no clock in the room at
all, and afterward Nadia laughs — once, low, entirely unguarded, a
sound I have never once heard her make on air or off — and neither of
us reaches for a broadcast link to deflect with, because for the first
time there isn't one available, and instead of that being a problem it
is, quietly, an enormous relief.

It is, without any real competition, the best evening either of us
can currently name.

We stay up long past any sensible hour, the record player having
worked through whatever was on it twice, talking in the loose,
unstructured way of two people with nowhere they need to be and no
rundown to hit. She tells me about growing up in a flat two streets
from where she lives now, one of five children, all of them loud
except her, which she says like it's a small joke rather than the
central fact of a whole childhood spent finding one quiet job in a
loud house and keeping it. I tell her about running the graveyard-shift
show at twenty-two and falling asleep, once, on air, for four minutes,
mid-song, and waking up to find the phone lines had lit up with
listeners checking I was alright rather than complaining, which was
the exact moment I understood what this job could actually be for.

"You've never told anyone that," Nadia says. Not a question.

"No."

"Why me?"

"Because you'd ask a follow-up question instead of just laughing," I
say, "and you did," and she looks, for a second, entirely undone by
that, in a way I file away to think about for a long time afterward.

"What was the question you actually wanted someone to ask," she says,
after a while, "back then. At twenty-two. Not what happened next in
the story. What you actually wanted to be asked."

I think about it properly, because she's asked it properly, and it
takes me longer to answer than I expect it to.

"Whether I was lonely," I say, eventually. "Nobody ever asked that. Not
my mum, because she had enough of her own to carry. Not Kirsty, because
we were always too busy being brilliant together to talk about the
version of me that existed outside the two of us. I don't think I'd
have known how to answer it properly even if someone had asked. But I
wanted someone to ask."

"Were you? Lonely?"

"Constantly," I say, and it's the first time I've said the word out
loud about myself in what feels like a decade, "and extremely good at
making sure nobody could tell."

Nadia doesn't say anything clever back to that. She just moves closer
on the sofa, close enough that our shoulders touch, and stays there,
which is, I am beginning to understand, her actual answer to almost
everything that matters — not a sentence, but a presence, offered
without conditions, for exactly as long as it's wanted.

We fall asleep there, eventually, sitting up, which is a genuinely
terrible way to sleep and neither of us moves to fix it, and I wake at
some point in the small hours with a crick in my neck and her head on
my shoulder and the record player still turning, silently, on a
groove that finished hours ago, and lie there for a long while not
moving, not wanting to be anywhere else in the world badly enough to
risk waking her.

She wakes properly around four, disoriented for a second in the way
people are when they've fallen asleep somewhere they didn't plan to,
and looks at me, and at the room, and at the record player still
spinning its silent groove, and says, quietly, "we should move to a
bed at some point. In theory."

"In theory."

Neither of us moves. We stay exactly where we are until the light
outside starts doing its grey September thing at the edges of the
curtains, and it is, of all the mornings in this strange autumn, the
one I'd choose to keep if I could only keep one — nothing dramatic
happening in it at all, just two people, a stiff neck, and a record
that finished hours ago and nobody minding in the slightest.

·

We do, eventually, make it to an actual bed, sometime past six, and
sleep properly for a few hours, and I wake at half nine to the
specific domestic strangeness of a flat that isn't mine, sunlight
coming in at an angle I don't recognise, and Nadia already up, standing
at the window with two mugs of tea, watching the rain that's started
again in earnest.

"You're doing the thing where you watch me sleep," I say, not
entirely awake yet.

"I'm doing the thing where I make tea and it happens to take place
near a window you're also in," Nadia says, entirely unbothered, and
hands me a mug, milk, correct amount, no sugar, which she's clearly
made from memory rather than asking, and something about that small
unremarkable accuracy undoes me slightly more than the whole of the
previous evening did.

"Can I tell you something," she says, sitting on the edge of the bed,
"that I haven't fully worked out how to say properly yet, so it might
come out wrong."

"Go on."

"I've spent three years making sure nobody got close enough to matter.
Not out of nothing — out of the thing I still haven't told you the
whole of. And I want you to know that this," she gestures, vaguely, at
the room, the bed, the two mugs, "isn't nothing to me. It's the first
time in three years I've let it be anything at all. I don't entirely
know what to do with that, so I'm just going to say it plainly and let
you do whatever you want with the information."

"I'm not going to do anything with it except be glad you said it."

"That's a very restrained response."

"I've had a lot of practice being restrained," I say. "I'm choosing,
this once, not to manage this into something smaller than it is."

Nadia looks at me for a long moment, then, and something in her face
settles — not relief exactly, but close to it, the specific look of
someone who has said a frightening true thing and found the room still
standing afterward.

"Good," she says, simply, and drinks her tea, and we sit there for a
while longer, in a flat that isn't mine, in a morning that belongs
entirely to the two of us, with nothing on the agenda for once except
each other.

·

We do, eventually, have to leave the flat and rejoin the actual day,
and on the walk to the station Nadia stops, halfway across the humped
bridge, and looks down at the Kelvin running fast and brown below us
after the night's rain.

"I want to tell you something else," she says, "while I've still got
the nerve from this morning left in me. Not the whole of it. Just
enough that you'll understand why some evenings I go quiet in a way
that isn't about you."

"Okay."

"The podcast wasn't just about a breakup, packaged up neatly with a
beginning and an end," she says, carefully, watching the water rather
than me. "It was about someone deciding my actual grief over losing
that relationship was interesting content. Twelve episodes of it,
turned into a story with an arc, a title, a strings-heavy underscore —
and strangers messaging me for months afterward to say how *moved*
they'd been by my pain, like it was a performance I'd given rather than
a thing that had actually happened to me, live, in a life I hadn't
agreed to hand over."

I go very still, because I can hear, underneath the careful phrasing,
the shape of something considerably larger than I'd understood the
podcast to be about, and I don't ask the obvious next question, because
I can see, in the set of her shoulders, that she's given me exactly as
much as she can manage on a bridge on a Tuesday morning.

"Thank you for telling me that much," I say instead.

"That's the whole of it, for today," Nadia says, and there's something
almost apologetic in it, which I hate, immediately, on her behalf.

"You don't owe me the rest on a schedule."

"I know," she says. "I just wanted you to have that piece properly,
rather than guessing at the shape of it from what I don't say." She
looks up from the water, finally, and something in her face has
loosened slightly, the specific relief of a person who's handed over a
weight and found the other person still standing there afterward,
unchanged, willing to keep carrying whatever comes next alongside her.

We walk on to the station without saying anything further about it,
and I hold the new piece of information carefully, the way you'd hold
something breakable you've only just been trusted with, turning it
over gently rather than examining it too hard, grateful, mostly, that
she chose to hand it to me at all.

·

I don't tell anyone what she's told me, not Priya, not Fiona, not
anyone, and the not-telling costs me something, because I've spent this
whole autumn slowly unlearning the habit of carrying things alone. But
this one isn't mine to lighten by sharing. It's hers, on loan to me,
and the least I can do with it is keep it exactly where she put it.

I do notice, though, in the days that follow, a small shift in how I
listen to her — less waiting for the next fact, more simply being
present for whatever she's willing to say, in whatever order it
arrives. When she goes quiet mid-sentence now, I don't fill the gap
with a question. I just let the gap exist, the way she's taught me to
let most gaps exist, and find, more often than not, that she finishes
the sentence herself, eventually, once the silence has done whatever
work silence does that a question never quite manages.

"You've stopped asking follow-up questions," she says, one evening,
noticing the change before I've consciously registered making it.

"Should I be asking them?"

"No," Nadia says. "I just wanted to say I've noticed. It's a good
change. Most people think listening means waiting for their turn to
ask something. You've started actually just listening instead."

"I had a good teacher."

"You did," she agrees, without any false modesty about it, which I've
come to find, oddly, one of the most reassuring things about her —
she takes credit for the things she's actually good at, plainly, the
same way she states every other fact, and somehow that plainness makes
the compliment land more than any amount of deflection would have.

·

I ring Fiona that same evening, mostly to tell her about the record
player and the stiff neck and the whole unremarkable, extraordinary
morning after, and she listens all the way through without once
interrupting, which is, for Fiona, its own small miracle.

"That's the one," she says, when I've finished. "The morning with the
record player. That's the one you'll still be telling people about in
twenty years, long after you've forgotten most of the actual broadcast
segments."

"How do you know that?"

"Because it's the one where nothing happened, and you're still
telling me about it three separate times in one phone call, which
means it mattered more than any of the ones where something did." A
pause, warmer. "Those are always the ones that last, in my experience.
Not the big dramatic nights. The ordinary mornings where you realise,
quietly, that you'd rather be exactly there, doing exactly nothing,
than anywhere else on earth doing anything more impressive."

I don't have anything to add to that, so I let her be right about it,
the way I'm learning to let the people who love me be right about
things more often than I used to, and we talk a while longer about
nothing much at all, the ordinary furniture of two sisters staying
close across a few hundred miles, before ringing off, leaving me
sitting with the whole of that morning a little longer, exactly where
I want it.


## Chapter Sixteen — The Tape

She turns up at my flat with a cassette player from the station's store
cupboard, unasked, because I mentioned the tape once, in passing, weeks
ago, in the specific offhand way I mention things I'm not ready to have
a conversation about, and Nadia — whose love language, it turns out, is
doing the practical thing rather than saying the soft one — has clearly
been carrying that mention around ever since, waiting for the right
evening to put it down.

"You don't have to," I say, looking at it on my kitchen table like it's
live.

"I know," she says. "I brought it anyway."

I play four seconds of my mother's voice. That's all I manage — four
seconds of a woman I haven't heard in years saying something entirely
ordinary about the weather — and then I turn it off, and Nadia doesn't
fill the silence, and doesn't fix it, and doesn't tell me it's alright.
She just sits there, present, for as long as it takes, which turns out
to be considerably longer than four minutes.

"You don't have to be the reliable one in this room," she says,
eventually. "I'm not going anywhere because you cried at a cassette
player."

I don't have a clever answer for that. I don't think one exists. I
just sit with it — with her, in the quiet — and something that has been
held very tightly in my chest for a long time loosens, not all the way,
but enough that I notice the difference.

"Tell me about her," Nadia says, after a while, carefully, in the
voice she uses when she's checking whether a door is actually open or
just unlocked.

So I do. Not the funeral, not the last year, which I've told
approximately nobody in any real detail — but the ordinary things, the
ones that don't hurt in the acute way, only in the low background way
that never quite goes: that she taught me to read a rate card the way
some parents teach fractions, at the kitchen table, with a red pen;
that she used to ring the station every single Tuesday at exactly the
same time, not to check up on me, just because Tuesday was Tuesday
and she liked the routine of it; that the last thing she ever said to
me, in person, not on a tape, was an instruction about the insurance
that I still, six years later, follow to the letter, as though
following it keeps some part of the conversation open.

Nadia doesn't try to make any of it neater than it is. She just
listens, the way she listens to everything, head tilted a few degrees
off centre, and somewhere in the telling I stop performing being
alright and start actually being, briefly, not alright, in front of
another person, on purpose, which — I am realising, as I say it — may
be the single hardest thing I have done in this entire strange autumn.

·

Later that week, a quiet night at the desk, both of us working late for
no practical reason except that neither of us wants to be the one who
leaves first.

The room narrows the way a room does. My hand goes to her jaw, slow,
no plan behind it, and she leans into it before either of us has said
a word, and for one entire second I am certain of exactly what's about
to happen.

Priya's key in the front door lock — badly timed, entirely innocent,
completely unaware of what she's just interrupted — ends it before it
becomes anything more than that.

We both laugh about it, too loudly, too fast, in the specific way of
two people who were about a second from something neither of us has
said out loud yet, and the not-saying, by now, is doing more work than
any single sentence could.

"For the record," Priya says, dropping her bag on the good chair,
entirely oblivious, "I need the desk for twenty minutes, there's an
admin thing." She looks between the two of us, at whatever's left on
our faces, and adds, with the specific weary patience of a teenager
who has clearly decided this is simply how adults behave now, "I'm
going to keep interrupting this, aren't I. Forever. Until you sort it
out."

"Probably," I say.

"Great," Priya says, and sits down at the desk, entirely unbothered,
and neither Nadia nor I says another word about it, though I catch her
eye once, across the room, and something passes between us that isn't
disappointment at all — closer, if anything, to relief, that whatever
this is, it isn't going anywhere, interruption or not.

We end up on the fire escape again, later, once Priya's admin thing
has swallowed the desk properly and there's nowhere else quiet enough
to stand. The rain's stopped for once, which feels, after six weeks of
almost constant weather, like the city taking a breath.

"Do you ever think," I say, "about how much of this we've built out of
places that aren't actually private? A fire escape. A corridor with
buckets in it. A stool that keeps moving six inches at a time."

"I think about it," Nadia says. "I think it's because neither of us
has ever had anywhere private that felt safe enough to use properly.
So we've been building it out of the scraps instead — the bits of the
building nobody else wants."

"That's a very bleak way of putting something quite nice."

"It's not bleak," she says. "It's just accurate. You can build
something real out of scraps. People do it all the time. It's just
slower, and it looks strange from the outside, and you have to trust
the scraps a lot more than you'd have to trust something that started
out whole."

I think about that for the rest of the evening — about scraps, and
what you can build out of them if you're patient and mean it — and
find that I don't mind, particularly, that ours has been built this
way. Some of the sturdiest things I know were never meant to hold this
much weight, and hold it anyway.

·

We start, without ever formally agreeing to it, spending most evenings
together that neither of us is scheduled to work — not always at
either flat, sometimes just walking, down along the river path where
the Kelvin runs fast and shallow over its stones, sometimes as far as
the Botanics, where Nadia turns out to have an entire private
taxonomy of which benches are dry after rain and which only look dry.

"You've mapped this whole park," I say, the third time she steers us
away from a bench that looks, to my eye, perfectly fine.

"I've had a lot of evenings to fill," she says, which I understand,
by now, to be doing a great deal of quiet work underneath its
simplicity — three years of evenings, mostly alone, mapped out street
by street and bench by bench, the specific loneliness of someone who
built herself a whole private geography rather than ask anyone to
share the ordinary kind.

I don't say anything about that. I just remember which benches are
dry, going forward, and start noticing, myself, which ones aren't,
until the mapping becomes something we do together rather than
something she carries alone.

·

Priya, predictably, notices the pattern of these evenings before
either of us has said anything about them out loud, and produces, one
Monday morning, a printed calendar with our unofficial routine
plotted onto it in colour-coded blocks, labelled, with no attempt at
subtlety whatsoever, THINGS THAT AREN'T FOR THE SHOW.

"I've noticed a pattern," she announces, sliding it across the desk.

"Priya."

"I'm not saying anything about the pattern. I'm just documenting it.
For posterity." She taps a Tuesday. "This one's the Botanics. This
one's the chip shop. This one I couldn't work out, so I've just
written QUIET NIGHT, which could mean anything, and I've chosen not to
speculate further, out of professionalism."

"Your professionalism is doing a lot of work today."

"It usually does," Priya says, entirely unbothered, and goes back to
whatever she was doing before, leaving the calendar on the desk where
it stays, pinned up eventually, next to the wall of fame, an
accidental record of a life quietly assembling itself out of ordinary
Tuesdays.

·

We have, by this point, a routine, which is a thing I have never
once in my adult life had with another person outside a work
schedule, and which I find myself guarding, privately, with a
fierceness that surprises me.

Tuesdays are the Botanics, weather permitting, or the good chip shop
if it isn't. Wednesdays are usually quiet — Nadia's longest shift of
the week, and I let her have the evening to herself without treating
the absence of contact as anything other than exactly what it is.
Fridays, after the broadcast, we cook badly together in one flat or
the other, taking it in turns, an arrangement that has produced, so
far, one inedible curry, one entirely acceptable pasta, and one
memorable disaster involving a kitchen timer neither of us thought to
set.

"We're bad at this," I say, surveying the wreckage of the timer
incident, a smoke alarm still ringing faintly somewhere down the hall.

"We're new at this," Nadia corrects, fanning smoke away from the
hob with a tea towel, entirely unbothered by the chaos in a way that
tells me, more than any single conversation could, how much safer she
feels in a burnt kitchen with me than she's felt in a great many
tidier rooms with other people. "Give it time. Everything gets easier
with practice, even the bad cooking."

I believe her. I've started, this month, believing most things she
tells me, on the grounds that she has yet to be wrong about a single
one of them.

·

Priya, inevitably, has opinions about the burnt-dinner incident, having
heard about it secondhand from the smoke alarm's continued and
apparently permanent grudge against the kitchen, which now goes off
whenever anyone so much as toasts bread.

"You've broken the smoke alarm," she announces, the following Monday.
"It's traumatised. It goes off if I so much as look at the grill."

"That's not how smoke alarms work, Priya."

"It's exactly how this one works now. I've named it. It's called
Kevin, after the incident, and Kevin does not trust either of you near
an open flame ever again." She says this with the specific gravity of
someone who has genuinely decided a domestic appliance has developed a
personality disorder as a direct result of witnessing our cooking.

"We'll buy a new alarm."

"You will not replace Kevin. Kevin has been through something. Kevin
deserves better than being discarded the moment he becomes
inconvenient." She pauses, softening slightly. "Also I've noticed you
two are cooking together at all now, regularly, which four months ago
would have seemed about as likely as the transmitter fixing itself, so
I'm choosing to interpret Kevin's trauma as evidence of genuine
progress rather than a problem to solve."

"You've built an entire philosophy around a smoke alarm."

"I contain multitudes," Priya says, echoing, without any apparent
awareness that she's doing it, almost exactly my own line back at me
from weeks earlier, and goes back to her spreadsheet, leaving me
standing in the kitchen wondering, not for the first time, how much of
my own vocabulary has quietly become the building's shared dialect
without my ever noticing the transfer taking place.

·

Sandra, our long-serving widow-caller, drops by the station in person
one Friday afternoon — the first time she's ever visited rather than
simply rung in — bearing a tin of shortbread she insists is "nothing
special, just a thing I do," which turns out, on tasting, to be
considerably better than anything Dev's ever brought in penance.

"I wanted to see the place," she says, looking round at the buckets,
the whiteboard, the wall of fame now three rows deep in printed
messages. "You talk about it every week like it's a person. I wanted
to meet the person properly."

"It's mostly damp and cold tea," I tell her, honestly.

"It's not, though," Sandra says, with the specific firmness of someone
who has clearly rehearsed the correction. "It's the thing that's in my
kitchen every Thursday at seven, keeping me company when the flat's
too quiet to bear on its own. That's not damp and cold tea. That's
considerably more than that, whatever it looks like from where you're
standing."

I don't have a ready answer for that, so I offer her more tea instead,
and she stays the better part of an hour, telling Priya and me stories
about her late husband that have nothing to do with the show at all —
a holiday in Millport gone comically wrong, a wedding-day mishap
involving a borrowed car that wouldn't start — and it occurs to me,
listening, that this is the part of the job Nadia was right about
weeks ago: you rarely see where the work lands, and then, occasionally,
it turns up at your kettle with a tin of shortbread, and reminds you,
plainly, exactly what all of it was ever actually for.

·

Fiona rings that evening, mostly to hear about Sandra's visit, having
apparently decided that the ordinary, unglamorous details of the
station's regulars are, at this point, as interesting to her as
anything happening between me and Nadia directly.

"I like that you've got a whole cast now," she says. "Sandra. Grant.
Priya. That teenager Dev with his apology shortbread. You used to tell
me about the roof and the numbers, mostly, when you rang. Now you tell
me about people."

"I suppose I do."

"It's a good change. It means you're actually living in the place
rather than just managing it from slightly outside." A pause. "I think
that's rather what's happened with Nadia too, if I'm honest. You've
gone from managing a relationship to actually being in one, and it
shows in every story you tell me now, even the ones that aren't
directly about her."

I don't have an immediate response to that, so I let it sit, turning
it over the way I've learned to turn over most of what she says that
lands harder than a simple observation probably should, grateful, in a
way I don't fully examine, for a sister who's watched this whole
transformation from several hundred miles away and somehow still
manages to see it more clearly than I can from directly inside it.


## Chapter Seventeen — Static

"Before anything else happens," Nadia says, very late, in her own flat,
with the record player finally silent, "there's a thing I need you to
know. I need to say it once, and I need you not to make a face about
it while I do."

I put my mug down. "Okay."

"I'm Static."

I don't make a face. I want that on the record, because it costs me
something not to, and I think she can see that it costs me something,
which is, I suspect, exactly why she trusts me enough to keep going.

Static is the station's most-loved segment and has been for two years
— an anonymous late-night voice, unpaid, uncredited, on air between
midnight and one, talking about nothing and everything in a register
so unguarded it's become, without anyone officially naming it, the
actual reason half our overnight listeners tune in at all. I've spent
two years assuming Static was some rotating cast of volunteers, or a
syndicated feed, or — once, embarrassingly, out loud, to Priya — a
retired presenter doing us a favour. I have never once, not for a
second, suspected the woman sitting six feet away from me with her
headphone cup permanently half off.

"How long," is all I manage.

"Two years. Since the podcast." She says it like she's naming a
country she used to live in. "I stopped being a person people talked
about and started being a voice with a desk in front of it. It's the
only shift I've ever felt safe on. Nobody's ever looking at Static.
They're just listening."

I think back through two years of nights I've spent in this building
— the ones I stayed late for no good reason, the ones I told myself I
was just checking the overnight schedule — and try to place Static
against them, and find that I can, more easily than I'd like. The
segment that ran the week after the transmitter nearly failed, gentle
and unhurried, when the whole building had spent the day in crisis.
The one about doors — an entire fifteen minutes on doors, why some
swing open and some you have to pull, why some rooms only ever have
one way out — that I'd listened to twice, alone, in my car outside my
own flat, without once wondering who was behind it.

"I used to think Static sounded like someone who'd been hurt and
decided to be kind about it anyway," I say. "I told Priya that once.
She said I was reading too much into a radio segment."

"You weren't."

"The touring years," I say, carefully, because I know, roughly, the
shape of this now, from the fringe theatre envelope and the four words
about a cable and everything she hasn't said in between. "The
relationship that ended in public."

"The podcast made about it still exists," Nadia says. "I've never once
listened to it all the way through. People send me clips sometimes, by
accident, thinking I'll find them funny. I don't find them funny."

I don't say I'm sorry, because I've learned, by now, that sorry is a
door she doesn't want opened on her behalf. I say the only thing I
actually know to be true.

"I know exactly which nights Static ran," I say, "and which nights you
looked like you hadn't slept. I've known for two years and never once
put the two things together, because I wasn't looking for the pattern.
I'm looking now."

Something moves in her face — not relief, exactly. Recognition, maybe.
The specific look of a person who has just found out the thing they
were most afraid of being seen for has, in fact, already been half-seen,
gently, by someone who didn't make it a story.

·

We don't move from where we're sitting for a long time. When we do,
it's Nadia who says the next thing, in the tone she uses for
everything that actually matters — flat, exact, no room in it for
either of us to mishear.

"I want to say the rest properly," she says. "Not for the segment.
For us."

"Say it."

"This isn't for the panel," I say first, because I want it said before
she has to ask. "I need you to hear that as its own sentence, separate
from everything else I've ever said to you in this building."

"I know," Nadia says. "I wouldn't be here if I thought it was."

We talk, properly, for the first time, about what we both actually
want — not the segment's version, not the six-week version, the real
one. I say the true thing about my own fear, out loud, in full, maybe
for the first time in my adult life: that I am afraid of asking for
something and being told no where anyone could count it, which is
exactly why I have spent a decade making myself so indispensable that
nobody ever has the occasion to say no to me at all.

Nadia says hers back, just as plainly: that she is afraid of being
loved as a bit, a story, a thing people talk about at parties, rather
than as a person anyone actually knows.

"I want this," she says, at the end of it. "All of it. I'm asking you
properly, so there's no version of tonight where either of us gets to
say afterward that it wasn't discussed."

"You have it," I say. "Same back. Properly asked."

"There's one more thing," Nadia says, "and it's smaller, and I want to
say it because I think it matters more than it sounds like it should."

"Go on."

"Ask me again next week. And the week after that. Not because you
think the answer's going to change — because I need to know this
isn't a box you tick once and then assume stays ticked. That's the
thing the podcast took from me. Not the relationship. The ability to
believe that anyone's consent to anything is still active unless
they've said otherwise."

"I'll ask you every week," I say. "For as long as there are weeks."

"That's a big promise for a Tuesday."

"It's not a Tuesday promise," I say. "I've only got the one kind."

She tells me the rest of it, eventually, in pieces, over the course of
the evening, the way people tell you things when they've decided to
trust you but haven't yet worked out how to hand it all over at once.

The relationship itself wasn't the story, she says. That's the part
that took her the longest to accept — that the actual thing between
her and the person the podcast is about was, for two years, ordinary
and good and entirely private, the way relationships are supposed to
be, and that the story only started once it ended, once one half of
it decided the ending was interesting enough to build a following
around. Twelve episodes. A named host, an anonymous "source close to
the situation" who was never, technically, named, though anyone who
knew where to look could work it out inside a week. Nadia never
listened past episode two. She didn't need to. She'd lived episode
two. She knew, roughly, the shape the rest of it would take, because
grief follows a formula whether or not somebody's monetising it.

"The worst part wasn't being talked about," she says. "It was that
people I actually knew — people at work, people I'd toured with for
years — started treating me like a character in something, rather
than a person who'd had a bad year. They'd ask how I was in this
particular tone, this sort of hushed, delighted tone, like they were
checking in on a plot development. I stopped being able to tell the
difference between someone who cared and someone who was just
collecting material."

"Is that why you stopped touring?"

"That's why I stopped touring in front of people who knew who I was,"
Nadia says. "Overnight radio in a basement in Partick is about as far
from that as I could get and still be doing the thing I'm actually
good at. Nobody here was listening to the podcast. Nobody here cared.
I could just be an engineer. It took about eight months before I
stopped flinching every time somebody said my name into a live mic."

I think about the two years I've spent walking past that door,
assuming — because it was easier, because it required nothing of
me — that she simply preferred it quiet in there. I think about how
wrong easy assumptions usually are, when you finally get close enough
to check them.

"Thank you," I say, "for telling me the whole of it. I know it isn't
small."

"It isn't small," Nadia agrees. "But it isn't the only thing about me
either, and I think that's what actually mattered — not that I told
you, but that telling you didn't turn me back into the story again. It
just made me a person you know a bit better than you did this
morning."

"Can I ask one more thing," I say. "About the name. Static. Was that
deliberate, the pun, or did it just arrive the way On the Level did?"

"Deliberate," Nadia says. "Completely deliberate. Static's the sound
between stations — the bit nobody wants, the bit you tune past to get
to something clearer. I liked the idea of being the thing people
usually skip past, except choosing to make it worth staying on. It
felt like the most honest name available for what I actually was for
two years: the noise nobody expected to matter, doing its best to
matter anyway."

"It mattered," I say. "It still does."

"I know that now," Nadia says, and there's something in the way she
says it — quiet, certain, entirely without her usual guardedness —
that tells me this particular evening has done more work in both of
us than either of us has fully reckoned with yet.

We stay up talking long past any sensible hour, the way we did the
first night we admitted anything real to each other, except this
time there's nothing left unsaid between us, no envelope in a drawer,
no reason left to guard. She tells me about the fringe theatre
company properly, this time, the actual work rather than the reasons
to avoid it — a two-hander about grief that toured village halls for
three winters, a lighting rig she rebuilt from scratch out of
spare parts because the budget didn't stretch to a new one, the
specific pride of making something work with almost nothing that she
says she's never quite managed to explain to anyone who hasn't done
it themselves.

"You'd have liked it," she says. "The work, I mean. Not the touring."

"I believe you."

"I mean it. You'd have understood the making-do. That's the whole of
what you do here, isn't it — keeping a building running on almost
nothing, out of sheer refusal to let it stop."

"That's rather generous."

"It's accurate," Nadia says, "which is better than generous," and I
don't argue with that, because by now I've learned there's very
little point arguing with a sentence that's simply true.

Nothing about the rest of that evening is for anyone but the two of
us, which is, in the end, the entire point.

·

I ring Fiona the next morning, mostly because I need to say some of it
out loud to someone who isn't in the room where it happened, and tell
her, in careful, general terms, that Nadia's told me something
significant about her past, and that I'm not going to repeat the
specifics because they aren't mine to repeat, but that it's changed how
I understand nearly everything about her.

"Are you alright?" Fiona asks. "Not her. You. Carrying somebody else's
weight like that is its own kind of work."

"I'm alright. I think I'm honoured, actually, more than anything else.
That she trusted me with it."

"That's the right response," Fiona says, approvingly. "The wrong one
would have been to immediately start trying to fix whatever it is.
I take it you didn't do that."

"I didn't do that."

"Good. You're learning." A pause, warmer this time. "I'm glad you've
found someone who tells you the true, difficult things rather than
just the easy ones. That's rarer than people think, and harder to
build than the easy kind of closeness. I hope you know how lucky that
makes you, having someone willing to do it."

"I know," I say, and mean it more than the two words let on, and we
talk for a while longer about smaller things, the ordinary furniture of
two sisters checking in on each other, before Fiona rings off with her
usual efficiency, leaving me sitting with the whole of the previous
evening a while longer, turning it over gently, grateful for every
piece of it Nadia's chosen, so far, to hand me.

·

Priya, entirely without meaning to, gives me the clearest picture yet
of how much has changed between me and Nadia when she mentions, in
passing, that she's stopped bothering to leave the room when we talk
quietly at the desk.

"I used to give you two space," she says, "back when it still felt
like watching two people rehearse something. I don't do that anymore.
It doesn't feel like a performance to leave space around now. It just
feels like two people talking, the way anyone talks, and there's no
reason for me to make a whole production of tiptoeing out."

"When did you stop noticing the difference?"

"I don't think there's a single moment," Priya says, considering it
properly. "It was more like — one week I was still doing the
tiptoeing thing, out of habit, and the next week I just wasn't, and I
only noticed I'd stopped when I caught myself not doing it. It's a bit
like the automation queue, actually. You don't notice a system's
started running properly on its own. You just notice, one day, that
you've stopped checking it every five minutes."

I think about that comparison for a long time afterward — a fake
relationship becoming so unremarkably, ordinarily real that even the
building's sharpest observer stopped clocking the seam — and find it,
somehow, the most reassuring thing anyone's said about the two of us
in weeks, delivered, as most of the important things in this building
seem to be, by a seventeen-year-old who wasn't even trying to say
anything profound at the time.


## Chapter Eighteen — First, and Real

What happens next belongs to the two of us, and I'd like to keep it
that way, if it's all the same to you.

What I'll say is this: six weeks of performing a couple for an
audience, and then, finally, nobody watching at all. No cue sheet. No
rundown. No fifty-four-minute mark to hit. Just two people who have
run out of reasons to wait, and choose, plainly, in words, not to.

I will say, too, that she asks. Not once, at the start, and then never
again — properly, more than once, in the specific way she said she
would, checking rather than assuming, and that there is something in
being asked, gently and repeatedly, by someone who clearly means the
question each time, that I don't have adequate words for yet and may
never fully manage to.

Morning finds us still there, unhurried, neither of us first up to
leave, which — for two women who have spent a combined seven years
being the first one up in every room they've ever been in — feels like
its own small miracle. Somewhere around seven, with the rain starting
properly on the window and neither of us showing any sign of moving,
Nadia says, into my shoulder, not fully awake, "I keep waiting for you
to have somewhere else to be," and I say, "I don't," and she says,
"good," and that's the whole conversation, and it's the best one
either of us has had in years.

·

Tea, badly made, on a kitchen counter that has never once been
photographed for the show.

"Two more broadcasts," Nadia says, wrapped in a blanket that used to
belong, I gather, to a touring life she's mostly stopped mentioning.
"Then it's just us. We decide what anyone gets told, and when."

"Our timeline," I say. "Not the panel's."

"Not the panel's." She looks, for a second, genuinely happy in a way
I don't think I've seen from her before — not dry, not guarded, just
plainly, unguardedly pleased with the shape of her own life. "Two more
Thursdays and then we tell the truth on our own terms, once nobody can
call it a stunt."

"What do we say," I ask, "when we do tell people. What's the actual
sentence?"

Nadia considers this properly, the way she considers everything.
"The truth," she says. "In whatever order it makes sense to tell it.
Started as a favour, became something else somewhere around the
fourth broadcast, and neither of us noticed until it was too late to
call it anything but real."

"That's a good sentence."

"I've had six weeks to think about it."

"Since when?"

"Since about the second broadcast," Nadia admits, and it's such an
unguarded thing to say, so far from the eleven-words-in-three-years
version of her I first knocked on a glass door for, that I have to put
my mug down for a second before I trust myself to respond to it
properly.

I don't say anything about the fact that dramatic irony is, at this
exact moment, sitting quietly in the room with us, entirely unnoticed
by either party, because I don't yet know it's there. I just drink the
badly made tea, and watch the rain start on the kitchen window, and
let myself, for one whole morning, believe that the hardest part is
already behind us.

"There's a thing I want to ask you," Nadia says, later, once the tea's
gone cold and neither of us has moved to make more. "Not about the
show. About after it."

"Ask."

"When this is over — the six weeks, the panel, all of it — what do
you actually want your life to look like? Not the station's life. Not
the roof's. Yours."

Nobody has asked me that question in years, possibly ever, in those
exact words, and I find, sitting at her kitchen counter with cold tea
in front of me, that I don't have a rehearsed answer, which is itself
remarkable, because I have a rehearsed answer for nearly everything.

"I don't know yet," I say, honestly. "I think I want to find out."

"That's allowed," Nadia says. "You don't have to know already. You
just have to be willing to ask."

I think about that for the rest of the morning, and for a good part of
the following week, and I don't come to any tidy conclusion, which —
for a woman who has spent a decade being uncomfortable with anything
that doesn't resolve neatly by the end of a shift — I count, quietly,
as its own kind of progress.

·

We spend that whole week doing ordinary things, deliberately, as
though testing whether the extraordinary version of us can survive
contact with the mundane. Grocery shopping, once, at the big Tesco on
Byres Road, where Nadia reveals an entirely unexpected and deeply
held opinion about the correct brand of porridge oats that leads to an
argument conducted in hushed, furious whispers in the cereal aisle
and resolved only when we agree to buy both brands and settle it
properly, at home, with a blind taste test that Priya, informed of it
by group chat, insists on adjudicating remotely via video call.

Nadia wins. She always wins these things, in the small print, in the
detail nobody else bothered to check.

"I told you," she says, insufferably pleased, holding up the winning
tub.

"You didn't tell me. You simply were, apparently, correct, which isn't
the same as telling."

"It's the same as telling if you'd been listening properly the first
time."

I don't have a response to that, so I make the porridge her way, and
it is, infuriatingly, better.

·

The two remaining broadcasts, five and six, sit ahead of us that week
like something we're both trying not to look at directly, the way you
avoid looking straight at a deadline that's going to change everything
about how a story gets told. Nadia mentions it once, in the kitchen,
apropos of the rota. "Two more shows and then we tell the truth," she
says, and I catch, for the first time, a note of something underneath
the certainty — not doubt exactly, but the specific caution of someone
who has learned, the hard way, that plans made in a kitchen on a
Tuesday morning don't always survive contact with a live studio.

"You alright?" I ask.

"I'm fine," she says, and then, correcting herself, because we've
both got better at this over six weeks: "I'm nervous. Telling people
on our own terms is still telling people. I've spent two years making
sure nobody got to do that to me again, and I'm about to do it to
myself, on purpose, live, twice."

"We don't have to do it live."

"Yes, we do," Nadia says. "If it's not live, it's not really ours.
It's just another recording someone else could get their hands on."

I don't argue with that, because she's right, the way she is about
almost everything, and because I've learned, by now, that the things
worth doing properly are rarely the comfortable ones.

·

Broadcast five happens, in the end, almost entirely without incident,
which in hindsight I understand as the calm the whole book has been
saving up its weather for. A gentle hour. A caller with a genuinely
funny complaint about a mother-in-law's Sunday roast schedule that
gets resolved, on air, with a compromise nobody in the studio expected
to actually work and that, according to the messages afterward,
apparently did. Nadia and I fall, for the full thirty minutes, into
the easy rhythm we've built over five weeks without either of us once
thinking about the rundown.

Afterward, in the corridor, she says, quietly, "one more."

"One more," I agree.

"And then it's just us."

"And then it's just us," I say back.

·

We spend that evening at my flat, for once rather than hers, and Nadia
looks around it properly for the first time — the good armchair, the
lack of anything on the walls, the specific bareness of a place built
by someone who never expected to need to explain it to a guest.

"You could put something on the walls, you know," she says, turning
slowly on the spot in my living room. "It wouldn't kill the aesthetic.
There is, currently, no aesthetic to kill."

"I never got round to it."

"You've lived here nine years."

"I've been busy."

"You've been avoiding making anything look permanent," Nadia says,
with the specific gentleness she uses for observations she knows will
land somewhere tender, "in case you had to leave it in a hurry. I know
that particular decorating philosophy. I've practised it myself, for
considerably longer than nine years."

I don't argue with her, because she's right, and because I'm slowly
learning that the correct response to her being right isn't defence,
it's just sitting with it until it stops stinging.

"What would you put up," I ask, "if it were your flat?"

She considers the question properly, walking the room once more.
"Something from before. Not a photo — I don't have many of those I'd
want on a wall. Something that just meant a thing, once, to someone,
and still does, even if nobody else looking at it would ever guess
why."

"Like what?"

"I'll show you sometime," she says, which isn't a no, and isn't quite
a yes either, and I let it sit exactly where she's left it, because
I've learned by now that some doors open slowly, on their own
schedule, and pushing on them only ever makes them stick.

Priya, in the meantime, has been quietly rebuilding relationships of
her own — specifically with Dev, whose recovery from food poisoning
has left him with a slightly overcorrected sense of duty toward the
station, and who has taken to arriving early for every single shift,
checking every cable twice, in a way that Priya finds both touching
and, by her own admission, mildly exhausting to manage.

"He's apologising to the building now," she tells me, with the
specific weary fondness of someone describing a friend's endearing but
tiresome habit. "Not to me. Not to you. To the actual building, out
loud, like it's got feelings about the shortbread incident. I told him
it's fine. He said fine isn't the same as forgiven. I said in this
building it usually is. He didn't believe me."

"He'll come round."

"He will," Priya agrees, "once he stops treating every single shift
like a redemption arc. Some of us just want him to check the cable and
go home at a reasonable hour, redemption arc optional."

I laugh at that, properly, and file it away alongside everything else
this small, strange, over-loyal community has taught me this autumn —
that most of the actual work of running a place like this isn't the
roof, or the panel, or even the show, but the steady, unglamorous
business of people forgiving each other for small things quickly, so
that when a large thing eventually comes along, there's enough goodwill
already banked to survive it.

I ring Fiona that same week to tell her, finally, in full, that the six
weeks have turned into something real, and that we're planning to say
so publicly once the last broadcast is done.

"I knew it," Fiona says, with enormous satisfaction. "I knew it before
you did, probably, which I recognise is an obnoxious thing to say, but
I'm going to say it anyway because I've earned the right after years
of watching you not have this."

"You didn't know it. You were worried I was building a surveillance
file on a woman I'd never spoken to."

"That was week one. This is week six. People change their minds when
the evidence changes," Fiona says, entirely unbothered by the
correction. "I'm coming down for the last broadcast, by the way. I've
already booked the train. I want to be in the room, or as close to the
room as they'll let a member of the public get, when you two finally
say it properly, on the record, to everyone."

"That's very sentimental of you."

"I contain multitudes," Fiona says, echoing a phrase she's clearly
picked up secondhand from somewhere in my own retelling of this whole
autumn, and rings off before I can ask her where she got it, leaving me
smiling at my phone in a way that's become, this month, something of a
theme.

Priya, told the news properly rather than via the group chat this
time, insists on marking it with a hand-drawn certificate, presented
with enormous ceremony in the kitchen, that reads, in full: THIS
CERTIFIES THAT ROBYN AND NADIA ARE, AS OF THIS WEEK, ACTUALLY REAL,
WITNESSED BY PRIYA (AGE 17, WHO KNEW ALL ALONG).

"You didn't know all along," I say. "You suspected. There's a
difference."

"I knew," Priya says, with the specific unshakeable confidence of a
teenager rewriting her own history in real time, "from approximately
the third broadcast, and I would like that documented for posterity,
alongside this certificate, which I have laminated, because I take
my documentation seriously."

"You laminated it."

"I laminated it at the library, on my lunch break, because some
occasions deserve permanence." She hands it over with genuine, if
slightly overwrought, solemnity, and I take it, and it goes, eventually,
onto the fridge in my own flat, next to nothing else at all, the first
thing I've put up on a wall in nine years that I didn't immediately
regret making permanent.

We fall asleep that night with the radio on, low, tuned to nothing in
particular, just the specific hush of a station between programmes,
and it is, I think afterward, the most ordinary and the most
extraordinary night of the entire six weeks — nothing happening in it
at all except two people who have run out of reasons to be anywhere
else, and neither of us has the faintest idea, falling asleep to
static and rain, how completely wrong that particular piece of
optimism is about to turn out to be.


## Chapter Nineteen — Hot Mic

Here is how it happens, because it matters, afterward, that it happens
exactly this plainly and this stupidly and not in any of the ways
either of us would have braced for.

Priya is updating the automation queue on the Wednesday afternoon —
the ordinary admin that keeps the overnight feed running itself between
midnight and six, stings and idents and the archive loop nobody
listens to — and she drags the wrong file into the wrong slot. That's
the whole of it. No malice, no carelessness even, really, just the
single most ordinary kind of accident there is: a folder with two
similarly named files in it, a Tuesday afternoon, a seventeen-year-old
trusted with more of this building than anyone her age should
reasonably be trusted with, doing the job the way she always does it,
and getting, this once, unlucky.

The file that airs is a recording made weeks ago in the tech cupboard,
while Nadia was testing a new mic, never meant to leave the machine it
was made on. It has the two of us on it, talking — really talking,
not for anyone, about the podcast, about being loved as a bit rather
than as a person, about things Nadia has never once said to another
living soul.

It plays out over the automated overnight feed at half past midnight,
with a timestamp on it, to however many people happen to be awake and
listening.

·

I find out at seven in the morning, from Priya, who is crying before
she's finished the first sentence, which tells me everything I need to
know about how badly this has already gone before I've heard a single
detail.

"I dragged the wrong file," she says. "I checked the name, I swear I
checked the name, and it was—" She can't finish it. She doesn't have
to. I've already opened the automation log myself, and I can see
exactly which file went out, and exactly how long it ran, and exactly
how many listens it's had before anyone thought to pull it.

"How many people were listening at half past midnight," I ask, mostly
because I need a number to hold onto, something with edges, before I
can deal with anything that doesn't.

"Forty-one," Priya says. "That's what the log says. Forty-one people."

Forty-one people is nothing, in the scheme of things — less than a
busy Thursday broadcast, less than a tenth of what On the Level pulls
in on a good week. It is also, I understand immediately and
completely, not nothing at all, because it is forty-one people who
heard something Nadia has spent two years making sure nobody heard,
and the number of listeners has never once, in the whole history of
this, been the actual measure of the damage.

Priya is still talking, in the specific rapid, unstoppable way people
talk when they're trying to outrun their own guilt with words. "I've
already pulled it from the loop, it can't play again, I checked the
file three times before I put it in and I don't know how I got it
wrong, I've done that queue a hundred times, I don't understand how
I—"

"Priya." I make myself say it gently, because whatever else this
morning is, it is not her fault in any way that deserves this much of
her crying alone in a corridor about it. "It's an accident. I need you
to hear that from me, clearly, before anything else happens today.
You didn't do this on purpose. Nobody thinks you did this on purpose."

"Nadia might."

"Nadia won't," I say, with a confidence I don't, in this exact moment,
actually have.

I call Nadia. She doesn't answer. I call again. She doesn't answer
that either, and by the third call I am standing in the office with my
hand shaking in a way I don't remember deciding to let happen.

I find her at the overnight studio door, which is shut, in a way I've
never once seen it shut during her actual shift, not even on the
worst night either of us has had.

"Nadia."

Nothing.

"Nadia, I know what happened. Priya's told me. It was a mistake, it
was an accident, nobody did this to you on—"

"It doesn't matter that it was an accident."

Her voice, through the door, is the flattest I have ever heard it — not
the professional flat, the working flat, the flat she uses to say true
things. This is a different flat entirely. This is the sound of
someone who has gone somewhere I can't follow her to yet.

"I told you," she says. "The podcast. Being a story instead of a
person. I told you what that costs, and I told you I needed it to stay
off the record, and it didn't. It's out there. With a timestamp. With
my actual voice on it, saying actual things, and I don't get to decide
who heard it or what they do with it, and that's — that's the entire
thing, Robyn. That's the exact thing."

"I promised you—"

"I know what you promised me," she says, and for the first time there
is something in her voice besides flatness, something that sounds,
horribly, like it might be the beginning of tears she is not going to
let me hear finish. "I know it wasn't you. I know it was an accident. I
believe every single word of that, and none of it changes what's
sitting on the internet right now with a timestamp on it."

I don't have an answer for that, because there isn't one. There is no
sentence available to me that makes an accident stop being true.

"Can I do anything," I ask, which is, I recognise even as I'm saying
it, the single most useless question available, the caretaker's
reflex firing uselessly against a door that has nothing behind it for
me to fix.

"No," Nadia says. Not cruel. Just accurate. "That's the whole of it,
Robyn. There isn't a thing to do. That's what this is. You can't do
anything, and I can't undo forty-one people having heard my actual
voice say actual things, and neither of those facts is going to change
no matter how long either of us stands on either side of this door."

I stand there anyway, for a long time, because leaving feels like a
second kind of abandonment on top of the first, even though staying
accomplishes precisely nothing that she's just told me, plainly, it
would accomplish. Eventually the silence on the other side of the
door goes on long enough that I understand she isn't coming out, not
this morning, maybe not today at all, and I make myself walk away
from a door I have never once, in six years, walked away from while
someone was still upset on the other side of it.

·

Within the hour, the panel's office calls.

"Manufactured content becoming a liability," is the phrase the admin
uses, reading it, I suspect, off a prepared line someone more senior
gave her. "The panel will need to reconsider the renewal in light of—"

I don't hear the rest of the sentence properly. I'm still standing
outside a shut studio door that has not opened once, not for me, not
for Priya, not for anyone, and somewhere behind it is a woman whose
worst and most specific fear I have just watched come true, by
accident, through nobody's malice, which turns out to make it no
easier to bear at all.

·

I spend the rest of that day doing the only thing I know how to do
under pressure, which is manage the parts of the catastrophe that will
hold still long enough to be managed.

I call the panel's admin back and get, for my trouble, twenty minutes
of hold music and then a further conversation with Douglas himself,
who uses the phrase *optics* four times in ninety seconds and never
once asks how Nadia is. I draft a statement for the station's own
social accounts and delete it three times, because every version
reads like an institution protecting itself rather than a workplace
that has genuinely hurt somebody, and I am not willing to put my name
to that, whatever it costs the renewal.

I check the automation logs again, properly this time, all the way
through, and confirm what I already suspected: the file sat in a
folder with eleven other similarly named recordings, all of them test
files from the mic upgrade in July, none of them ever meant for
broadcast, an entire folder that should have been archived and deleted
weeks ago and wasn't, because archiving old test files was a task on
a list that, like most such tasks, kept losing to more urgent things.
It is, in the end, my failure as much as Priya's — a system I should
have had properly tidied months before a seventeen-year-old ever had
to touch it under pressure.

I find Priya in the kitchen, still red-eyed, staring at a mug of tea
she hasn't drunk.

"It was an old test file," I tell her. "It should have been deleted in
July. That it wasn't isn't your fault, it's mine — I run this
building, and the folder structure was mine to fix, and I didn't fix
it, because I was too busy fixing everything more visible instead."

"That doesn't make me feel better."

"It isn't supposed to make either of us feel better," I say. "It's
supposed to be accurate. Feeling better can come later, once we've
actually said the true thing about who let this happen and how."

Priya doesn't say anything for a while. Then: "Is she going to be
alright?"

"I don't know," I say, which is the truest thing I've said all day,
and the hardest one to leave sitting in a kitchen with a seventeen-
year-old who wants, more than anything, to be told it will all be
fine.

·

I go back to the overnight studio door twice more that day, and both
times leave without knocking, because there is a version of persistence
that becomes its own kind of pressure, and I am, for the first time in
my adult life, genuinely trying to tell the difference before I cross
it.

The clip, meanwhile, does the specific ugly thing recordings do once
they're loose in the world. Somebody's cut it down to ninety seconds
and shared it in a Glasgow community group, with a caption that reads,
with the particular cruelty of people who mistake curiosity for
entitlement, *is this the real story behind Kelvin Row's "couple"?*
By evening it's had several hundred views, which is nothing by the
standards of the actual internet and everything by the standards of a
woman who has spent two years building a life specifically designed to
never again be several hundred people's evening's entertainment.

I don't share any of this with Nadia directly — she'll have seen it
herself, I have no doubt, and the last thing she needs from me is a
running commentary on exactly how bad it's got. I do, however, spend
an hour I don't really have drafting a polite, entirely reasonable
message to the group's admin, asking that the clip be taken down out
of basic respect for a private individual, and am mildly astonished
when it actually works within the hour, a small, unglamorous act of
internet housekeeping that does nothing to undo what's already been
heard but at least stops the number from climbing any further.

It's not a fix. Nothing about that day has a fix. But it's something,
and something, on a day with nothing else available, turns out to
matter more than I'd have guessed.

·

I don't sleep at all that night, and around two in the morning I give
up pretending I might, and sit at the kitchen table with a notebook,
doing the thing I do when a problem refuses to resolve, which is
write down everything I actually know rather than everything I'm
afraid of.

What I know: it was an accident. What I know: Nadia's fear was never
about accidents specifically, it was about control — about who gets
to decide when her voice becomes public and on what terms — and an
accident is, in its own way, the purest possible violation of that,
worse in some respects than malice, because there's no one to be
angry at, nothing to push against, just a fact that has already
happened and cannot be made to un-happen by any amount of apologising.

What I know: I cannot fix this by managing it. What I know: I am
going to want to try anyway, repeatedly, for days, because managing
things is the only language I have ever been fluent in, and learning
a new one under this much pressure is going to be slow and clumsy and
I am going to get it wrong more than once before I get it right.

I write all of that down, in full, at two in the morning, and it
doesn't make the night any shorter, but it does give me something to
hold onto besides the specific helplessness of standing outside a
closed door with nothing useful to offer the person behind it.

·

Fiona rings at seven, having seen some version of the clip herself —
it's reached Aberdeen too, apparently, in the specific way bad news
travels faster and further than good news ever manages — and doesn't
waste time on preamble.

"How bad," she says.

"Bad."

"Is she going to be alright?"

"I don't know," I say, the same honest, unmanaged answer I'll give
Priya a few hours later, because it's the only true one currently
available to me. "I don't think 'alright' is even the right question
yet. Right now it's just about getting through today without making
it worse."

"What do you need from me?"

"Nothing, actually. I just needed to say it out loud to someone who
isn't in the building, before I have to go back in and be steady for
everyone who is."

"Then say it," Fiona says, gently. "I'm listening. Take as long as you
need."

I do, in the end, say more of it than I'd planned to — the automation
log, the forty-one listeners, the specific flatness in Nadia's voice
through a shut door — and Fiona listens the whole way through without
once trying to fix any of it, which I understand, now, having spent two
months learning the difference from a woman with headphone cups and a
level meter, to be the single most useful thing anyone can offer
another person on a morning like this one.

"I love you," Fiona says, at the end of it. "Go back in. Be steady.
Ring me tonight, whatever's happened by then."

"I will."

I put the phone down and sit for a moment longer in the grey early
light, gathering whatever version of steady I can currently manage,
before going back out to a building that, today more than any other
day since I've run it, needs every scrap of it I've got.


## Chapter Twenty — The Door Stays Shut

Three days of a closed studio door.

Nadia does her shifts — she doesn't miss a single one, which I
understand, eventually, is its own kind of statement, a woman making
absolutely certain that whatever else is broken, her actual job is not
one of the casualties — but she does them with both headphone cups
sealed for the first time since I've known her, and texts read and not
answered, and a version of professional distance so complete it makes
the first week of this whole arrangement look intimate by comparison.

The theatre contract, I notice, is no longer quite so unopened.

I try every register I own. Apology — three different versions,
increasingly specific, all of them, in retrospect, doing exactly what
she's spent six weeks asking me not to do, which is manage the
situation instead of simply sitting in it. Explanation, which lands as
a worse kind of apology. Silence, which I try for one entire day and
find I am constitutionally incapable of sustaining. More apology.

Every single one of them lands as management. Every single one of them
is, structurally, the precise thing she asked me, at the very start,
never to turn this into.

Priya, for her part, is barely functioning. She offers to resign twice
in the first two days, both times in the specific dramatic register of
a seventeen-year-old who has never before been the cause of a real
adult catastrophe and has, as a result, no proportionate way to hold
the feeling. I refuse both times. The second time, I sit her down
properly and tell her the thing I actually believe, which is that a
station run entirely without the possibility of human error isn't a
station, it's a machine, and machines don't run community radio, and
the day I start firing seventeen-year-olds for automation mistakes is
the day this place stops being worth saving in the first place.

She doesn't fully believe me. I'm not sure I fully believe myself. But
she stops offering to resign, and starts, instead, quietly rebuilding
the entire automation system from scratch in her spare time, a
project nobody's asked her to undertake and that I don't have the
heart to tell her isn't strictly necessary.

"I'm not asking you to fix it," Nadia says, once, through a half-open
door, the only time in three days she opens it even that far. "I'm
asking you to understand that you can't."

I don't have an answer for that either. I go home, and I do the thing
I have spent my entire adult life doing instead of grieving anything
properly, which is make myself indispensable to a problem that, this
time, does not want fixing.

·

Kirsty finds me at a coffee shop near the Chambers, off the clock,
in a coat instead of a blazer, which I notice before she's said a
single word.

"I heard," she says, sitting down without asking. "The whole panel's
heard. I wanted to say something before it comes up in a meeting where
it'll sound like policy instead of like a person talking to another
person."

"Go on, then."

"Douglas is already drafting language about *reputational exposure*,"
Kirsty says, with the specific distaste of someone who has spent eight
years learning to speak that language fluently and has never once
stopped finding it repellent. "I want you to know that before you walk
into whatever room he eventually calls this in. He's not thinking
about Nadia. He's thinking about a line in next year's funding
application."

"That's reassuring."

"It's not meant to be reassuring. It's meant to be useful." She
wraps both hands round her cup, finally, without drinking from it.
"There's a version of the next few weeks where you spend all your
energy managing Douglas's language and none of it on the actual
person this happened to. I've watched you do exactly that for six
years, over smaller things. I don't think you can afford to do it over
this one."

"I don't know how to do anything else," I say, and it comes out more
honestly than I mean it to. "Managing it is the only tool I've got."

"It isn't, though," Kirsty says. "It's just the one you reach for
first. There's a difference, and I think you know there's a
difference, because you wouldn't be sitting across from me looking
this wretched if some part of you hadn't already worked out that
managing it isn't going to be enough this time."

"I left breakfast radio eight years ago," she goes on, "and I've spent
every one of those eight years telling myself I made the ambitious
choice. National reach. Bigger platform. All the things you say when
someone asks why you left." She turns her cup a quarter-turn on the
saucer. "I made the safe choice, Robyn, and I
called it ambition, because staying meant being seen failing at it in
public, on a local station, in front of people who'd known me for
years. Leaving let me fail privately instead, somewhere nobody I grew
up next to would ever hear about it."

I don't say anything. I'm not entirely sure, yet, what this has to do
with me, or with the closed door I've spent three days standing
outside of.

"You're the one who stayed," Kirsty says, "and did the hard, visible,
unglamorous thing, in front of everyone, for six years, and I have
never once said that to you properly. I'm saying it now. Late. Over
coffee. Because it's easier than saying it in a meeting."

It isn't absolution. I don't think it's really about Nadia at all,
strictly speaking. But it hands me something I didn't have this
morning — the actual, specific distinction between performing safety
and choosing something, out loud, where it can be refused, which is
precisely the choice I've been failing to make in front of a closed
studio door for three days.

I leave the coffee shop knowing exactly what I have to do next, and
still, entirely, not knowing whether it will work.

"There's one more thing," Kirsty says, catching my sleeve as I stand
to go, in a gesture so unlike the professionally warm distance she's
kept for eight years that I actually stop moving. "For what it's
worth — whatever happens with the renewal, whatever Douglas ends up
saying in whatever meeting he calls this in — I'll argue for the
show. Properly. Not because it's good optics. Because I've listened
to it, every week, and it's the best thing on this station's schedule
in a decade, and I'm not going to sit in a room and let anyone call
that a liability without saying so."

"Thank you," I say, and mean it more than the two words let on.

"Don't thank me yet. Douglas is going to be extremely tedious about
it first."

"I'd expect nothing less."

She almost smiles at that — the first almost-smile I've had from her
in eight years that isn't professionally calibrated for a room full of
other people — and lets me go, and I walk back toward the station with
something in my chest that isn't quite hope, not yet, but is at least
no longer only dread.

·

Priya is waiting for me at the bottom of the basement stairs when I
get back, sitting on the second step from the bottom in a way that
tells me, before she says anything, that she's been rehearsing this
conversation for a while.

"I want to apologise properly," she says, standing up. "Not the
resigning kind. The actual kind, where I say the specific thing I did
wrong and don't make you talk me out of feeling bad about it."

"Okay."

"I got complacent," Priya says. "I've done that queue a hundred times
and I stopped checking it as carefully as I should have, because it'd
never gone wrong before, and I told myself that meant it couldn't. I
was wrong. I'm sorry. Not just for the mistake — for how long it took
me to actually look at why I made it instead of just how bad I felt
about it."

It is, I think, standing on a basement staircase with a seventeen-
year-old who has clearly done more genuine reflection on her own
error in three days than most adults manage in a year, one of the
more remarkable things anyone has said to me this whole strange
autumn.

"Thank you," I say. "That's the actual apology. I accept it."

"Is Nadia going to accept it too?"

"I don't know yet," I say, honestly. "But I think she'll hear it, when
you're ready to say it to her directly, and I think that'll matter
more than anything I could say on your behalf."

Priya does go to her, in the end, on the second day, standing outside
the same closed studio door I've been failing to talk my way through
for two days running.

She tells me about it afterward, in the kitchen, in a much quieter
voice than her usual one. "I said the thing I said to you. The whole
of it. She didn't open the door either. But she said, through it,
'thank you for telling me properly instead of just feeling bad about
it where I couldn't hear you,' and I think that's the most an apology
can actually do, isn't it. Just be heard, even through a door."

"That's exactly what an apology can do," I say. "It's more than most
people manage."

"Do you think she'll forgive me?"

"I think she already has," I say, "in the sense that matters. She's
not angry at you, Priya. She's frightened, and hurt, and those aren't
the same thing as angry, even though they can look the same from the
outside for a while."

Priya nods, slowly, taking that in with the specific gravity of
someone filing away a distinction she suspects she'll need again
someday, and goes back to the automation queue, quieter than usual,
but steadier too, in a way that tells me the apology did exactly what
it needed to do, for both of them.

·

Fiona rings again that evening, checking in properly this time, having
heard from me only in fragments over the previous three days.

"You sound tired," she says.

"I am tired. Three days of a closed door will do that."

"Is she talking to you yet?"

"A little. Through the door, mostly. She let it open a few inches
yesterday, which felt like a genuine landmark at the time, and today
it's shut again, which I'm trying very hard not to read as a step
backward, because I don't think it is one. I think grief doesn't move
in a straight line, and I think I'm only just starting to actually
believe that, rather than just knowing it as a fact."

"You've never been good at things that don't move in a straight line,"
Fiona says, not unkindly. "You like problems with a fix at the end of
them. A roof you can patch. A budget you can balance. This one doesn't
have a fix, and I think that's the actual test in front of you right
now — not whether you can solve it, but whether you can stay standing
next to it without needing to."

"I'm trying."

"I know you are. That's all anyone can ask of you right now, including
her, whether or not she's able to say so from behind a door this
week." A pause, warmer. "You'll get it right eventually. Not perfectly.
Eventually. That's usually enough."

I sit with that for a long time after we hang up, in a flat that feels
quieter than usual without a single word from the other side of a
studio door, and let myself believe, for the length of one phone call
at least, that eventually might actually be close enough to console
myself with tonight.

·

Dev turns up on the third day, unprompted, with a box of shortbread
that has, by now, become something of a running joke about his
particular method of processing guilt, except this time it isn't his
own guilt he's trying to atone for.

"I heard what happened," he says, setting the box down carefully on
the kitchen counter. "I don't really know Nadia well. I know you,
though, a bit, from four years of shifts, and I know you're the kind
of person who'll try to carry all of this yourself rather than let
anyone help, so I brought biscuits, because it's the only language I've
got for *I'm thinking about you and I don't know what else to do*."

"You didn't have to."

"I know I didn't. I wanted to." He hovers for a moment, clearly
working up to something. "For what it's worth — and I recognise I'm
not really in a position to have deep insight into your relationship —
but the version of you I've watched running this place for four years
is somebody who fixes things. I just wanted to say, in case it's
useful, that maybe this one isn't a fixing problem. Maybe it's a
staying problem. Just — being there, steadily, without trying to solve
it. I don't know if that helps."

It helps considerably more than he probably realises, and I tell him
so, and he goes slightly pink with the specific embarrassment of a man
who has said something genuinely wise and doesn't quite know what to do
with the fact of it landing.

"I'm not generally good at the wise thing," he says. "I mostly just
bring biscuits."

"The biscuits help too."

"Good," Dev says, visibly relieved to have discharged his duty, and
leaves the shortbread on the counter, and goes back to his shift,
leaving me with a box of biscuits and a sentence I turn over for the
rest of the evening — *maybe this one isn't a fixing problem* — that
turns out to be exactly the reframe I needed to hear from someone
standing far enough outside the situation to say it plainly.


## Chapter Twenty-One — What She's Not Rehearsing

I go to her flat with nothing rehearsed, for the first time in the
whole of this — no cue sheet, no format, no line I've managed into
shape on the walk over.

That's not quite true, if I'm honest with myself, which I am trying
very hard to be at this specific stage of the story. I did try to
rehearse it. I got as far as the second sentence, three separate
times, on the walk from the subway, and abandoned each attempt somewhere
around the bit where I would have started explaining myself, because
I have finally, painfully, started to understand that explaining
myself is not the same thing as being honest, and that the two have
been getting confused with each other in my head for the better part
of a decade.

So I knock, and I stand there with nothing prepared, which feels, if
I'm being accurate about it, like standing somewhere very high up
without a railing.

The door doesn't open. I can hear her on the other side of it,
though — not moving, just present, the specific quality of quiet that
means someone hasn't walked away.

"I can't fix what happened to you," I say, through the wood, because
I've learned by now not to assume I've earned the right to say it to
her face. "I'm not going to try. I know trying would just be the same
thing I've done every other time, dressed up as an apology. I'm asking
for one thing. Thursday. Not for the panel. It's the last scheduled
show, and I would like it to be honest, and I can't make it honest
without you, and I know that's not a fair thing to ask of someone I've
just watched get hurt because of me."

Silence, for long enough that I start composing, in my head, the
version of this evening where I go home having said it into an empty
stairwell, into a door that never opens, into a Thursday that never
comes.

"You'd be asking me to go back on air," Nadia says, eventually, "after
the last time I was on a recording that got out without my say-so cost
me two years of my life."

"I know."

"That's not a small thing to ask."

"I know that too. I'm asking anyway, because I've run out of things to
offer you that aren't just more careful versions of the same
management I've been doing since the day I met you, and I'd rather
ask for something real and get told no than keep offering you
apologies you've already told me you don't want."

Another silence, shorter this time.

"Why does it have to be Thursday," Nadia says. "Why not just — let it
end. Quietly. The renewal's probably gone either way. Nobody would
blame either of us for just letting the whole thing lapse."

"Because if we let it lapse," I say, "the last thing anyone ever hears
of On the Level is an accident. Forty-one people at half past midnight
and a recording neither of us chose to make public. That's not the
ending. That's just where the accident happened to stop. I don't think
either of us gets to decide anything real again until we've put
something real back in its place, on purpose, where the accident was."

"That's a very tidy argument."

"I know. I've had three days to build it. I'm aware it might not be
enough."

"I'll think about it."

That's the whole answer. Flat, through the wood, no warmth in it that
I can detect. But it's the first uncertain yes this entire book has
had, and I walk home in the rain not knowing — which, for a woman who
has spent a decade managing every outcome down to a known result, is,
I am slowly coming to understand, its own small and terrifying kind
of growth.

I don't call Priya to tell her how it went. I don't call anyone. I
just walk, in the rain, past the chip shop with its shutters half up
for the evening trade, over the humped bridge where the Kelvin is
running high and fast after three days of weather, and let the not-
knowing sit in my chest the whole way home, uncomfortable and
entirely unmanaged, which is, I suspect, exactly what it's supposed to
feel like.

·

Wednesday passes with no word. I don't text. I don't call. I go to the
station and run the ordinary business of the place — a broken cart
machine, a volunteer's holiday request, a conversation with Priya
about the rebuilt automation queue that she's finished a full week
early and is, understandably, enormously proud of — and let the
silence from Nadia's direction simply exist, without trying to fill
it or manage it or fix it into something more comfortable.

It is, I think, the hardest single day of self-restraint I have
managed in six years of running this building, and nobody watching
would have any idea, from the outside, that anything unusual was
happening at all. I answer emails. I check the roof fund. I make tea
for a volunteer who's had a difficult week and doesn't especially want
to talk about it, and let him not talk about it, because I am, it
turns out, actually rather good at that particular skill once I stop
trying to apply it to myself.

Thursday morning, at ten to six, my phone buzzes once. *I'll do it.
Six fifty-eight, same as always. Don't make a thing of it when I get
there.*

I don't make a thing of it. I sit with the message for a long moment,
in the grey half-light of a Thursday that is about to become, one way
or another, the most important broadcast this station has ever put
out, and then I get up, and get dressed, and go to work.

I text Priya on the way in, the only person who needs to know before
the desk needs to know. *She's coming. Six fifty-eight. Whatever
happens tonight, I need you steady on the automation queue, no
surprises, nothing off script that we haven't planned for.*

*Understood*, Priya writes back, and then, a moment later: *I've
already triple-checked everything twice. I'm not letting anything go
wrong tonight. Not this one.*

I believe her completely, which is itself new — six weeks ago I'd have
checked the queue myself regardless of what she told me, out of the
same reflex that's run this building for nine years. This morning I
just put the phone away, and trust her, and keep walking.

I ring Kirsty, too, before I reach the station, because I've decided,
somewhere in the small hours, that if tonight is going to be honest,
it should be honest with the people who might be able to help as well
as the ones who might be hurt by it.

"I need a favour," I say, "and I need you not to ask why until
afterward."

"Go on."

"If tonight's broadcast goes the way I think it might, the panel's
going to hear about it fast, and I'd like someone in that room who
understands it wasn't a stunt before Douglas gets to frame it as one."

There's a pause, brief, and then: "I'll be listening. Live, from home,
not from the office. And if it's what I think it's going to be, I'll
say so to whoever needs to hear it, first thing tomorrow."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet," Kirsty says, echoing, without knowing it, almost
exactly what she said to me over coffee weeks ago. "Just do the thing
properly. I'll handle my end of it."

I hang up feeling, for the first time all week, that the odds might
not be quite so stacked against us as they've felt every single day
since the automation queue picked the wrong file.

·

Fiona rings that same afternoon, having heard some garbled version of
events through the group chat's ever-widening circle, and doesn't ask
me to explain the whole of it, which I'm grateful for, because I don't
currently have the words to make it make sense to someone who hasn't
lived every hour of the last three days.

"I just want you to know I'm here," she says, "whichever way tonight
goes. You don't have to manage me through this one either. I just
wanted to say it before it happens, in case there isn't a good moment
after."

"Thank you."

"Is she going to be alright? Really?"

"I don't know," I say, which is the only honest answer I've had all
week for that particular question, and Fiona, for once, doesn't push
for a more comfortable one.

"You've done the right thing, asking rather than managing," she says,
eventually. "I know it doesn't feel like enough. It rarely does, when
it's the right thing rather than the easy one. But it is the right
thing, and I wanted you to hear that from someone who isn't currently
standing in the middle of it with you."

I sit with that for a while after we hang up, in the office, with the
amber bulb glowing faintly through the wall, and let myself feel,
properly, for the first time in days, how frightened I actually am —
not managed-frightened, not filed-away-for-later frightened, just
plainly, uncomfortably afraid of how tonight might go, and of how
little control I actually have over the outcome, however carefully
I've built the case for it.

It doesn't fix anything. But it does, somehow, make the fear easier to
carry into the evening than it would have been left unexamined,
which is, I am slowly learning, most of what feeling a thing properly
is actually for.

·

Priya finds me still sitting there twenty minutes later and doesn't
say anything at first, just puts the kettle on, its usual four-minute
cycle ticking over in the background while neither of us fills the
silence with anything at all.

"You're allowed to be frightened too, you know," she says, eventually,
handing me a mug I don't remember asking for. "Everyone keeps checking
on Nadia. Rightly. But you've been managing a catastrophe for four days
straight and nobody's asked you how you're actually doing, including
me, until right now."

"I'm alright."

"You're not, but I appreciate you saying so anyway." She sits on the
edge of the desk, mug in hand, in the specific posture of someone
settling in for a conversation she's decided is necessary regardless of
whether the other person wants to have it. "You've built this entire
building out of being the person who's alright so everyone else doesn't
have to worry about you. I've watched you do it for four years. I just
want you to know that some of us have noticed, and some of us don't
actually need you to be alright all the time for the building to keep
standing."

"When did you get so wise, Priya?"

"I've always been this wise," she says, with enormous, entirely
justified confidence. "You've just been too busy managing everyone
else's crises to properly notice." She softens, slightly. "Tonight's
going to be hard, whichever way it goes. I need you to actually let it
be hard, rather than managing your way past the hard part before
you've let yourself feel any of it. That's the only thing I'm asking."

I don't have a ready answer for that, so I just drink the tea she's
made me, and let the kettle's quiet click as it finishes stand in for
whatever words I don't currently have, and am grateful, not for the
first time this autumn, for a seventeen-year-old who sees rather more
of me than I generally let anyone see.

·

I ring Fiona once more before the evening's out, mostly because I've
promised her I would, and mostly because saying it all out loud twice
in one day seems to be doing more good than saying it once.

"She said yes," I tell her. "Or something close enough to a yes that
I'm choosing to treat it as one."

"How do you feel?"

"Terrified. Hopeful. Both, constantly, in a way that doesn't seem to
resolve into just one or the other no matter how long I sit with it."

"That sounds about right, for something that actually matters," Fiona
says. "The stuff that doesn't matter resolves easily. The stuff that
does tends to just sit there being both things at once until it's
over, one way or the other."

"That's not very comforting."

"I'm not trying to comfort you. I'm trying to be accurate," Fiona
says, and I recognise the phrase immediately as pure Nadia, filtered
somehow through my sister after two months of secondhand reports, as
though the whole of my life has quietly started running on the same
vocabulary from every direction at once. "Go to bed. Sleep if you can.
Tomorrow's going to need whatever you've got left in the tank, and
none of it's going to arrive by staying up worrying about the version
of tonight that hasn't happened yet."

I do try to sleep. I manage perhaps two hours of it, in patches,
before the grey light starts at the edges of the curtains and there's
nothing left to do except get up and go and find out, at last, which
version of tomorrow we're actually going to get.

·

I think, in the last stretch of dark before I finally give up on
sleep entirely, about everything this building has taught me across
nine years, and about how little of it, until this particular autumn,
ever touched the parts of me that actually needed teaching.

I learned how to source a transmitter part from a decommissioned
hospital in Dundee. I learned how to write a letter that changes a
councillor's mind. I learned, at nineteen, how to be the reliable one,
and I got so good at it that reliability quietly became the entire
architecture of my adult life, load-bearing and mostly invisible, the
way the best-built things in an old building tend to be, right up
until the day something tests them properly for the first time.

Tonight is testing it properly. Not the roof. Not the panel. A woman
behind a door who has told me, plainly, that she can't be fixed, only
accompanied, and a version of myself that has spent a decade equating
love with usefulness, now being asked to prove it knows the
difference.

I don't know, lying in the grey half-light, whether I've actually
learned the difference in time, or whether tonight is simply the exam
I've been unconsciously revising for across this entire strange
autumn, without ever quite realising that's what all of it was for. I
get up, eventually, without an answer to that either, and go to find
out the only way there ever is: by showing up, properly, and doing the
thing.


## Chapter Twenty-Two — On the Level (Broadcast Six)

She's at the desk at six fifty-eight, headphones on, both cups on this
time, which I clock the second I walk in and don't trust myself to say
anything about.

"We're live in ninety seconds," Nadia says. "Get to the desk."

I get to the desk. I have a rundown in my hand that I wrote three days
ago, in a version of this evening where everything had gone according
to plan, and I know, looking at her, that we are not doing that
rundown tonight.

Priya's on the desk beside her, headset on, more composed than she has
any right to be given the week we've all just had, and she catches my
eye once, briefly, in a look that says, as clearly as words could,
*I've got the desk, whatever you two are about to do, I've got the
desk.* I have never in my life been so grateful for a seventeen-year-
old.

"You came," I say to Nadia, quietly, under the pre-show chatter, the
only thing I let myself say that isn't strictly business.

"I said I'd think about it."

"You did."

"I thought about it," Nadia says, "for three days, and then I thought:
if I don't come tonight, the last thing anyone hears from this show is
an accident. I'd rather the last thing be mine."

We go live. The rundown lasts about two minutes — long enough for an
opening link, not long enough to reach the first scripted question —
before I do the thing I came here to do, the only thing I actually
planned, walking over: I hand her the second mic.

"This is Nadia," I say, on air, live, to however many people are
listening at seven o'clock on a Thursday. "She's been the voice behind
Static for two years, and she's about to tell you why, if she wants
to. And if she doesn't, that's the whole segment, and we'll play a
song."

I mean every word of it. I have no idea, saying it, which way this
goes.

Nadia is quiet for a second that feels considerably longer than a
second, on live radio, with dead air doing exactly what dead air does
to two people who have spent six weeks being professionally terrified
of it.

The phone lines start lighting up almost immediately, which none of
us expected, four calls in the first ten seconds, all of them, Priya
tells me afterward, from regulars who've clocked exactly what
"Static" means and have simply been waiting, patiently, for weeks, for
somebody to say the word out loud. Priya doesn't put any of them
through. She just watches the board light up, and watches Nadia, and
waits, along with the rest of us, for whatever comes next.

Then she talks.

She tells the truth about Static, in her own words, on her own terms —
the two years, the reason, the podcast that still exists and that she
has never listened to all the way through. She tells it plainly,
without performing it, the way she's told me every true thing since
the night on the fire escape, and somewhere in the middle of it I
understand why it had to wait exactly this long: not because anyone
was hiding it from the listeners forever, but because it had to wait
until it was hers to give, on her own schedule, in her own voice,
chosen rather than taken.

"Two years ago," she says, "somebody made a podcast about the worst
year of my life, using my name, without asking me. Twelve episodes.
I've listened to two of them. I don't need the other ten, because I
lived them, and I know how the story goes, and I've spent two years
letting a desk and a set of headphones be the version of me that
nobody could turn into content. That's what Static was. It still is,
some nights. But I'm tired of it being the only version of me anybody
gets, and I decided, about a week ago, that I'd rather risk being seen
badly than keep being unseen carefully, forever, on principle."

There's a caller Priya finally lets through, near the end, an older
woman who says only, "hen, that took some nerve," and hangs up before
Nadia can answer, which turns out, somehow, to be exactly the right
amount of response for the moment to hold.

"I stopped being a person people talked about," she says, near the
end of it, "and started being a voice with a desk in front of it. I'm
choosing, right now, live, not to be that anymore. Not because anyone's
making me. Because I want to try being seen and see what's actually
left standing."

I let the silence that follows sit for exactly as long as it needs to.

Then, because I can hear her breathing change, I say the only thing
that's actually mine to say into that silence: "You don't have to say
anything else. That's enough. That was always going to be enough."

"I'm not finished," Nadia says, and something in her voice has
shifted — steadier now, less like a person confessing and more like a
person deciding. "I want to say the other thing too, the one I've
never said on any recording, private or otherwise. I was frightened
of exactly this. Being a story again. And I did it anyway, because
somewhere in the last six weeks I found out there's a difference
between being a story and being known, and I'd rather risk the second
one than keep hiding from both."

Then I say the thing I promised her on a fire escape, six weeks and a
lifetime ago, out loud, on the record, in front of everyone.

"What's real off that mic stays off the record," I say. "Every time.
I promised her that on the night before our first broadcast. And
everything she's just told you, she chose to put on the record
herself. That's the difference. It's the only one that's ever
mattered."

·

Off air, the ON AIR bulb goes dark — amber, still, never fixed — and
we stand in a basement that smells of damp and cable insulation and
say nothing useful to each other for a while, because nothing useful
is required.

"You didn't have to hand me the mic," Nadia says, eventually.

"I know," I say. "I wanted you to have it before you decided whether
you wanted it."

Priya comes and stands with us for a minute, not saying anything, just
present, the three of us in a basement that smells of damp and cable
insulation, having just done the strangest and most honest hour of
live radio any of us will ever be part of.

"The phones haven't stopped," she says, eventually, holding up a
handset. "I stopped answering about four minutes in. I didn't think
you'd want an audience for whatever this was going to be."

"Thank you," I say, and mean it more than the two words can carry.

"Don't thank me. Go home. Both of you. I've got the desk, and I've
got the phones, and I've got, apparently, a great deal more sense than
either of the two adults currently running this station." She says it
with a smile that undercuts the sternness entirely, and shoos us both
toward the stairs like she's the one who's been managing this building
for six years and not the other way round.

We go up the stairs into a Glasgow evening that has, for once, no
rain in it at all, just a clear, cold September dark with a moon low
over the tenements, and stand on Dumbarton Road for a moment, not
saying anything, letting the last hour settle into something we can
actually hold rather than something still happening to us.

"I need to say the last thing," Nadia says, eventually, "and then I'm
done talking for the night."

"Say it."

"I'm not fixed. What happened on Wednesday doesn't get undone because
tonight went well. I'm going to have bad days about this for a long
time, probably. I wanted you to know that going in, so tonight
doesn't get to be the ending of it in your head, even though it's
going to feel like one."

"I know," I say. "I wasn't expecting fixed. I was expecting you, and
I got you, and that was always the whole of what I was hoping for."

We walk for a while without any particular destination, down toward
the river in the cold clear dark, past the chip shop, over the humped
bridge where the Kelvin runs black and fast below us, retracing, more
or less exactly, the route of the first ordinary evening we ever
spent together, chips on a wall on Byres Road, before any of this had
a name.

"Do you remember," Nadia says, at the bridge, "the first time we
walked back from that chip shop, and neither of us closed the gap
between our hands, on purpose, because we'd agreed to that without
saying so?"

"I remember."

"I think about that walk more than almost anything else from the last
six weeks. Not tonight. Not the broadcast, or the mic, or any of it.
That walk, where nothing happened at all, except two people deciding,
without a single word between them, that something was worth being
careful with."

"Why that one?"

"Because it's the one where it was still entirely ours," Nadia says.
"Before the panel knew. Before the podcast. Before any of it got
complicated by anyone else's opinion about what it meant. Just two
people on a bridge, not touching, on purpose, because the not-touching
mattered more than touching would have."

I take her hand properly this time, there on the bridge, and she lets
me, and neither of us says anything else about it, because some
things, having been said once, don't need saying twice to be true.

·

We stand there a while longer, the Kelvin running black and fast below
us, the city's ordinary Thursday-night noise carrying up from
Dumbarton Road behind us, and Nadia says, eventually, quietly, "I want
to tell you the rest of it. Not tonight. Soon. The whole of what the
podcast actually cost me, properly, not in pieces."

"Whenever you're ready."

"I know. That's rather the point of telling you now — that I trust the
whenever, finally, instead of bracing for you to need it sooner." She
looks out at the water. "Three years ago I'd have said this bridge was
just a bridge. Tonight it's the place I told you the truth had to wait
until it was mine to give. I don't think I'll ever cross it again
without thinking of that, and I find, oddly, that I don't mind."

"Neither will I."

"Good," she says, and that's the whole of it, standing on a bridge in
a clear cold September dark, two women who've spent six weeks building
something out of scraps and found, in the end, that it held.

We walk the rest of the way home slowly, in no hurry at all, past the
chip shop with its shutters half up for the late trade, and Nadia
stops once more, outside my own close this time, before we go up.

"I want to say one more thing," she says, "and then I really am done
talking for the night, properly this time."

"Go on."

"Thank you for handing me the mic before you decided whether I wanted
it. I don't think I've properly said that yet, not the actual thank
you, underneath everything else I said on air. It mattered more than
the broadcast did. It mattered that you did it without knowing which
way I'd go."

"I'd do it again."

"I know," Nadia says. "That's rather why it mattered in the first
place," and we go up together, into a flat that has slowly, over six
weeks, stopped being a place I arranged to survive being alone in, and
become, instead, somewhere I actually live.

I ring Fiona before I sleep, briefly, just to say it went well,
without going into the whole of it, because some of tonight belongs
only to the bridge and the two of us who stood on it.

"That's all you're giving me? 'It went well'?"

"For tonight. I'll tell you properly another time."

"Fine," Fiona says, with theatrical, entirely good-natured
reluctance. "I'll allow the withholding, this once, because you sound
happier on the phone right now than I've heard you sound in about a
decade, and I'm choosing to take that as sufficient information for
one evening."

"Goodnight, Fiona."

"Goodnight. Go to sleep smiling. You've earned it," she says, and rings
off, and I do exactly that, in a flat that no longer feels like
somewhere I merely arranged to survive being alone in.

I lie awake a while longer anyway, not out of nerves this time, just
the specific reluctance to let a good day end before you've turned it
over properly at least once. I think about the whole of it, start to
finish — a Tuesday, a folder of bad numbers, a sentence I said out loud
before I'd checked whether it was true, and everything that grew,
slowly, out of a lie that turned out to have more true parts hiding
inside it than either of us had accounted for at the start.

I think, too, about Malcolm's two rules, and my mother's cassette tape
saying almost exactly the same thing years before either of us
compared notes, and Priya's laminated certificate on my fridge, and
Fiona's voice down a phone line telling me, patiently, for months, that
staying wasn't the same as settling. All of it, somehow, arriving here,
on this particular ordinary Thursday night, in a basement that still
leaks in three places and a flat that finally has something worth
keeping on its walls.

Whatever finishes here finishes privately, off any record at all,
which is exactly and only how either of us would have wanted it.


## Chapter Twenty-Three — Terms Renegotiated

The voicemail box is full by nine the next morning, which has never
once happened in the entire history of this station, not even the year
we accidentally aired eleven minutes of dead air during a council
byelection.

Most of the messages are listeners, and most of them are kind, and
several of them are people naming, in their own words, their own
version of being loved as a bit rather than as a person, glad to hear
someone say it back to them at last. A regional paper runs two hundred
and twenty words under the headline COMMUNITY RADIO'S HONEST FINALE,
which is not, in my experience, the kind of coverage a manufactured
stunt generally earns.

Priya's spreadsheet gets a third tab, unprompted, titled, in full,
GOOD NEWS.

The panel's silence, for once, does not read to me as a threat. It
reads like an institution quietly working out how to change its own
mind without saying so directly, which — I am learning, six weeks into
what started as an entirely fictional relationship and turned real
somewhere around week four — is roughly how institutions do
everything.

Nadia doesn't come down to the office to see the coverage. She's back
on her ordinary shift by the Saturday, both headphone cups doing their
usual thing, one on and one off, and when I ask how she's doing she
doesn't reach for a level or a timing.

"Tired," she says. "Relieved. Frightened, a bit, that it's not over
yet, because it isn't, not really — there'll be more clips, more
people who find the segment because of the coverage rather than
because they were already listening at midnight. I'm trying to decide
if that's alright."

"Is it?"

"Ask me again next week," she says, which I recognise now, gratefully,
as the actual answer rather than an evasion of one.

·

Day forty-four, exactly where the clock always said it would land: the
committee room, the same strip lighting, the same folder of numbers,
though this time I haven't rehearsed a single word of what I'm going
to say.

Douglas opens, predictably, with a version of *reputational exposure*,
though he's had three weeks to soften it and it comes out closer to
*learnings* than to anything resembling an actual objection. Someone
from the finance side asks a genuinely fair question about whether
funding an anonymous segment sets an awkward precedent, and I answer
it properly, at length, without once managing myself into a smaller
shape to make the room more comfortable — the first time, I realise
partway through, that I've ever done that in this specific room.

The renewal is approved. Not on the original metric — not on
*audience-identifiable relational content* — but on a rewritten one:
Static, named outright, funded as its own line item, no longer
something that has to hide inside a stunt in order to survive.

Kirsty argues for it in the room, plainly, on the record, in a way
that costs her something with the rest of the panel, and afterward, in
the corridor, she says the thing she should have said eight years ago
and has finally found the nerve to say over a folder of committee
minutes instead.

"I should have said what I said in that coffee shop a long time ago,"
she says. "For what it's worth — I'm glad you stayed."

"I'm glad you're saying it now," I say, and mean it, and that's the
whole of the repair either of us needs. Not a reconciliation with a
bow on it. Just two people, finally, saying a true thing out loud, on
the record, where it can be heard.

The show survives changed, not merely spared, which turns out to be
the better outcome by every measure that actually matters.

Priya, waiting in the corridor outside the committee room with a
coffee she's clearly bought specifically for this moment, hands it to
me the second I'm out the door.

"Well?"

"Renewed. Rewritten. Static's on the books, properly, with a fee."

Priya makes a sound I have never heard her make before, something
between a cheer and a sob, and hugs me, briefly, fiercely, before
remembering that she is seventeen and has a reputation for composure
to maintain, and stepping back, straightening her jacket, entirely
unconvincing.

"I'm very calm about this," she says.

"I can tell."

"I'm going to go build a spreadsheet."

"Of course you are."

She goes, at something close to a run, and I stand in the corridor of
the Chambers annexe for a moment, holding a coffee I didn't ask for,
in a building I've walked out of feeling defeated more times than I
can count over six years, and let myself, properly, feel the opposite
of that for once.

·

I ring Nadia from the corridor before I've even left the building,
because some news doesn't survive the walk back intact if you don't
say it the moment you have it.

"Renewed," I say, the second she picks up. "Rewritten. Static's on the
books, named, funded, properly credited. It's yours, in writing, by
the end of the week."

There's a silence on the line, longer than I expect, and for a second
I think the call's dropped.

"Nadia?"

"I'm here." Her voice does something I've only heard it do a handful
of times, something that isn't quite steady. "Sorry. I just — I've
spent two years assuming the safest version of me was the invisible
one. I'm still getting used to the idea that the visible one gets to
be funded too."

"She does," I say. "Properly. In writing."

"In writing," Nadia repeats, like she's testing the shape of the
phrase, the way you test a floor before you trust your whole weight to
it, and I let her have the silence that follows, because some
sentences need room to actually land.

·

The formal paperwork takes another fortnight to arrive, in the way
these things always do — a licence variation, a budget line, a letter
from Douglas that manages, even now, to sound faintly put-upon about
the whole arrangement, though I notice he signs it without a single
further objection. Nadia frames the letter, eventually, not out of
sentiment exactly but because she says she wants to be able to look at
it on a bad day and remember that somebody, somewhere, put something
real about her in writing and meant it.

It hangs, in the end, in the overnight studio, just above the desk,
where she can see it every single shift, a small rectangle of official
council letterhead doing considerably more work than its size would
suggest.

·

Priya insists on marking the renewal properly, in the way she insists
on marking everything she considers significant, which turns out to
mean cake, bought from the good bakery on Byres Road rather than the
supermarket, and a hand-lettered banner strung across the kitchen that
reads, in full, KELVIN ROW: STILL HERE, STILL BORING, STILL FUNDED,
which she considers, correctly, the accurate summary of the entire
last two months.

We eat cake standing up in a kitchen that smells of damp and
transmitter oil, all four of us — me, Nadia, Priya, and Dev, who has
fully recovered from his food poisoning and has, in the intervening
weeks, become something of an unofficial fifth member of the group
chat, still called, at Priya's insistence, OPERATION COUPLE, though
the operation part has long since stopped meaning what it originally
meant.

"To the roof," Priya says, raising a paper cup of orange squash like
it's champagne, "which is still leaking, and will presumably always be
leaking, and which none of this changes even slightly."

"To the roof," we all agree, and drink to a leaking roof with the
specific sincerity of people who have learned, this autumn, exactly
what a building like this one is actually worth keeping standing for.

·

Malcolm turns up that same afternoon, unannounced as ever, using the
key he was never asked to hand back, and takes in the banner, the cake
crumbs, the general state of a basement that has clearly just been
celebrating something, with the specific slow satisfaction of a man
watching a project he handed over eleven years ago finally arrive
somewhere he'd hoped it might.

"Heard you got the renewal," he says.

"Rewritten, not just renewed."

"Better," he says, lowering himself into the good chair without
asking, same as always. "Renewed means they let you keep doing the
same thing. Rewritten means they finally understood what the thing
actually was." He looks round the room once more, at Priya's banner,
at the wall of fame now visibly overflowing its corkboard. "Don't let
it become boring," he says, the way he always says it, "and don't let
it close."

"Working on both."

"I can see that." He accepts a slice of cake from Priya, who presents
it to him with the specific ceremony she reserves for the station's
founder, and eats it slowly, looking round the basement one more time.
"I'll tell you the thing I never told you, eleven years ago, handing
you the keys," he says, eventually. "I didn't think you'd manage it.
Not because I doubted you specifically — I didn't know you well enough
yet to doubt you specifically — but because nobody manages it, usually.
Stations like this one fold within a couple of years, most places. I
handed you the keys mostly because there wasn't anybody else standing
in the room, same as you've told me happened to you."

"Why are you telling me now?"

"Because you've just done the thing I didn't think was possible, and I
think you should know that I noticed, and that it mattered, and that
I'm not in the habit of saying so, which is probably a fault of mine
rather than a virtue." He finishes the cake, sets the plate down. "Nine
years. Not bad, for someone I handed a coal store and a set of keys
to, with no plan beyond two rules."

"Two rules were enough."

"Apparently so," Malcolm says, and gets up to leave, slower than he
used to, and pauses at the top of the basement stairs to look back
down at the four of us still standing in the kitchen. "Don't let it
become boring," he says once more, "and don't let it close," and this
time, for the first time in eleven years, I understand it isn't an
instruction at all. It's a blessing, dressed up as one, the only kind
he's ever known how to give.

·

I ring Fiona from the corridor outside the committee room before the
news has even had time to properly settle in me, because some things
need saying to the person who's carried the worry alongside you before
they're said to anyone else.

"Renewed," I say, the second she picks up. "Rewritten, actually. Better
than renewed."

There's a whoop down the line loud enough that I have to hold the
phone slightly away from my ear, entirely unlike Fiona's usual
composed register, and it takes her a moment to recover enough to form
actual words.

"I'm coming down this weekend," she announces, once she's got her
breath back. "I don't care what's on. I want to see the place properly,
finally, now that it's not about to disappear out from under you."

"You've seen the place plenty of times."

"I've seen the place when it was something you were fighting to keep.
I want to see it now it's something you've actually won." A pause,
softer. "I'm proud of you. Properly. Not just about the renewal. About
all of it — the whole autumn. You've done more growing up in two
months than I watched you do in the previous decade, and I include
myself in the category of people who should have pushed you toward
some of it sooner."

"You can't make yourself responsible for my decisions, Fiona."

"I know. I'm just saying it anyway, because it's true, and because
you've spent your whole life being the one who says the true, difficult
things to other people, and I think it's time somebody said one back
to you, for a change." She laughs, a little. "Now go and celebrate
properly, and tell Nadia I'll see her this weekend, and that I already
like her enormously based entirely on secondhand reports, which I
recognise is not a high bar for her to clear, but I intend to be
thoroughly won over regardless."

I promise to pass it on, and ring off smiling in a corridor of the
Chambers annexe I've walked out of feeling defeated more times than I
can count, finally, for once, feeling nothing but the opposite.

·

Fiona does come down that weekend, exactly as promised, and meets
Nadia properly for the first time over cake in the station kitchen,
the two of them sizing each other up with the specific caution of a
sister and a partner meeting for the first time, both aware that
whatever's said in the next hour will set the tone for years of future
gatherings.

"So you're the overnight engineer," Fiona says, by way of opening.

"I'm the overnight engineer," Nadia agrees, entirely unbothered by the
scrutiny, in the specific flat register I've come to find endlessly
reassuring rather than off-putting. "Also, apparently, the reason your
sister's stopped ringing you every Tuesday sounding like she's about
to collapse under the weight of a leaking roof single-handedly."

"I noticed that too," Fiona says, visibly recalibrating her opening
line, warming immediately. "I like you already. Most people meeting me
for the first time try very hard to be charming. You've just stated a
fact."

"I find facts generally land better than charm," Nadia says. "Charm
runs out. Facts don't."

Fiona laughs, properly, the specific laugh she reserves for people
she's decided, quickly and permanently, that she approves of, and the
rest of the visit passes easily, the two of them trading stories about
me — Fiona's from childhood, Nadia's from the last two months — with
the specific delighted cruelty only two people who both love you
properly can manage without it ever once feeling like an attack.

"I like her enormously," Fiona tells me privately, before she gets back
on the train. "Properly. Not just 'she seems nice.' She's exactly the
kind of person I'd have chosen for you myself, if anyone had ever
asked me, which nobody ever does, being your sister rather than your
actual decision-maker in these matters."

"You've made your opinion known plenty over the years regardless."

"That's simply due diligence," Fiona says, entirely unrepentant, and
hugs me at the platform, and gets on her train back to Aberdeen,
leaving me to walk back to the station with the specific settled
feeling of having watched two of the most important people in my life
meet properly, at last, and like each other exactly as much as I'd
hoped they would.


## Chapter Twenty-Four — Out of Office

Priya, formally paid now out of the renegotiated budget, hires two more
volunteers off her own initiative and hands me a finished roster
instead of a problem, the following week, without once asking me first.

"You didn't have to run this past me," I say.

"I know," Priya says. "That was the point. Try it. Not asking."

I try it. It's uncomfortable, in the specific way that letting go of a
thing you've held too tightly is always uncomfortable, and I do it
anyway, which is, I think, the entire remaining shape of the thing I
needed to learn.

Nadia, at the desk, headphone cup off on one side as always, doesn't
say anything about it. She just catches my eye across the room, in
exactly the way she did in week one, except everything about what it
means between us has changed completely.

The theatre contract, in the end, goes back in its envelope, unsigned,
not because she decided against it in one dramatic conversation but
because the six months on offer clashed with a season of Static she'd
already, without telling anyone, started planning properly for the
first time — segments with themes, a loose arc across the autumn,
the unmistakable behaviour of someone building something she intends
to still be building next year.

"You could still take work like that," I say, once, carefully, not
wanting the question to sound like a test. "Touring. If you wanted
to."

"I know I could," Nadia says. "I'm choosing not to, this year, because
I want to see what happens if I stay somewhere on purpose instead of
because leaving felt too loud to risk." She looks at me for a second.
"Ask me again next year."

"I will," I say, and I mean it exactly as literally as she'd want me
to.

·

Kirsty comes by, once, on a Thursday with nothing scheduled, just to
see the place properly for the first time in eight years — not for the
panel, not with a lanyard, in an ordinary coat like anyone else who
might wander down Dumbarton Road and in through a basement door.

"It hasn't changed," she says, looking round at the buckets, the ON
AIR bulb, the whiteboard still faintly ghosted with six weeks of
crossed-out titles nobody's bothered to wipe clean.

"It has, actually. New roof fund. New line item for Static. New
volunteer roster that doesn't fall over every time somebody gets food
poisoning."

"I meant it hasn't changed in the way that matters," Kirsty says. "It's
still the place people actually need. I'd forgotten what that looked
like, up close."

I don't know what to say to that, so I offer her tea instead, badly
made, in a station mug with a chip in the rim, and she takes it, and
stays longer than either of us expects her to, and it is, in its own
small unremarkable way, its own kind of repair.

·

An ordinary Thursday, some weeks later, with nothing scheduled at all.

The amber bulb is still not fixed. The kettle still takes four minutes.
Priya runs a roster that no longer needs me in it, which I am,
slowly, learning to be glad about rather than threatened by.

"How are you," I ask Nadia, at the desk, not for the show, not for
anyone.

And she answers the actual question, for once, in words, rather than
in levels or timings or durations. "Good," she says. "Better than good.
I keep waiting for the segment to end, and it doesn't."

I write a sign for the studio door, half as a joke and half not: OUT
OF OFFICE, in Priya's careful hand, propped against the amber bulb
whenever the two of us are actually off the clock, which is a category
that exists now in a way it never quite did before.

It was never about being away from the desk. It's the one hour a week
— sometimes considerably more than an hour — that belongs to nobody's
audience but us.

No proposal. No five-years-later. Just two people who have stopped
performing anything for anyone, including, at last, each other,
choosing, on an ordinary Thursday, to come back to the same room on
purpose, with nothing at all left to broadcast.

Priya finds the sign a few weeks in and adds a second line underneath
it, in the same careful hand, without asking permission, which I've
come to understand is simply how she operates now: BACK TOMORROW. LEVELS
ARE FINE.

I leave it there. It's true, as far as I can tell, on every register
that matters — the desk's, and the other one, the one neither of us
had a word for six weeks ago and now, mostly, don't need one for at
all, because it's simply what's happening between us, plainly, out
loud, on and off the record, every ordinary Thursday since.

·

If you want the tidy version — the one the regional paper never quite
managed to print, the one Priya's spreadsheet doesn't have a tab for
— it goes something like this.

Static runs Tuesdays and Fridays now, properly funded, properly
credited, though Nadia still won't say her surname on air and nobody
who matters has ever once asked her to. The wall of fame by the kettle
has run out of corkboard twice and been rebuilt twice, wider both
times. Sandra, the widow from week two, still writes in most Fridays.
Grant from Maryhill sorted out the cutlery drawer situation and now
sends in updates nobody asked for and everybody reads anyway.

Kirsty comes down to the station more often than either of us
predicted, usually with no excuse at all, which I've come to understand
is itself the excuse — she doesn't need a reason to be somewhere she
wants to be, and she's slowly, visibly, relearning how that works,
same as I am.

Priya starts university in two years with, I have absolutely no doubt,
a media studies degree and a plan for the station that will make my
own five-year plan look like a shopping list. I've already told her
the manager's job is hers whenever she wants it. She's already told me
she's not taking it until I've finished, in her words, "learning how
to have a life outside this basement," which is either the kindest or
the most insufferable thing anyone in this building has ever said to
me, and possibly both.

And Nadia and I are, on any given Thursday, simply two people who work
together, and live four streets apart, and have stopped needing a
rundown to know what the other one means.

I ring Fiona in Aberdeen the week after the renewal comes through,
mostly to tell her properly, in full, the whole of what I only gave
her in fragments back when it was still a secret worth keeping.

"So it's real," she says, once I've finished. "Actually real. Not
just for the show real."

"Actually real."

"Good," Fiona says, with the specific satisfaction of a sister who has
waited a long time to be allowed to say *I told you so* about
something. "You've been lonely since you were nineteen years old and
decided somebody had to be the reliable one. I'm glad you finally
found someone who noticed."

I don't have an answer to that, so I don't try to build one. I just
sit with it, the way I've learned to sit with true things this
autumn, and let her be right about it, and go back downstairs to a
basement that smells of damp and diesel and, underneath it all, cold
tea, where somebody is waiting for me who noticed a long time before
I ever worked up the nerve to ask.

I take the tape out of the box in the loft, finally, on an ordinary
Sunday some weeks after the renewal, and this time I don't put it back
unplayed.

I play the whole of it, sitting on my own kitchen floor with the box
open beside me, and Nadia sits with me through the whole four minutes,
not fixing anything, not filling the silence, just present, the way
she was the first time, except this time I let the entire message
play — the bit about the transmitter insurance, the bit about the
weather, and then, right at the end, a bit I'd forgotten was even
there, four seconds I somehow never got to on the loft ladder that
first Sunday: *"Tell that station of yours I'm proud of it. And of
you. I don't say that enough. Don't let it become boring, hen, and
don't you dare let it close."*

I sit with that for a long time afterward, the coincidence of it —
my mother, unknowingly, years before Malcolm ever said the words to
me directly, using almost exactly his phrase, as though the whole
building had simply always run on the same two rules, passed hand to
hand, mother to founder to me, without any of them ever comparing
notes.

"She sounds like she knew you very well," Nadia says, quietly, once
the tape's finished and the room's gone back to ordinary quiet.

"She did," I say, and for the first time in six years, saying that
sentence doesn't cost me anything at all. It just feels true, and
settled, and finally, properly, grieved.

·

Fiona comes down from Aberdeen again a few months later, for an
ordinary weekend rather than a wedding, and meets Nadia properly for
the first time, over a dinner that Nadia insists on cooking despite
her well-documented culinary limitations, which produces, predictably,
one slightly burnt but entirely edible attempt at something involving
chicken.

"I like her," Fiona tells me afterward, in the kitchen, drying dishes
while Nadia and Priya argue good-naturedly in the other room about
whether the automation queue needs a fourth safeguard or whether three
is, as Priya insists, already excessive. "I like her a lot, actually.
She listens to you properly. I've watched people talk at you for years,
Robyn. She's the first one I've watched actually listen."

"I noticed that too. Eventually."

"You always were slow with the important things," Fiona says, not
unkindly, nudging me with her shoulder the way she has since we were
children, "and remarkably fast with everything else. Roofs. Rotas.
Buckets. Just terribly slow with anything that mattered to you
personally."

"I'm working on it."

"I can see that," Fiona says, and looks through to the other room,
where Nadia's just won whatever the automation argument was about, and
Priya's conceding with enormous, theatrical bad grace, and something in
my sister's face settles, the specific look of someone who has spent a
long time worrying about a person and has finally, gladly, been allowed
to stop.

·

Priya does, in the end, start university two years later exactly as
predicted, media studies with a minor she invents largely on the spot
during her interview, and hands the manager's job back to me with the
specific ceremony of someone bestowing a great honour rather than
returning a burden, on the grounds that she's "not ready to run the
whole building yet, only most of it."

"You already run most of it," I tell her.

"I know," she says. "I wanted it on the record officially, though,
before I go off and let some university lecturer tell me things I
already learned properly in a basement." She hugs me, briefly and
fiercely, exactly the way she did in the corridor outside the committee
room two years earlier, before remembering her reputation and stepping
back to compose herself. "I'll be back every summer. Kevin the smoke
alarm still doesn't trust either of you, and somebody needs to keep an
eye on that situation."

"We'll manage."

"You'll manage badly, and cheerfully, the way you always do," Priya
says, "which is, I've come to understand, rather the entire operating
philosophy of this building, and I wouldn't change a single thing
about it."

She goes off to her first term with a list of instructions for the
volunteer rota she insists on leaving behind despite nobody asking for
it, and the station runs on, exactly as it always has, one leaking
roof and one amber bulb and one very good couple at a time.

I still ring Fiona most Sundays, the same as I always have, except now
there's considerably more to tell her than a roof fund and a rota, and
she still says the same thing she's said since we were children,
half joke and half genuine instruction, at the end of every call: don't
do anything she wouldn't do. I have, by now, an actual answer for that,
which I give her, most weeks, and which never once stops making her
laugh: "Too late. Already did."

·

Kirsty asks me once, months later, over a coffee that's become
something of a habit between us now rather than a rare, careful
event, whether I ever regret how it all started — the lie to the
panel, the whole elaborate scaffolding of a fake romance built to save
a leaking roof.

"Not for a second," I tell her. "I regret that it took a crisis to make
me finally ask her. But I don't regret the shape it took. I think if
I'd asked her plainly, out of nowhere, in year two or three, she'd
have said no, and rightly, because neither of us was ready yet for
what it actually turned into. The six weeks weren't the lie. They were
the only door either of us knew how to walk through at the time."

"That's a generous way of looking at it."

"It's an accurate way of looking at it," I say, borrowing, without
meaning to, exactly Nadia's own preferred phrase, and Kirsty laughs,
recognising it immediately, having heard it enough times now from both
of us that it's become something of a running joke between the three
of us whenever anyone tries to dress up a hard truth as a kindness.

"You two have started sounding alike," she says. "I mean that as a
compliment, for what it's worth. I don't think I've ever watched two
people become so completely fluent in each other's way of seeing the
world without either of them losing their own voice in the process."

I think about that observation for a long while afterward, the way I
think about most things Kirsty says now that she's finally decided to
say the true things rather than the careful ones, and find that she's
right, in the way people who've known you a long time are sometimes
uniquely positioned to be — not about the show, not about the panel,
just about the plain, ordinary fact of two people who've built
something real enough that it's started reshaping the language they
use to describe the whole of their lives.

That's the whole of it. It was never going to be a bigger story than
that. I've come to think that's rather the point.

## Chapter One — The Panel

I should say, before any of this, that I am not a person who does
things like this. I file paperwork on time. I keep a spreadsheet of
which volunteer owes which shift to which other volunteer, going back
four years, cross-referenced by favour and grudge. I have never once,
in six years of running a community radio station out of a basement
that used to be a coal store, said a single sentence in an official
meeting that I had not already rehearsed, out loud, on a bus, twice.

Then a Tuesday happens, and apparently that's the version of me you
get instead.

Here is the sentence I say to the funding panel, out loud, with my whole
chest, before I have checked whether a single word of it is true:

"Yes, of course we have a new couples hour. Six weeks, live, Thursdays."

I want it on the record — my own record, the one I keep in my head for
later, at four in the morning, when I run back over every stupid thing
I have ever said out loud — that I did not plan to say this. I had a
folder. The folder had numbers in it, and every column of numbers was
bad, and I had rehearsed, on the bus, a version of this meeting that
involved words like *restructuring* and *engagement pathway* and
absolutely nothing about anybody's love life.

The room is a committee room in the Chambers annexe, the kind with a
carpet designed specifically to disguise thirty years of spilled
coffee and a ventilation system that makes everyone sound slightly
underwater. There are six people at the table and a folder in front of
each of them, all identical, all containing the same set of numbers I
have spent a fortnight arranging into the most flattering order
available, which is still not a flattering order.

Douglas opens with the transmitter contract, which is not going well.
Then he opens with the roof, which is going worse. Then, somewhere
around minute eight, a woman in a blazer I don't recognise — new to
the panel, evidently, because everyone else at the table has the
specific weary fondness of people who've sat through four years of my
folders — leans forward and asks, not unkindly, whether Kelvin Row has
"any relationship-facing content in the pipeline."

I have never heard the phrase *relationship-facing content* before in
my life. I will hear it in my sleep for the rest of this book.

"It's part of the newer engagement criteria," she adds, when I don't
answer fast enough. "Audience-identifiable, relatable, ideally
recurring. Stations doing well on renewal this cycle have all had
something like it. A cookery segment with a couple. A local sports
double-act. That sort of thing."

I look at my folder. My folder has, among its other failures, no
cookery segment, no local sports double-act, and absolutely nothing
resembling a couple, recurring or otherwise, because the entirety of
my own relationship history for the past four years could be summarised
as *occasionally texts back*.

And something in me — six years of running a station on a leaking roof
and a prayer, probably, if I'm being generous to myself, which I try
not to be — just goes.

"Yes."

The room does the thing rooms do when somebody has said something
interesting. Three people who were checking their phones stop checking
their phones.

"A couples hour," I say, because apparently I am doing this now. "Live.
On air. Real people, working through it, in real time." I have no idea
what *it* is. I have no idea who the real people are. I am, as far as I
can currently establish, the only real person in this scenario, and I
am lying to a room full of civil servants about my own love life, which
does not exist, in order to save a transmitter.

"Six weeks," I say. "Thursdays."

I don't know where the six comes from. It sounds like a number a serious
person would say. Six is a good number — long enough to be a
commitment, short enough that nobody has to actually watch me fail at
this in real time for more than a month and a half.

I look properly, for the first time since I sat down, at the far end
of the table, and understand, with an unhelpful lurch, why the room
felt different the moment I walked in. Kirsty Lennox is on the panel
now. Eight years ago we did breakfast together, three hours a day,
five days a week, and now she's in a blazer with a lanyard that says
GLASGOW COMMUNITY MEDIA RENEWAL PANEL and she is writing something
down without once looking up at me.

"That's very encouraging," Douglas says, in the tone of a man who has
never once, in four years, said anything that wasn't a euphemism.
"The panel would want to see that content develop properly across the
renewal period. Something the community can follow. Root for."

"Of course," I say.

"We'll look forward to it," Douglas says, and that's it. That's the
whole meeting. Twelve minutes, one folder of numbers nobody looked at,
and a promise I have made in public, in a room with minutes being
taken, about a relationship that is currently occupied by exactly
nobody.

I walk out into the rain on George Street not knowing one single fact
about the show I have just committed the station's entire licence
renewal to, including — and I want to be honest about the scale of this
— who is in it.

·

The thing about Glasgow rain in September is that it doesn't fall so
much as arrive, sideways, off the Clyde, in sheets you can watch coming
up the street like weather with an agenda. I stand under the awning of
the Chambers annexe for four minutes deciding whether to get the subway
or just accept the rain as a fact about the rest of my day, and
somewhere in those four minutes I stop thinking about the meeting and
start thinking about the roof.

The roof is not a metaphor. The roof is an actual roof, over an actual
basement, and it leaks in three specific places that Priya has labelled
by bucket size, and every single one of those buckets is downstream,
structurally and financially, of what just happened in that room.

I get the subway. I do not enjoy it. The Glasgow subway is a small
orange circle that takes you nowhere quickly and everywhere eventually,
and I have never once, in six years, managed to have a thought on it
that wasn't interrupted by the specific screech the tunnel makes
between Kelvinbridge and St George's Cross, which today feels like
the whole transport network personally weighing in on my decision-
making.

By the time I'm coming down Dumbarton Road toward the tenement, I have
run the maths four times and got four different answers, none of them
good, and the sign over the close mouth — KELVIN ROW RADIO, hand-painted,
1987, repainted twice since and never once redesigned — is doing the
thing it does in rain, which is look exactly as permanent and exactly
as precarious as it actually is.

I go down the basement stairs into the smell that means home to me now
in a way I try not to examine too closely: damp concrete, warm dust off
old equipment, and somewhere underneath it, always, cold tea.

I came here first at nineteen, a volunteer with a media studies
diploma nobody had asked for and an ability to stay calm on a phone
line that turned out to matter considerably more than the diploma
ever did. The founder, a retired shipyard electrician called Malcolm
who ran the station out of pure stubbornness for eleven years before
his knees gave out, handed me the keys on his last day with the exact
words, "don't let it become boring, and don't let it close," which I
have taken, ever since, as the entire job description, more useful by
a distance than anything in my actual contract.

I became manager at twenty-five, not because I was ready, but because
there was nobody else standing in the room when the question got
asked, and I have spent the nine years since discovering that most of
running a community station is exactly that: being the one still
standing in the room when a question needs answering, whether or not
you feel qualified to answer it. The roof has been failing since
before I arrived. The transmitter is older than most of our
volunteers. None of that has ever once been the actual point. The
point is a red bulb over a door that tells the street outside somebody
is talking to somebody else in this city right now, live, uncut, and
that the somebody talking might, on a good night, say something worth
hearing.

·

There are three buckets in the corridor before you even reach the
studio, and I know them by name because Priya has named them. SMALL
LEAK is a margarine tub under the fuse board. MEDIUM LEAK is an actual
bucket, from an actual builder's merchant, under the join where the
extension meets the original close. BIG LEAK — capital letters, Sharpie,
underlined twice — sits directly under a patch of ceiling in the main
studio that has been the colour and texture of a well-used teabag since
before I started here.

Priya is sitting under the ON AIR bulb with a laptop, a headset half on,
and the specific expression of a seventeen-year-old who has been running
a Saturday shift alone since two in the afternoon and has strong
feelings about it that she is choosing, for now, not to escalate.

"How was the panel," she says, not looking up, in the tone of someone
asking how the dentist went.

"They want a couples hour."

"They want *what*."

"Audience-identifiable relational content." I say it the way Douglas
said it, all six syllables of *relational* laid out like a man setting
a trap he doesn't understand is a trap. "Live, Thursdays, six weeks."

Priya looks up properly now. She has the specific stillness of a
teenager deciding whether the adult in front of her has finally lost it,
weighing that against four years of evidence that the adult in front of
her has never, technically, had anything to lose it from, because there
has never been very much to begin with.

"You don't have a couple," she says.

"No."

"You have a roof problem and a Tuesday."

"I'm aware."

She closes the laptop, which from Priya is the equivalent of standing
up and pacing. "Robyn. I want to be supportive. I do the gossip *and*
the desk, I am extremely well positioned to be supportive. But you have
just promised a licensing panel a segment that requires two consenting
adults who are, ideally, dating, and the only two consenting adults in
this building right now are you and me, and I am seventeen, and even
setting that aside I would frankly rather eat the mic foam."

"I'm not asking you."

"I would like that on record too."

I sit down on the good chair — the one with all four wheels, which
Priya has clearly claimed as her own and which I am, this once, taking
back by seniority — and put my head in my hands for exactly as long as
I allow myself these things, which is about four seconds.

"The roof," I say, into my palms. "The transmitter contract. Your
stipend, which is the only wage that comes out of this building that
isn't mine, and mine doesn't count because I'd do this for nothing and
we both know it."

"I know what the renewal pays for."

"So I need a show. A real one. Live."

Priya looks at the ceiling — at BIG LEAK, specifically, which has
chosen this exact moment to produce a single, theatrical drip into its
bucket — and back at me.

"You don't have a couple," she says again, more gently this time, "but
you might have a candidate."

"Don't."

"I'm not saying anything."

"You're saying something."

"I'm saying," Priya says, picking her laptop back up, entirely too
pleased with herself for someone who is about to go back to running a
Saturday shift alone, "that there's a woman two rooms over who's been
here every single overnight for three years, who nobody looks at twice,
who could not be less interested in being *seen*, which — and I want
you to notice I am not naming her — is precisely the qualification you
currently need."

I don't say anything.

Priya, watching me not say anything, allows herself the smallest,
most infuriating version of a smile, and puts her headset back on.

"I'm not doing it," I say, eventually, to the back of her headset,
which does not respond, because she has, with characteristic
efficiency, already gone back to whatever she was doing on the
laptop and is choosing not to dignify the sentence with a reply.

"I mean it, Priya."

"I heard you the first time," she says, without looking up. "You said
it very firmly. I noted it down. I've also noted down that you're
still standing in this doorway forty seconds later instead of leaving,
which tells me something rather different."

I leave. Properly this time, up the stairs, past BIG LEAK's bucket,
which has filled another quarter-inch since I last looked at it, and
out into a rain that has, if anything, got worse in the last hour, as
though the whole city has opinions about my afternoon and has decided
to express them meteorologically.

I don't go back to my flat straight away. I walk the long way, down
past the overnight studio's external door — the one that opens onto
the lane rather than the foyer, the one only the overnight engineer
ever really uses — and stand there for a moment in the rain doing
absolutely nothing useful at all, before telling myself, sternly, that
this is not a reconnaissance mission, and going home.

My flat, when I finally get there, is on the second floor of a
tenement two streets from the station, chosen nine years ago
specifically for its proximity rather than any charm of its own,
which it does not particularly have. I make tea I don't drink, which
is becoming something of a theme for the evening, and sit by the
window watching the rain do its thing to Dumbarton Road, and think,
for longer than is strictly useful, about the precise expression on
Nadia Quinn's face through a pane of studio glass three years running,
and what it might mean that I've apparently been cataloguing it
without ever quite admitting to myself that I was doing so.

·

My sister rings at nine, which is either very good or very bad timing,
depending on how the next forty minutes go, because Fiona has never
once in her life let a piece of family news go un-interrogated, and I
have not yet decided how much of today I'm prepared to hand over.

"You sound strange," she says, by way of hello, before I've said
anything at all beyond my own name. "Strange how?"

"I promised the licensing panel a couples hour."

There's a pause on the line long enough that I can hear her doing the
thing she does when she's recalibrating — a small exhale, the specific
sound of a woman who left the family business of worrying about Kelvin
Row behind eleven years ago and has never quite managed to stop doing
it from a distance. Fiona wanted the bigger thing, always did, and got
it — a proper job, a proper flat, a proper distance from a basement
that smells of damp and cold tea — and some nights I think she's the
only person in my life who understands exactly what staying cost me,
because she's the one who watched me choose it.

"With who," she says, eventually.

"I haven't worked that out yet."

"Robyn."

"I said yes before I'd worked out the who. I know how that sounds."

"It sounds," Fiona says, in the specific tone she's used on me since
we were children and she was twelve minutes older and considerably
more sensible, "like you did the thing Mum used to do. Say yes to the
big thing first and work out the mechanics standing in the rain
afterward."

I don't have an answer for that, because it's accurate, and because
hearing our mother mentioned so plainly, on a random Tuesday, still
does something to my chest six years on that I haven't fully learned
to brace for.

"How's the roof," Fiona asks, gentler now, which is her way of letting
me off the hook for the Mum comment without either of us having to
name that she's doing it.

"Leaking in three places. Priya's named the buckets."

"Of course she has." A smile in her voice, finally. "You know you
could just let it close, don't you. Nobody would think less of you.
You've given it nine years. Nobody gets nine years out of a person and
then calls them a failure for stopping."

"I know."

"You're not going to let it close, though."

"No," I say, "I'm not," and she laughs, not unkindly, the specific
laugh of someone who asked a question she already knew the answer to
purely to hear me say it out loud, and we talk for a while longer
about nothing in particular — her new flat, a nephew's school play,
the ordinary furniture of two sisters staying in each other's lives at
a manageable distance — before she rings off with the same thing she
always says, half joke and half genuine instruction: "don't do anything
I wouldn't do," to which I no longer have a good answer, given what I
appear to have already done.

·

I don't sleep well, which is not, on its own, remarkable — I haven't
slept well on a Tuesday in roughly six years, on the theory that
Wednesday's admin always arrives whether or not I'm rested for it —
but tonight it's a different flavour of not-sleeping, the kind where
the mind keeps circling back to one specific fact rather than the
usual scattered worry about rotas and roofs.

This is not the first time the panel has come close to closing us.
Three years ago it was the transmitter, a fault that would have cost
more to fix than the station's entire annual grant, and I spent six
weeks that spring learning more about second-hand broadcast equipment
than any reasonable person should ever need to know, sourcing a part
from a decommissioned hospital radio service in Dundee that, by any
sensible measure, should not have worked, and did, for reasons I still
don't fully understand and have chosen not to examine too closely in
case examining it makes it stop.

The year before that it was a funding review that very nearly folded
four stations like ours into one regional service, run out of a proper
building with proper heating and none of Kelvin Row's actual history
in it, and I spent that entire summer writing letters, individually, by
hand, to every councillor who'd listen, until the review quietly
changed its own mind and left small stations alone, at least for that
cycle.

I have never once lost this building. I have come close enough times
that the closeness itself has become a kind of muscle memory, a
specific tightness in my chest that arrives whenever a meeting runs
long or an email from the panel's admin address lands with no subject
line. Tonight, lying awake with a woman's name circling in my head
that has nothing to do with the roof at all, I notice, for the first
time in longer than I can place, that the tightness isn't there. Just
the ordinary nerves of having said something reckless out loud in a
committee room, and underneath it, something else entirely, that I
don't yet have a name for and am not in any hurry to find one for
tonight.

## Chapter Nine — Levels

I bring tea down to the overnight desk on the Tuesday, unasked, which
I have never once done for anyone in this building I wasn't
technically managing at the time.

It isn't a large gesture. I want to be accurate about the scale of it,
because in the retelling it's the kind of thing that gets inflated —
Priya, later, will tell this story as though I'd walked down there
with a violin section. It's a mug of tea, milk, no sugar, because I've
watched her drink it that way for three years without once asking if
that was actually how she took it, which turns out, happily, to have
been a correct guess rather than an intrusive one.

Nadia looks at the mug, then at me, in the manner of a woman
recalculating a fact she thought was settled.

"You don't have to do that," she says.

"I know."

"I didn't ask you to."

"I know that too." I put the mug down on the one clear patch of desk
that isn't covered in cue sheets or the specific brand of energy drink
she pretends not to have a preference for. "It's tea. It's not a
grand gesture. Please don't make it into one."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were about to say something about it being unnecessary."

"I was," Nadia admits, "and now I'm not going to, because you've
pre-empted it, which is its own kind of irritating."

I sit on the spare stool — there's always a spare stool at the
overnight desk, for reasons nobody's ever explained to me, possibly
just institutional memory of a producer role that hasn't existed since
before I started here — and watch her work for a minute, the way I've
started doing more evenings than I'd admit to anyone directly.

"For the record," she says, eventually, not looking up from the desk,
"that ran fifty-five seconds over a fifty-four-second cue."

I know exactly what she means, and she knows I know, and there is a
whole conversation compressed into that one flat sentence about a
kiss that happened four days ago that neither of us has mentioned
since.

"For the record," I say back, and put the mug down properly this
time, and don't say anything else, because I have learned, in four
weeks, that with her the silence after a true sentence is the part
that actually counts. Filling it would be, I am starting to
understand, its own kind of lie.

She goes back to the fader. I stay a minute longer than I strictly
need to, ostensibly checking a rundown that does not, in fact,
require checking, mostly because leaving now would mean admitting that
I came down here for more than the tea and I'm not ready to admit
that, not even to myself, not with the words fully formed.

Somewhere in that minute, a level she's riding on the desk holds a
beat longer than the meter strictly needs — a half-second where her
hand could have moved and simply doesn't — and she looks up at exactly
the same moment I do, and for a second neither of us is doing anything
that could be described, in any professional sense, as work.

Neither of us says one further word about it. But something between
us has moved — I'd swear to it in any register she'd accept — and both
of us know it moved, flat-footed and entirely unwilling, yet, to call
it by its name. I finish the rundown check I didn't need to do, and
say goodnight, and walk home in the specific quiet that comes after a
thing has shifted and neither party has said so out loud, which is, I
am coming to learn, the exact temperature at which this entire
relationship seems determined to run.

Priya clocks the spare stool the following week — notices, with the
specific forensic attention of a teenager who has appointed herself
the building's unofficial intelligence service, that it has moved
six inches closer to the main desk than its official position and has
stayed there for four consecutive nights.

"I'm not going to say anything," she announces, apropos of nothing, in
the kitchen, holding a mug she has not yet filled with anything.

"Good."

"I'm just going to note, for the record, that the stool has moved."

"Noted."

"And that it keeps not moving back."

"Priya."

"I'm not saying anything," she says again, with enormous satisfaction,
and fills her mug, and leaves me standing there wondering exactly how
much of what I think is private is actually, in this building, a
matter of public record.

·

I try, that same week, to have an honest conversation with myself
about what exactly I think I'm doing, and manage about four minutes
of it before I give up and go and check on the roof instead.

Four minutes is enough, though, to establish the basic shape of the
problem. I am supposed to be running a fake relationship for the
benefit of a licensing panel. I have, somewhere in the last two weeks,
started bringing a woman tea she didn't ask for, noticing which stool
she prefers, and lying awake reverse-engineering the exact temperature
of a look that lasted less than a second. None of that is in the
format. None of that is, strictly, my job. And yet I find, standing in
the corridor with BIG LEAK dripping steadily into its bucket, that I
have absolutely no interest in stopping any of it, which is either the
best or the worst piece of information I've gathered about myself all
year, and I genuinely can't yet tell which.

·

I ring my sister that evening, mostly out of the specific unease that
comes from having noticed something about yourself you don't quite
know what to do with. Fiona — the real one, my actual sister, not the
one on my midnight candidate list and not the caller from broadcast
three either, a coincidence of names I've stopped finding funny by now
— lives in Aberdeen with two children and a husband who does something
with oil rigs that neither of us has ever fully understood, and rings
me back within the hour, which is unusual for her, and immediately
suspicious.

"You never ring on a Tuesday," she says. "What's happened."

"Nothing's happened."

"Robyn."

"I think I might be developing feelings," I say, in the flattest
voice I can manage, "for the woman I'm pretending to date for a
funding panel."

There's a pause on the line long enough that I can hear her muting
the television.

"That's the best thing you've said to me in about four years," Fiona
says, delighted, entirely unhelpful. "Tell me everything."

I don't tell her everything. I tell her enough — the format, the fire
escape, the tea, the stool — and she laughs at exactly the right
points and goes quiet at exactly the right points too, and by the end
of the call I feel, if not resolved, at least considerably less alone
with the specific unease of the thing, which turns out to be most of
what I actually needed.

"Can I say one thing before you go," Fiona says, "as your older
sister, with all the authority that comes with three whole years of
seniority?"

"Go on."

"You've spent your whole adult life being the reliable one. The one
who stays, who covers, who doesn't ask for anything because asking
means someone might say no. I've watched you do it since you were
nineteen, and I've never once said anything, because it seemed to be
working for you, in its own grim way." A pause, the specific one that
means she's about to say something she's been saving up. "This is the
first time in years you've rung me sounding like you actually wanted
something for yourself, rather than reporting on how well you were
managing everyone else's. I just wanted to say I noticed, and I'm
glad, and you don't have to manage this one, Robyn. You're allowed to
just want it."

I don't have a response to that which doesn't risk my voice giving out
entirely, so I just say, "thank you," and mean it more than the two
words can hold, and we say goodnight, and I sit for a while afterward
in a flat that feels, for the first time in longer than I can
remember, less like a place I've arranged to survive being alone in
and more like somewhere I might actually want to be found.

·

Priya's having a rough week at school, it turns out, though it takes
me three days of noticing she's shorter-tempered than usual before I
actually ask about it rather than simply working around it.

"Highers," she says, when I finally ask, in the specific clipped tone
of someone who would rather not be discussing this at all. "Everyone's
decided this is the year that determines the rest of my life, which is
an enormous amount of pressure to put on a set of exams I'm sitting in
a building that smells permanently of the chip fryer next door."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It is exhausting. Mum's doing nights at the QE, so I'm on my own for
the actual revising, and school keeps sending letters home about
*application strategy* that Mum doesn't have time to read before her
shift, so I end up reading them myself and pretending to her that I've
got it handled, because she's got enough on without also worrying about
university admissions timetables."

I don't say anything straight away, because I recognise, uncomfortably,
a version of my own nineteen-year-old self in that sentence — a
teenager quietly managing the adult-shaped gaps in her own life so
efficiently that nobody around her ever has to notice there were gaps
at all.

"You don't have to handle it alone," I say, eventually. "I mean that
practically, not just kindly. I can read a UCAS form. I've read
considerably worse paperwork for this station over the years."

Priya looks at me for a long moment, the specific wary look of someone
who isn't used to being offered help without having to ask for it
first, loudly, several times.

"You'd actually do that?"

"I'd actually do that."

"Okay," she says, in a smaller voice than her usual one, and pulls a
crumpled letter out of her bag that has clearly been read and reread
and folded back up several times without ever quite being dealt with,
and slides it across the desk toward me, and we spend the next forty
minutes going through it properly, line by line, until the whole thing
looks considerably less like a mountain and considerably more like a
form, which is, in the end, all it ever actually was.

"Thanks," Priya says, at the end of it, not looking up from the now
much less crumpled letter. "For not making a whole thing of it."

"I'm not going to make a whole thing of it. I'm just going to keep
asking, from now on, when you're having a rough week, instead of
waiting for you to tell me. That's not a whole thing. That's just
paying attention properly, which I should have been doing already."

She doesn't say anything else about it, but she leaves the letter on
the desk rather than folding it back into her bag, which I understand,
without either of us naming it, to be its own kind of thank you.

·

Nadia catches the tail end of the UCAS conversation from the overnight
desk, close enough to have heard most of it, and doesn't say anything
until Priya's gone home for the night.

"You're good with her," she says, simply, cleaning down the desk for
the evening.

"I try to be."

"It's not trying. I've watched people try to be good with teenagers.
It usually looks like performing patience at them. What you just did
was actually listen to what she needed and give her exactly that,
without making it about how generous you were being for offering."

"She reminds me of myself, a bit. At that age."

"I know," Nadia says. "I've noticed. I think it's why you're good at
it — you're not guessing what a seventeen-year-old in that situation
needs, you're remembering." She pauses, considering something. "Were
you close with anyone, at her age? An adult who did for you what
you're trying to do for her?"

I think about the question properly before answering, because it's a
fair one and deserves more than a reflexive deflection.

"Malcolm, a bit," I say, eventually. "Not in the way you mean — he
wasn't warm, exactly, he's never been warm. But he never once treated
me like I was too young for the job, which turned out to matter more
than warmth would have. Nobody else really stepped in. My mum was
doing her best with what she had, which wasn't much spare capacity by
the time I was Priya's age. I mostly worked it out myself, which I
suppose is exactly why I don't want Priya to have to."

"That's not a small thing you're doing for her."

"It doesn't feel like much. It's just paying attention."

"That's the whole of what most people never get," Nadia says, "so I
wouldn't undersell it," and goes back to the desk, and I let the
compliment sit rather than deflecting it, which is, this month,
becoming something of a small personal project of its own.

·

Priya finishes the UCAS form properly a few days later, and brings it
to me for a final look before she submits it, which I understand,
watching her hover in the office doorway rather than simply handing it
over, to be its own small act of trust rather than a mere formality.

"It's good," I tell her, reading it through properly. "Better than
good, actually. Your personal statement's the best piece of writing
I've seen out of this building in years, and I include several grant
applications I've personally laboured over in that assessment."

"You're just saying that."

"I'm not just saying that. I don't just say things, Priya, you've told
me so yourself, repeatedly, usually while criticising my handwriting."

She almost smiles at that, ducking her head to hide it, and takes the
form back with visibly more confidence than she brought it in with.

"I'm putting media studies first choice," she says, "but I've put a
business course second, in case anyone official ever asks me to prove
I've thought about *contingencies*." She says the word with the exact
weary distaste Kirsty reserves for panel jargon, and I laugh, properly,
recognising immediately where she's picked up the inflection.

"You sound like Kirsty."

"I sound like someone who's spent four years listening to every adult
in this building complain about bureaucratic language," Priya
corrects. "I'm allowed to have opinions about contingencies. I've
earned them."

She submits the form that evening, from the good chair, with the
specific ceremony of someone marking a genuine occasion, and texts the
group chat a single word — *DONE* — that gets more replies, faster,
than almost anything else posted there all autumn.

·

Nadia buys her a small bar of chocolate the next day, unprompted,
leaving it on the desk with no note, which Priya finds and holds up,
baffled.

"Is this from you?"

"It's from whoever left it there," Nadia says, entirely unbothered,
not looking up from the fader, and Priya, recognising immediately that
this is as much acknowledgement as she's going to get, eats the
chocolate at the desk with the specific quiet satisfaction of someone
who understands, without needing it spelled out further, exactly what
kind of currency this building actually trades in.

"You're both terrible at expressing things directly," she informs us,
"and I find it extremely charming, which I want on the record as a
begrudging compliment rather than an invitation to keep doing it."

"Noted," I say, and Nadia, for her part, says nothing at all, simply
goes back to the desk with the specific satisfied stillness of a woman
who has said everything she needed to say in the form of one small bar
of chocolate, left without ceremony, exactly where it would be found.

## Chapter Fifteen — Off the Air, For Real

Broadcast four is, by now, brief enough on the page that I could tell
you the whole of it in one sentence: a chores argument, mediated with
real warmth, no stunt beat, no near miss, both of us genuinely good at
this in a way that has stopped requiring engineering at all.

That, if I'm honest, is what frightens me about it. The show has
started running itself. A listener writes in asking where we got
engaged, and neither of us has an answer prepared, and we improvise
past it, badly, live, laughing so hard Priya has to fade the sting in
over the top of us.

"You realise," Priya says afterward, headset around her neck, "that
you don't have an engagement story because you're not actually
engaged, and also that you both completely forgot that mid-broadcast,
which is either extremely good acting or something I'm not going to
say out loud because I value my job."

"Say it," Nadia says.

"I'm not saying it."

"Priya."

"I have chosen," Priya says, with enormous dignity, "not to editorialise
about my employer's love life a second time this month," and picks up
a mic stand and goes, leaving the two of us to sit with the fact that
neither of us has corrected her.

·

Nadia's flat, later, on an evening neither the panel nor the rota has
any claim on: a record player going quietly in the corner, a kettle
that takes even longer than the station's, no cameras, no cue sheet,
nothing between us but the actual room.

"Can I ask you something," I say.

"You're always allowed to ask."

"If I wanted to do that again — the thing from the first broadcast.
Not for the show. Not at fifty-four minutes. Just because I wanted to
— would that be alright?"

I ask it plainly, because the terms she gave me on a cue sheet in week
one were scoped to the segment, and I am not going to assume for one
second that they cover this, whatever this is turning into.

Nadia takes the question exactly as seriously as she's taken every
question I've ever asked her, which is to say, entirely. She looks at
me for a long moment, thinking it through properly, the way she thinks
through everything, and when she answers it's with a new kind of yes —
private, hers to give, belonging to nobody's rundown but ours.

"Yes," she says. "Not for the show."

"Not for the show," I agree.

The kiss that follows doesn't hit a cue. Nobody's counting the seconds.
It isn't over in fifty-five, because there's no clock in the room at
all, and afterward Nadia laughs — once, low, entirely unguarded, a
sound I have never once heard her make on air or off — and neither of
us reaches for a broadcast link to deflect with, because for the first
time there isn't one available, and instead of that being a problem it
is, quietly, an enormous relief.

It is, without any real competition, the best evening either of us
can currently name.

We stay up long past any sensible hour, the record player having
worked through whatever was on it twice, talking in the loose,
unstructured way of two people with nowhere they need to be and no
rundown to hit. She tells me about growing up in a flat two streets
from where she lives now, one of five children, all of them loud
except her, which she says like it's a small joke rather than the
central fact of a whole childhood spent finding one quiet job in a
loud house and keeping it. I tell her about running the graveyard-shift
show at twenty-two and falling asleep, once, on air, for four minutes,
mid-song, and waking up to find the phone lines had lit up with
listeners checking I was alright rather than complaining, which was
the exact moment I understood what this job could actually be for.

"You've never told anyone that," Nadia says. Not a question.

"No."

"Why me?"

"Because you'd ask a follow-up question instead of just laughing," I
say, "and you did," and she looks, for a second, entirely undone by
that, in a way I file away to think about for a long time afterward.

"What was the question you actually wanted someone to ask," she says,
after a while, "back then. At twenty-two. Not what happened next in
the story. What you actually wanted to be asked."

I think about it properly, because she's asked it properly, and it
takes me longer to answer than I expect it to.

"Whether I was lonely," I say, eventually. "Nobody ever asked that. Not
my mum, because she had enough of her own to carry. Not Kirsty, because
we were always too busy being brilliant together to talk about the
version of me that existed outside the two of us. I don't think I'd
have known how to answer it properly even if someone had asked. But I
wanted someone to ask."

"Were you? Lonely?"

"Constantly," I say, and it's the first time I've said the word out
loud about myself in what feels like a decade, "and extremely good at
making sure nobody could tell."

Nadia doesn't say anything clever back to that. She just moves closer
on the sofa, close enough that our shoulders touch, and stays there,
which is, I am beginning to understand, her actual answer to almost
everything that matters — not a sentence, but a presence, offered
without conditions, for exactly as long as it's wanted.

We fall asleep there, eventually, sitting up, which is a genuinely
terrible way to sleep and neither of us moves to fix it, and I wake at
some point in the small hours with a crick in my neck and her head on
my shoulder and the record player still turning, silently, on a
groove that finished hours ago, and lie there for a long while not
moving, not wanting to be anywhere else in the world badly enough to
risk waking her.

She wakes properly around four, disoriented for a second in the way
people are when they've fallen asleep somewhere they didn't plan to,
and looks at me, and at the room, and at the record player still
spinning its silent groove, and says, quietly, "we should move to a
bed at some point. In theory."

"In theory."

Neither of us moves. We stay exactly where we are until the light
outside starts doing its grey September thing at the edges of the
curtains, and it is, of all the mornings in this strange autumn, the
one I'd choose to keep if I could only keep one — nothing dramatic
happening in it at all, just two people, a stiff neck, and a record
that finished hours ago and nobody minding in the slightest.

·

We do, eventually, make it to an actual bed, sometime past six, and
sleep properly for a few hours, and I wake at half nine to the
specific domestic strangeness of a flat that isn't mine, sunlight
coming in at an angle I don't recognise, and Nadia already up, standing
at the window with two mugs of tea, watching the rain that's started
again in earnest.

"You're doing the thing where you watch me sleep," I say, not
entirely awake yet.

"I'm doing the thing where I make tea and it happens to take place
near a window you're also in," Nadia says, entirely unbothered, and
hands me a mug, milk, correct amount, no sugar, which she's clearly
made from memory rather than asking, and something about that small
unremarkable accuracy undoes me slightly more than the whole of the
previous evening did.

"Can I tell you something," she says, sitting on the edge of the bed,
"that I haven't fully worked out how to say properly yet, so it might
come out wrong."

"Go on."

"I've spent three years making sure nobody got close enough to matter.
Not out of nothing — out of the thing I still haven't told you the
whole of. And I want you to know that this," she gestures, vaguely, at
the room, the bed, the two mugs, "isn't nothing to me. It's the first
time in three years I've let it be anything at all. I don't entirely
know what to do with that, so I'm just going to say it plainly and let
you do whatever you want with the information."

"I'm not going to do anything with it except be glad you said it."

"That's a very restrained response."

"I've had a lot of practice being restrained," I say. "I'm choosing,
this once, not to manage this into something smaller than it is."

Nadia looks at me for a long moment, then, and something in her face
settles — not relief exactly, but close to it, the specific look of
someone who has said a frightening true thing and found the room still
standing afterward.

"Good," she says, simply, and drinks her tea, and we sit there for a
while longer, in a flat that isn't mine, in a morning that belongs
entirely to the two of us, with nothing on the agenda for once except
each other.

·

We do, eventually, have to leave the flat and rejoin the actual day,
and on the walk to the station Nadia stops, halfway across the humped
bridge, and looks down at the Kelvin running fast and brown below us
after the night's rain.

"I want to tell you something else," she says, "while I've still got
the nerve from this morning left in me. Not the whole of it. Just
enough that you'll understand why some evenings I go quiet in a way
that isn't about you."

"Okay."

"The podcast wasn't just about a breakup, packaged up neatly with a
beginning and an end," she says, carefully, watching the water rather
than me. "It was about someone deciding my actual grief over losing
that relationship was interesting content. Twelve episodes of it,
turned into a story with an arc, a title, a strings-heavy underscore —
and strangers messaging me for months afterward to say how *moved*
they'd been by my pain, like it was a performance I'd given rather than
a thing that had actually happened to me, live, in a life I hadn't
agreed to hand over."

I go very still, because I can hear, underneath the careful phrasing,
the shape of something considerably larger than I'd understood the
podcast to be about, and I don't ask the obvious next question, because
I can see, in the set of her shoulders, that she's given me exactly as
much as she can manage on a bridge on a Tuesday morning.

"Thank you for telling me that much," I say instead.

"That's the whole of it, for today," Nadia says, and there's something
almost apologetic in it, which I hate, immediately, on her behalf.

"You don't owe me the rest on a schedule."

"I know," she says. "I just wanted you to have that piece properly,
rather than guessing at the shape of it from what I don't say." She
looks up from the water, finally, and something in her face has
loosened slightly, the specific relief of a person who's handed over a
weight and found the other person still standing there afterward,
unchanged, willing to keep carrying whatever comes next alongside her.

We walk on to the station without saying anything further about it,
and I hold the new piece of information carefully, the way you'd hold
something breakable you've only just been trusted with, turning it
over gently rather than examining it too hard, grateful, mostly, that
she chose to hand it to me at all.

·

I don't tell anyone what she's told me, not Priya, not Fiona, not
anyone, and the not-telling costs me something, because I've spent this
whole autumn slowly unlearning the habit of carrying things alone. But
this one isn't mine to lighten by sharing. It's hers, on loan to me,
and the least I can do with it is keep it exactly where she put it.

I do notice, though, in the days that follow, a small shift in how I
listen to her — less waiting for the next fact, more simply being
present for whatever she's willing to say, in whatever order it
arrives. When she goes quiet mid-sentence now, I don't fill the gap
with a question. I just let the gap exist, the way she's taught me to
let most gaps exist, and find, more often than not, that she finishes
the sentence herself, eventually, once the silence has done whatever
work silence does that a question never quite manages.

"You've stopped asking follow-up questions," she says, one evening,
noticing the change before I've consciously registered making it.

"Should I be asking them?"

"No," Nadia says. "I just wanted to say I've noticed. It's a good
change. Most people think listening means waiting for their turn to
ask something. You've started actually just listening instead."

"I had a good teacher."

"You did," she agrees, without any false modesty about it, which I've
come to find, oddly, one of the most reassuring things about her —
she takes credit for the things she's actually good at, plainly, the
same way she states every other fact, and somehow that plainness makes
the compliment land more than any amount of deflection would have.

·

I ring Fiona that same evening, mostly to tell her about the record
player and the stiff neck and the whole unremarkable, extraordinary
morning after, and she listens all the way through without once
interrupting, which is, for Fiona, its own small miracle.

"That's the one," she says, when I've finished. "The morning with the
record player. That's the one you'll still be telling people about in
twenty years, long after you've forgotten most of the actual broadcast
segments."

"How do you know that?"

"Because it's the one where nothing happened, and you're still
telling me about it three separate times in one phone call, which
means it mattered more than any of the ones where something did." A
pause, warmer. "Those are always the ones that last, in my experience.
Not the big dramatic nights. The ordinary mornings where you realise,
quietly, that you'd rather be exactly there, doing exactly nothing,
than anywhere else on earth doing anything more impressive."

I don't have anything to add to that, so I let her be right about it,
the way I'm learning to let the people who love me be right about
things more often than I used to, and we talk a while longer about
nothing much at all, the ordinary furniture of two sisters staying
close across a few hundred miles, before ringing off, leaving me
sitting with the whole of that morning a little longer, exactly where
I want it.

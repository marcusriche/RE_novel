## Chapter Seven — Sound Check

An hour to air, and Priya is running the desk like a small furious
adult who has finally, after four years of being handed jobs nobody
else wanted, been handed the one she actually wants.

"Rundown," she says, sliding a page across without looking up from her
headset. "Three pre-screened callers. Two stings. One planned—" she
glances at it, glances at me, glances away again, in the specific
manner of a seventeen-year-old declining to editorialise about her
employer's fake love life "—beat, at fifty-four minutes."

"Thank you, Priya."

"I didn't write the beat in. I found it already written in when I
opened the doc. I want that on the record."

I catch my own reflection in a dead monitor on the way to the booth —
a version of my face I do not recognise, nervous in a way that has
nothing, structurally, to do with a fake segment — and don't have time
to do anything with that information before Nadia's voice comes
through the talkback, flat and certain.

"Levels are good," she says. "So are you."

She's gone back to the fader before I can do anything at all with that
sentence, which is, I am coming to understand, precisely her method:
say the true thing once, cleanly, and get out before either of us has
to look directly at it.

·

On the Level goes out live for the first time at seven o'clock on a
Thursday, and it is, against every reasonable expectation I had walked
in with, good.

Not good like a fake thing performing competently. Good like an actual
show.

The first caller is a man called Grant from Maryhill whose partner
keeps rearranging the cutlery drawer without warning him, which sounds
like nothing until Grant starts talking and it becomes clear the
cutlery drawer is standing in, very obviously, for something much
larger about feeling consulted. Nadia takes the call, not me — she
leans into the second mic like she's done it a hundred times, which
as far as anyone at Kelvin Row knows she never has — and asks him,
flatly, "does she tell you why, or does she just do it," and the whole
studio goes quiet in the specific way a room goes quiet when somebody
has, by accident, asked exactly the right question.

Grant says she just does it.

"Ask her why once," Nadia says, "properly, not as an accusation. If
she can't tell you, that's the actual problem and it isn't the
drawer."

I have to look down at the rundown to stop myself staring at her.

The second caller is a woman called Debs who wants to know whether
it's normal to still fancy your partner of eleven years so much it's
distracting at work, which I field myself, badly, laughing so hard on
air that Priya has to cut to a sting early — an actual real laugh that
nearly costs us a full five seconds of dead air and somehow, judging
by the messages that come in afterward, makes the segment better
rather than worse. Debs, it turns out, is delighted rather than
offended, and texts in afterward to say so, which becomes, without
either of us planning it, the first entry in what Priya will later
start calling *the wall of fame* — a corkboard by the kettle, covered
in printed-out messages from listeners who liked something enough to
say so twice.

The third caller asks about anniversaries, and Nadia answers with four
words that land better than anything I'd scripted, and I catch myself,
mid-link, thinking: *this is not what I expected fake to feel like.*

At fifty-four minutes, on cue, we do the thing we agreed on the fire
escape.

It's scripted. It's consented to, off mic, the night before, in the
rain, in words. It's supposed to last about a second and a half,
because that's what the cue calls for, a neat, broadcastable, funny
little button on the hour.

It runs longer than that. Not dramatically — nobody listening would
clock it, I don't think, though I'll wonder about that for days — but
long enough that neither of us corrects it in the room, long enough
that when we come apart there's a half-second where neither of us says
anything at all, on air or off, which for two people whose entire
professional lives are built around never letting dead air sit is its
own kind of confession.

We finish the segment. We do the outro. We are, professionally, fine.

Priya, in the booth, has gone entirely still, headset around her
neck, watching the two of us through the glass with an expression I
can't fully read from where I'm standing — somewhere between
delighted and slightly alarmed, the specific look of a seventeen-year-
old who has just watched two adults do something considerably more
significant than either of them appears to have noticed themselves
doing.

She doesn't say anything over the talkback. She just presses the
button for the closing sting, and lets the segment end properly, and
saves whatever she's thinking for the corridor, which turns out to be
the correct professional call, and also, I'd guess, the kindest one.

·

Backstage — if you can call a corridor with three buckets in it
backstage — we talk very fast about everything except the thing that
just happened.

"The second caller's story really landed," I say.

"The stings were tight," Nadia says.

"Priya's timing on the cut was—"

"Excellent," Nadia agrees, and does the outro voice one more time,
entirely unnecessarily, as a joke that is also, very obviously, a way
of not saying anything else, and I let her, because I am doing the
exact same thing from the other side of the same silence.

Priya watches the two of us do this — watches us talk very seriously
about sting placement for a full ninety seconds without once
mentioning the actual final ninety seconds of the broadcast — and
says nothing at all, which from a seventeen-year-old who has said
plenty about everything else this week is, itself, a form of
commentary I choose not to examine too closely tonight.

Neither of us says the word *kiss* out loud, not once, the entire
evening. I go home and lie in the dark running it over anyway, on
repeat, the extra half-second of it, looking for the seam where the
performance ended and whatever came after started, and not finding one,
which is exactly the problem.

Priya texts the group chat at half eleven — a chat that currently
contains me, Priya, and Nadia, and which Priya has, without any
consultation whatsoever, renamed KELVIN ROW: OPERATION COUPLE — with a
single message: *that was so good I forgot to be embarrassed for both
of you.* Nadia doesn't reply. I don't either, though I look at it
four separate times before I put the phone face-down on the nightstand
and make myself, unconvincingly, go to sleep.

·

I run the whole broadcast again in my head the next morning, on the
walk in, the way you replay a good exam once it's safely behind you,
checking each answer for the mistake you're sure must be hiding
somewhere in it.

I can't find one. That's what unsettles me most, if I'm honest. Six
weeks ago I couldn't have told you what Nadia Quinn's voice sounded
like doing anything other than reading back a cue in the flattest
register available. Now I can replay, at will, the specific warmth she
put into four words about anniversaries, a warmth that wasn't in the
script and wasn't, as far as I could tell, performed for anyone's
benefit but the caller's.

I mention none of this to her when I arrive. She's at the desk, one
cup on and one off, running the overnight logs with her usual
unhurried thoroughness, and when she says good morning it sounds
exactly like every other good morning she's ever given me, which I
find, this particular Friday, almost unbearably reassuring and almost
unbearably disappointing in roughly equal measure.

"Numbers are good," she says, without looking up, sliding a printed
sheet across the desk. "Better than good."

I look at the sheet. She's right. She's always right. I am beginning
to suspect this is simply going to be a permanent feature of my life
from here on, and finding, to my own considerable surprise, that I
don't mind it in the slightest.

·

Douglas rings that same Friday, which nobody in the building
particularly wants, and asks, in the specific tone of a man reading
from a checklist he didn't write and doesn't fully understand, whether
he might "pop down and observe the format in situ" ahead of the next
funding cycle.

"He wants to watch us on air," Priya says, relaying the message with
the specific relish of someone delivering bad news she personally
finds entertaining. "Like we're a nature documentary."

"When."

"Next Thursday. He's bringing a clipboard. I know this because he
said the word *clipboard* out loud on the phone, unprompted, as though
it were a credential."

Nadia takes the news with her usual flatness, though I notice, for the
first time, something that might be the faintest trace of unease
behind it — gone again almost before I've registered it, filed away
under the growing list of things about her I'm learning to notice
rather than ask about outright.

"Does it change anything," she asks, "having him in the room?"

"It shouldn't."

"That's not an answer to whether it does."

I think about it properly, because she's asked properly, and because
by now I know better than to give her a managed version of a true
thing. "It changes how much I'll be performing rather than just doing
the job. Which is exactly the problem Priya keeps telling us not to
have."

"Then don't perform it," Nadia says, simply, as though the solution
were always this obvious and I'd simply been too busy dreading the
visit to see it. "Do the show. Let him watch the show happen. If the
show's good, it doesn't need help pretending to be good in front of
him specifically."

I hold onto that all week, the way I hold onto most of the things she
says that sound, on first hearing, like they ought to be simpler than
they turn out to be in practice — because Thursday arrives, and
Douglas does bring the clipboard, exactly as threatened, and sits in
the corner of the booth in a coat he does not remove for the entire
hour, writing things down at intervals that correspond to absolutely
nothing happening on air, in a way that makes Priya, watching him
through the glass, mouth the word *why* at me twice without sound.

We do the show anyway. We don't perform it for him. By the end of the
hour he's put the clipboard away entirely and is simply listening,
coat still on, in the specific stillness of a man who came in expecting
to tick boxes and has, instead, without quite meaning to, been drawn
into an actual broadcast.

"That was," he says afterward, in the foyer, visibly hunting for the
correct bureaucratic register and, for once, not quite finding it,
"rather good, actually. I'll say so in my notes."

"Thank you, Douglas."

"Don't let it go to your head," he adds, recovering some of his usual
stiffness on the way out the door, and I don't tell him that it isn't
his approval I'm interested in holding onto, though I suspect, from
the look Nadia gives me through the glass, that she's worked that out
already without needing to be told.

·

Priya debriefs the whole visit afterward with the specific relish of a
seventeen-year-old who has just watched an adult fail to be
intimidating and wants the moment properly documented for posterity.

"He wrote down 'clipboard' as a word," she reports, "and then, ten
minutes later, wrote down nothing at all for the rest of the hour,
which I am officially logging as a win, possibly the biggest win this
building has had against Douglas specifically in the whole four years
I've worked here."

"It's not a competition, Priya."

"Everything's a competition. That's the whole basis of local
government." She's already updating her spreadsheet with a new column,
unprompted, labelled DOUGLAS VISITS: WON. "I'm tracking it from now on.
I want a proper record for whenever the next renewal cycle comes round
and somebody claims we've never had positive engagement from a panel
member in person."

"You're remarkably invested in beating a man who's, structurally,
meant to be on our side."

"He's meant to be neutral," Priya corrects, with the specific precision
of someone who has clearly looked up the actual terms of reference for
the panel at some point out of pure spite. "Neutral isn't the same as
on our side. I intend to move him from neutral to actually on our side
by the end of the renewal cycle, and today's clipboard incident is
step one."

I don't ask what steps two through however-many are, mostly because I
suspect I'd find the answer both alarming and, in Priya's hands,
entirely likely to work.

·

Nadia and I stay late that same evening, going back over the broadcast
properly this time, without an audience or a corridor's worth of
adrenaline to rush us past the harder parts of the conversation.

"I want to ask you something," I say, "and I want you to know you
don't have to answer it if it's too much."

"Go on."

"That extra half-second. At fifty-four minutes. Was that you, or was
that both of us, or is that a question you don't actually have an
answer to either?"

Nadia considers it properly, the way she considers everything, turning
a fader idly without adjusting anything. "I don't have a clean answer,"
she says, eventually. "I know I didn't correct it. I know I could have.
I know that not correcting something is its own kind of decision, even
when it doesn't feel like one in the moment."

"That's not nothing."

"No," she agrees. "It's not nothing." She looks at me properly. "I
think we should probably talk about what it means. Not tonight. I'm not
ready tonight. But soon, before it becomes the kind of thing neither of
us can talk about because we've let too much silence build up around
it first."

"Soon," I agree, and it's enough, for tonight, to have named the thing
without yet having to solve it, the two of us sitting together in a
basement that smells of damp and old cable, letting a shared silence do
the work that neither of us is quite ready to hand to words yet.

·

I ring Fiona on the walk home, mostly to have somewhere to put the
particular restless energy of an evening that's ended in more questions
than answers.

"You sound unsettled," she says.

"I am, a bit. Good unsettled, I think. The kind where you've said a
true thing out loud and now you have to actually live with having said
it, rather than getting to take it back."

"That's usually the good kind," Fiona agrees. "The bad kind is when you
regret saying it. You don't sound like you regret it."

"I don't."

"Then you're fine," she says, with the specific certainty she reserves
for questions she considers already settled. "Go home. Sleep on it.
Whatever needs deciding will still need deciding tomorrow, and you'll
be better rested for it," and rings off, leaving me to finish the walk
home turning the evening over, more settled than I was five minutes
ago, exactly the way a good conversation with my sister generally
leaves me.

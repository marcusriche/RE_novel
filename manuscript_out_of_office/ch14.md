## Chapter Fourteen — Answering London

I write the email after hours, alone in the office, with the amber
bulb doing its unconvincing impression of red through the wall behind
me.

I write four drafts before I settle on one. The first is all
gratitude and hedging, the kind of email I could have sent five weeks
ago without changing a single fact about my actual life. The second is
too short, a single line, the sort of thing you send when you're
angry at the question rather than answering it. The third is,
horrifyingly, three paragraphs about the roof, as though the panel's
own admin had written it, which tells me everything about how much of
my personality is currently on loan to that building.

The fourth one I write properly — not out of the inertia that's kept
it sitting unopened for five weeks, but for real reasons, typed out in
full: the station. The roof, which is somehow both the smallest and
the largest reason available. Priya, who is seventeen and has nowhere
else that would let her run a Saturday shift like she owns it. Six
years of Thursdays that mean something to more people than a
spreadsheet can capture. And then, because I make myself, because the
sentence sits there on the screen and I let it, a reason I am not
ready to say out loud yet but am, apparently, ready to act on anyway.

I send it before I can revise it into something safer. My hand is
actually shaking a little when I close the laptop, which strikes me,
afterward, as an absurd amount of physiology for a job I'm turning
down rather than one I'm accepting.

Then I go and find Nadia, and I tell her the whole of it — the offer,
the five weeks it sat unanswered, the reminder I set myself and then
studiously ignored, the fact that answering it felt, the entire time,
like asking for something and risking being told no in a way I could
count, on a form, with a date on it.

"Five weeks," Nadia says, when I've finished. Not an accusation. Just
checking the number.

"Five weeks."

"That's a long time to carry a decision around without letting anyone
help you carry it."

"I know."

"Did you decide because of the station," she asks, "or because of
something else, and the station's just the part you're comfortable
saying out loud first?"

I don't answer that immediately, because it's the exact question I've
been avoiding asking myself, and hearing it said back to me, plainly,
in her voice, makes it considerably harder to keep avoiding.

"Both," I say, eventually. "The station's real. It's just not the
whole of it."

"Okay," Nadia says, and doesn't push for the rest, which — I'm coming
to understand — is the single most generous thing she knows how to
do.

She doesn't perform relief. She doesn't manage the moment for me, the
way I have spent my whole life managing moments for other people. She
just listens, all the way through, without once looking away, which
turns out to be its own kind of answer — better, if I'm honest, than
anything she could have said.

It's the first time in this whole strange arrangement that I've stated
a want out loud, plainly, to another person, without it being
necessary for a show, a panel, or anybody's engagement metric.

It does not feel like relief exactly. It feels like standing somewhere
higher up than I'm used to, with rather less to hold onto than I'd
like, and rather more room to actually see.

Kirsty texts two days later — a different register from the *just
checking in* of before, shorter, more careful. *Heard you turned down
London. Are you alright?* I stare at it for a while before I answer,
because it's the first time in eight years she's asked me a question
that isn't, in some fashion, about the panel or the format or the
renewal, and I don't quite know what to do with a Kirsty who's asking
after me rather than after the show.

*I'm good*, I write back, eventually. *Better than I've been in a
while, actually.* I don't say why. I'm not ready to, not with her, not
yet. But I mean every word of it, which is itself new enough to notice.

·

Priya finds out about the decision from the group chat, three hours
after Nadia does, and responds with the specific outrage of a
seventeen-year-old who considers herself entitled to first billing on
all major station news.

"You told *Nadia* before you told me?"

"I told Nadia in person. I told the group chat because I had to tell
someone before I lost my nerve entirely."

"That's not the same as telling me first."

"Priya, I run this building. I don't have to tell you things in a
particular order."

"You do, actually," she says, with the weary certainty of someone
citing an established statute. "I do the gossip *and* the desk. There's
a protocol."

I don't have an answer to that, and I don't try to construct one,
because there isn't a version of this conversation where I win it, and
because, if I'm honest, I don't especially want to. Being the subject
of Priya's outrage, this particular week, feels considerably better
than being the subject of nobody's attention at all, which is, more or
less, how most of my adult life has gone until now.

·

The recruiter from London rings, once, properly, rather than emailing,
a week after my answer's gone in, which I understand to be a genuine
attempt to talk me out of it rather than mere procedure.

"I want to be honest with you," she says, in the specific measured
tone of someone who has clearly rehearsed this call on her own bus.
"We don't usually follow up like this. But your reputation precedes
you, and I think you might be underestimating what you're turning
down."

"I don't think I am."

"The salary alone—"

"I know what the salary is," I say, gently, because she's only doing
her job and doesn't deserve the sharper version of this conversation.
"I've thought about it properly. It's not that I don't understand what
I'm turning down. It's that I've worked out, this year, what I'd be
turning down to take it, and the second column's heavier than the
first."

She doesn't have a counter for that, because it isn't the kind of
objection her script has an answer prepared for, and we end the call
politely, and I put the phone down feeling, for the first time since
the offer arrived, entirely and uncomplicatedly certain.

·

Priya asks me, a few days later, whether I regret it — the version of
the question a seventeen-year-old asks when she's genuinely curious
rather than fishing for reassurance, which is its own kind of
compliment.

"Not for a second," I say.

"Not even a little bit? It was a lot of money."

"It was a lot of money," I agree, "for a job that would have made me
someone else's version of myself, in a city where nobody knows this
building exists, running a schedule I didn't build for an audience I
didn't choose. I've spent nine years making this place mine. I wasn't
ready to trade that for a bigger office and a worse Tuesday."

"That's very poetic for someone who spends most of her time worrying
about buckets."

"I contain multitudes, Priya."

"You contain a leaking roof," Priya says, and goes back to her
laptop, and I let her have the last word, because it's a good one,
and because I've stopped needing to win every exchange with a
seventeen-year-old to feel secure in my own decisions.

·

Fiona rings that weekend to hear the whole story properly, having only
had the group chat's abbreviated version to work from, and makes me
tell it twice — once for the facts, once, in her words, "for how it
actually felt," which turns out to be the harder of the two tellings
by some distance.

"I'm proud of you," she says, once I've finished the second version,
in a tone she doesn't often use, because we're not, either of us, a
family that says things like that lightly. "Not for turning down the
money. For knowing what you actually wanted quickly enough to say no
before it had time to talk you round."

"It didn't feel quick. It felt like five weeks of staring at a
calendar reminder."

"Five weeks is quick, for you," Fiona says. "You've been not-deciding
things for considerably longer than that, historically. I still
remember you taking the better part of a year to admit you were
unhappy at the old flat before you finally moved."

"That's different."

"It's the same shape of thing, just a smaller decision." A pause,
gentler. "I think this Nadia's good for you, from what little I've
heard. Not because of the deciding-faster thing, though that's
something. Because you sound, on the phone, like someone who's
actually living her own life at the moment, rather than narrating it
to me after the fact once it's already safely over."

I don't have a response to that which doesn't risk giving away more
than I've fully worked out myself yet, so I just say, "I think she
might be," and let Fiona hear, in the way I say it, everything I'm not
yet ready to say in full sentences, and she, mercifully, lets that be
enough for one phone call.

·

Kirsty rings again, a few days after the London decision's settled
properly, this time about something else entirely — a genuinely
practical question about the transmitter contract, the kind of call
she'd have made to me routinely for years if either of us had ever let
our professional relationship stay merely professional — and at the end
of it, almost as an afterthought, she says, "for what it's worth, I
think you made the right call. On London. I nearly went, myself, when
they made me the same kind of offer, three years after I actually left
for the national job."

"You never told me that."

"There wasn't a version of telling you that didn't sound like I was
asking you to validate my own choices," Kirsty says. "I'm telling you
now because I think you've actually worked out something I still
haven't, eight years on, which is how to want the smaller, harder,
more local thing without treating it as a consolation prize."

"Is that what London would have been? A consolation prize?"

"No," Kirsty says, after a pause. "That's rather the problem. It would
have been genuinely good. That's what makes turning it down actually
brave, rather than merely comfortable. Anyone can turn down a bad
offer. It takes something else entirely to turn down a good one because
you've decided something smaller suits you better."

I don't have a ready response to that, and Kirsty, characteristically,
doesn't wait for one, moving briskly on to a genuinely dull point about
insurance clauses, and I let the conversation carry on in its ordinary,
practical register, grateful, in a way I don't examine too closely, for
the particular kindness of being complimented and then immediately
allowed to move past it without having to sit in the glow of it for
longer than is comfortable.

·

Nadia asks me, a few evenings later, what I'd have done if the answer
had been yes instead — if I'd taken the London job, packed up nine
years of buckets and rotas, and left the building to whoever came
after me.

"I don't know," I admit. "I hadn't let myself think that far into it,
because thinking that far into it would have meant admitting I was
genuinely considering it, rather than just going through the motions
of considering it before saying no."

"That's very you," Nadia says, without any particular edge to it. "Not
looking directly at the version of the decision that frightens you
most, in case looking at it makes it real."

"Is that a criticism?"

"It's an observation. I don't think you can help it yet. I think it's
something you're working on, the same way I'm working on not assuming
every door needs three conditions written on the back of a cue sheet
before I'll walk through it." She looks at me properly. "I just wanted
to say, since we're being honest about hypotheticals — if you had taken
it, I think I'd have understood. I wouldn't have liked it. But I'd have
understood, because I know what it is to almost take the safer,
bigger, further-away version of a life, and I'd never have held it
against you for choosing it."

"I didn't choose it."

"I know," Nadia says. "I'm glad. I just wanted you to know the door
wasn't closed on my end either way, because I don't want you thinking
you owe me the smaller life, as if staying were a debt you're paying
off rather than something you actually wanted for its own sake."

I think about that distinction for a long time afterward — staying as
a debt versus staying as a want — and find, turning it over, that she's
identified something I hadn't quite named for myself yet, the exact
line between the two that I've spent most of my adult life blurring
without ever noticing I was doing it.

·

I mention the conversation to Priya a few days later, in outline, not
the specifics, and she considers it with the specific gravity she
reserves for questions she's decided are genuinely worth her full
attention rather than the ones she just enjoys being clever about.

"I think," she says, eventually, "that most people who grow up having
to be reliable early — and I'd count myself in that category, for the
record, given my mum's shifts — end up thinking love and usefulness are
the same thing. Like the only way to deserve being kept around is to
keep being useful enough that nobody ever has a reason to reconsider."

"That's rather exactly it."

"I worked that out about myself a while ago," Priya says, with the
specific matter-of-factness of someone who has clearly given the
subject considerable private thought. "It's why I keep offering to
resign every time something goes wrong that isn't even my fault. I
think some part of me still believes I have to earn my place here
every single week, rather than just — having it, because people
actually want me around, not because I'm useful."

"You don't have to earn it."

"I know that now," Priya says. "You've told me that a few times this
autumn, and I'm choosing, slowly, to actually believe it rather than
just hear it. I think that's probably what you need to do too, with
whoever it is you're not naming just now. Believe you're wanted rather
than just useful. It's a slower thing to learn than it sounds."

I don't have a response that doesn't risk giving away more than I've
told her, so I just say, "that's very wise," and let her have the
moment, and think, walking home that evening, that I've apparently
built an entire building full of people quietly working out the same
lesson at their own pace, in their own time, together.

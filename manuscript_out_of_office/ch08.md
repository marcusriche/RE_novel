## Chapter Eight — The Numbers Come In

The email arrives at nine the next morning, from the panel's admin
address, not from Kirsty, which I notice and file away without knowing
what to do with it yet.

*Encouraging early engagement metrics*, it says, in the specific
bloodless prose of a person whose entire job is not committing to
anything in writing. In panel language, I am learning, that sentence
is roughly the temperature of a hug.

Overnight listenership is up — for a Thursday slot that used to run a
repeat of the shipping forecast to an audience of, as far as I've ever
been able to establish, one insomniac fisherman in Greenock. Priya has
already built a spreadsheet, unprompted, with the metric broken out by
quarter-hour, and there's a small, private satisfaction in watching a
seventeen-year-old take an institution's own language and turn it into
a weapon on our behalf.

I allow myself, for about ninety seconds, standing at the kettle
waiting for its own four-minute cycle, to believe this might actually
work — on every level I built it to, and on one I did not budget for
at all, and have no intention, this morning, of examining any further.

Priya finds me at the kettle and reads the email over my shoulder
without asking, which is a habit of hers I have long since given up
trying to correct.

"Encouraging," she reads aloud, in a voice that manages to make the
word sound like an accusation. "That's it? A whole show, live, unpaid
overtime for both of you, and the panel's response is *encouraging*?"

"It's better than what they usually say."

"What do they usually say?"

"Nothing. Usually there's just a longer silence before the next
review cycle."

Priya considers this. "So encouraging is good."

"Encouraging is very good."

"This building has extremely low standards for good," she says, not
unkindly, and goes to update her spreadsheet, and I stand at the
kettle a moment longer, watching the little orange light on it that
means it is, at long last, nearly done boiling, and think that she is
not wrong, and that I have spent six years making my peace with
exactly that fact.

·

I don't sleep properly that night, which is not, strictly, new — I
run a station on a leaking roof, sleep and I have an understanding —
but it's new in kind. I lie there reverse-engineering one extra
half-second of a scripted kiss the way I'd reverse-engineer a discrepancy
in the roof fund, running it for meaning that arithmetic was never
built to find.

I draft three texts to her about it. *Hey, so, about last night* — no.
*Not that it matters but* — worse. *Did you notice* — I delete that one
so fast I nearly drop the phone.

I send none of them. I lie in a flat I have arranged, very
deliberately, over several years, to be a place where being entirely
alone is comfortable — good light, one very good armchair, nothing on
the walls that requires explaining to a guest, because there are so
rarely guests to explain anything to — and find that tonight it is not
quite managing its usual trick, and I fall asleep still running the
sum, and get, as ever, no answer that holds up in daylight.

I wake at half six with the specific dread of a person who knows,
somewhere below conscious thought, that they are going to have to walk
into a basement in four hours and be normal at someone. I am not,
generally, someone who dreads seeing people. I have built an entire
professional identity around being extremely findable, extremely
available, always the one who picks up. This is new, and I don't like
it, and I get up and make tea I don't drink and go into work early
anyway, because the alternative is lying there letting the dread win,
and I have never once in my life let a feeling win a fight it hadn't
earned.

It turns out, walking down Dumbarton Road in the grey half-light,
that I needn't have dreaded it at all, because Nadia treats the
morning after exactly the way she treats every other morning: level,
unremarkable, precisely calibrated. She's at the desk when I arrive,
one cup on and one off, running through the overnight logs with the
specific unhurried thoroughness of someone who has never once in her
life let a broadcast go out without checking it twice, and she says
good morning to me the same way she's said it for three years,
without a flicker of anything I could point to and call a change.

I don't know, at the time, whether that's relief or its opposite. I
spend most of the walk home that evening deciding it's relief. I spend
most of the following morning deciding it might be the opposite. By
the time I've worked out which one it actually is, an entire week has
gone by and the question has, without my noticing, stopped mattering
nearly as much as I thought it would.

The wall of fame gets its second entry that Friday — a message from a
listener called Sandra who says she's been widowed three years and
that hearing two people argue gently about a cutlery drawer, live, at
seven o'clock on a Thursday, was the first time in a long while the
radio felt like company rather than noise. Priya reads it out loud in
the kitchen, and none of us says anything for a moment afterward,
because there isn't really anything to say to a sentence like that
except to go and make sure the next Thursday earns it.

·

I meet Sandra properly a few weeks later, entirely by accident, in the
queue at the good bakery on Byres Road, and only work out who she is
when she mentions, to the woman behind her, that she listens to "the
couple on Kelvin Row," in a tone that makes it sound like we're
practically celebrities, which we are, I suppose, to an audience of a
few hundred people who tune in on a Thursday specifically for us.

"You're the manager," she says, once she's clocked me properly, going
slightly pink. "Robyn. I recognise your voice."

"That's me."

"I hope you don't mind me saying — the show's got me through some
quite bad evenings this autumn. My husband used to fall asleep in the
chair by half seven most nights, near the end, and the flat's very
quiet without that particular kind of company. Your show fills it up
a bit."

I don't have a rehearsed response to that, which is, by now, becoming
something of a theme in my life. I just tell her, honestly, that it
means more to me than she probably realises, and mean it, and watch
her go off with her bread looking, I'd like to think, a fraction
lighter than she came in. It occurs to me, walking home, that this —
not the panel, not the renewal, not even Nadia, though certainly also
Nadia — is actually the whole point of the building I've spent nine
years keeping the lights on in.

·

I tell Nadia about Sandra that evening, expecting a small nod of
acknowledgement and nothing more, and instead she goes quiet for a
long moment, the specific quiet that means she's turning something
over properly.

"That's the thing nobody tells you about doing this job well," she
says, eventually. "You don't get to see most of it land. You put a
segment out into the dark and you hope it finds somebody, and
occasionally, if you're lucky, you meet them at a bakery and find out
it did. But most of the time you never know. You just keep doing the
work and trust that it's finding somebody, somewhere, even when you
can't see it."

"Is that hard? Not knowing?"

"It used to be," Nadia says. "I think I've made my peace with it,
mostly, by deciding that the not-knowing is actually the point. If I
needed to see the effect every time, I'd be doing it for the wrong
reason." She looks at me properly. "You met one Sandra today. There
are probably forty more out there we'll never meet, and that has to
be alright, or none of this is sustainable."

I think about that for the rest of the evening — about work you do
without ever seeing where it lands, and what it takes to keep doing it
anyway, faithfully, week after week, on the strength of forty
Sandras you'll never actually meet.

·

Kirsty comes down to the station in person that same week, rather than
sending an email or a text with a smiling emoji doing more work than
it should, and the difference in register is not lost on me — nor, I
suspect, on her.

"I wanted to see the numbers properly," she says, standing in the
foyer in a coat instead of her panel blazer, which I notice
immediately, the way I noticed the coat instead of the blazer once
before, at a coffee shop, eight years' worth of context attached to
the smallest sartorial choice. "Not the summary Douglas'll circulate.
The actual shape of it."

I show her Priya's spreadsheet, which has, by now, four tabs and a
colour-coding system Priya refuses to explain to anyone outside the
building. Kirsty studies it for longer than the numbers strictly
warrant, and I understand, watching her, that she isn't really reading
the data. She's using it as a reason to stand in this basement a
little longer than a five-minute professional visit requires.

"You built something real here," she says, eventually, not looking up
from the spreadsheet. "I mean that as a genuine observation, not panel
language. I sat through your first meeting fully expecting a fake
segment to look like a fake segment. It doesn't. Whatever's actually
happening on that mic, it's not what I expected to find when I took
this job."

"What did you expect to find?"

"Honestly? A station running on fumes and a manager too proud to say
so." She looks up properly now. "I've watched a lot of applicants over
the last year say the right things in the right order because they've
learned what the panel wants to hear. You've never once, in four
years, said the right thing in the right order. You just say the true
thing, badly timed, and somehow it keeps working anyway."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's an observation," Kirsty says, which I recognise, with some
amusement, as almost exactly Nadia's own preferred register, delivered
by a woman who couldn't be more different from her in every other
respect. "Take it as whichever one helps."

She leaves without asking to meet Nadia properly, which I notice
again, and don't yet know what to do with, filing it alongside the
other small facts I'm collecting about a woman who left this building
eight years ago and has, apparently, never quite stopped watching it
from wherever she landed.

·

I mention the visit to Nadia afterward, expecting the usual flat
non-reaction. Instead she's quiet for a moment, turning a fader idly.

"She still cares what happens to this place," Nadia says. "You know
that, don't you. Whatever else is going on with her. That wasn't a
panel visit. That was someone checking on something she still thinks
of as partly hers."

"I hadn't thought of it that way."

"You don't, generally, think of things that way until someone else
says them out loud," Nadia says, without any particular edge to it,
just an observation, the kind she's started making more often lately
about me specifically, and I let it sit rather than defending myself
against it, because by now I've learned that's usually the correct
response to a true thing said plainly.

·

I ring Fiona that evening to tell her about the Kirsty visit, mostly
because I've noticed, this month, that not telling her things has
started to feel like its own small dishonesty, a habit I'm trying,
slowly, to unlearn.

"She came down herself? Not an email?"

"In person. In a coat, not the blazer."

There's a pause on the line, the specific weighing pause Fiona does
when she's deciding how much to say. "That's not a professional visit,
Robyn. You know that, don't you. People don't drive across town in
their own coat to check a spreadsheet that could've been emailed."

"I know."

"Does it bother you? Nadia, I mean. Knowing Kirsty's still — whatever
she is. Orbiting."

I think about the question properly before I answer it, because it
deserves more than a reflexive no. "I don't think it does. Nadia
noticed before I said anything, and she wasn't bothered either, or if
she was, she didn't let it look like it. I think we've both got old
files we're carrying. Hers isn't mine to open. Mine isn't hers to
manage. We're just letting them both exist without either of us
needing the other one to solve it."

"That's remarkably mature," Fiona says, "for two people who started
this whole thing as a lie to a funding panel."

"I'm aware of the irony."

"Good," Fiona says. "Hold onto the awareness. It'll serve you well,"
and rings off with her usual efficiency, leaving me to sit with the
observation a while longer, turning it over the way I turn over most
of the things she says that turn out, on reflection, to be more useful
than they sounded in the moment.

·

Grant from Maryhill, our cutlery-drawer caller, rings in again a few
weeks later, unprompted, just to give an update nobody asked for, which
turns out to be exactly the kind of listener behaviour Priya's second
spreadsheet tab was built to celebrate.

"Took your engineer's advice," he tells me, live, delighted with
himself. "Asked her why, properly, like you said. Turns out she
rearranges the drawer when she's stressed about something else
entirely and doesn't want to talk about the something else. The drawer
was never the actual problem. It was just the only door she knew how
to knock on."

"How'd that conversation go?"

"Better than I expected. Worse than I hoped. Which I'm told," Grant
says, with the specific pride of a man repeating wisdom he's clearly
been turning over for weeks, "is usually about right, for the
conversations that actually matter."

I catch Nadia's eye through the glass while he's talking, and she
raises an eyebrow slightly — not quite a smile, but close enough that
I recognise it as one — because *the conversations that actually
matter usually land better than expected and worse than hoped* is,
almost word for word, something she said to a different caller weeks
ago, now travelling back to us secondhand, refracted through a
stranger's marriage, proof of exactly the kind of thing she once told
me you never get to see land, except this once, against the odds, we
did.

·

I tell Fiona about Grant's call that evening, mostly because it's the
kind of small, ordinary good news that doesn't survive being kept to
myself for very long these days.

"That's rather lovely," Fiona says. "A whole marriage, quietly
improved, because of a radio segment about a cutlery drawer."

"It wasn't really about the cutlery drawer."

"It never is, is it," Fiona says, with the specific warmth of someone
who's watched me build an entire career out of exactly that
principle. "I think that's the actual thing you've always been good
at, underneath the roofs and the rotas. Finding the real question
hiding inside the small one, and answering that instead."

I don't have a ready response to that, so I let her be right about it,
the way I'm slowly learning to let the people who know me best simply
be right, without needing to deflect it into something smaller first.

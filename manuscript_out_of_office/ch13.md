## Chapter Thirteen — The Voicemail

"That wasn't in the rundown," Nadia says, in the corridor, which from
her is practically a confession.

"No," I agree, and neither of us elaborates for a while. We stand
there not quite looking at each other, both aware that something has
shifted that a rundown can't route around anymore, however carefully
either of us tries.

Priya walks past with a mic stand under one arm. "You two are
insufferable," she says, with real, unmistakable affection, and keeps
walking, and neither of us corrects her.

"She's not wrong," Nadia says, once Priya's out of earshot.

"No," I agree.

"I keep waiting for one of us to say something about it. The four
seconds."

"Me too."

"Neither of us is going to, are we."

"Not tonight," I say, which isn't a no, and we both hear that it
isn't a no, and stand there in the corridor a moment longer before the
next thing on the rota calls one of us away, as it always, eventually,
does.

·

I find the box in the loft on the Sunday, looking for an old contract
of my own that turns out not to be in there at all — a stipend
agreement from my first year here, if you're curious, entirely
irrelevant, the kind of thing you decide you urgently need at eleven
on a Sunday morning specifically so you have a reason to be up a
loft ladder instead of doing anything that requires actual feeling —
and instead there's an answering machine tape with my mother's
handwriting on the label, unplayed since the funeral.

I sit up there for a while, cross-legged on loft insulation that
itches through my jeans, holding a cassette the size of a bar of soap
that contains, as far as I can work out, the last new recording of my
mother's voice that exists anywhere. She left it on the station's old
answerphone the week before she died, a message about nothing —
whether I'd remembered to renew the transmitter insurance, something
about the weather, ordinary Tuesday business — and somebody, at some
point, in some act of quiet kindness I've never properly thanked
anyone for, transferred it off the system and onto a cassette and
labelled it, in handwriting I don't recognise, MUM — DON'T DELETE.

My whole adult architecture, laid bare on one shelf: I became the
reliable one at nineteen because somebody had to be, and I have not
once, since, stopped long enough to grieve on anything resembling a
schedule. There was always a roof. There was always a rota. Grief, it
turns out, is remarkably easy to postpone indefinitely if you're
sufficiently good at being needed elsewhere.

I remember the week itself in fragments rather than a story — the
hospital corridor with the vending machine that only took exact
change, the specific way Priya's predecessor at the desk kept the
station running for nine days without once asking me to come in, the
funeral itself mostly a blur except for the very last thing, which was
walking back into the basement four days later because there was a
Thursday show that needed presenting and nobody else who could do it,
and discovering that doing the show was, in fact, easier than sitting
at home with the quiet. I have been discovering some version of that
same fact, on a loop, for six years. Work first. Feeling later, if at
all, on a schedule I control.

It has never once occurred to me, until this exact moment on a loft
ladder, cassette in hand, that *later* has simply never arrived, and
that I have built an entire life, a whole capable and well-regarded
life, around a debt to my own grief that I have quietly, competently,
never once paid.

I don't play the tape. I hold it for a while, and then I put it back
in the box, and I let myself cry about it for exactly four minutes,
with the timer on my phone running, because a timer is the only
permission structure I currently have access to.

I tell nobody. Not yet. I put the box back where it was and go back
downstairs to a rota that, as ever, needs me.

·

I think about the tape for the rest of the week, in the particular way
grief resurfaces once you've finally cracked the seal on it — not
constantly, not dramatically, just present, at odd moments, in the
gap between one task and the next. I catch myself, twice, reaching for
my phone to call her about something entirely ordinary — a broken
fader, a caller who reminded me of something she'd have laughed at —
before remembering, both times, with the same small fresh shock, that
there's nobody on the other end of that particular number any more.

I don't mention any of this to Nadia yet. It feels, somehow, too large
and too recent to hand over properly, and I've learned enough from her,
this month, to know the difference between sharing something and
simply offloading it onto the nearest available person. When I do tell
her, eventually, I want it to be because I've chosen to, not because
the grief got too heavy to carry alone for one more day. There's a
difference, and it's a difference I'm only just learning to
recognise, four days at a time, one avoided phone call at a time.

·

I do, eventually, ring Fiona in Aberdeen and tell her about the tape,
because there's a difference between offloading grief onto whoever's
nearest and choosing, deliberately, to hand a piece of it to the one
other person who actually knew our mother the way I did.

"I remember that message," Fiona says, quietly, once I've explained
which recording it is. "She left one for me too, the same week.
Different message. Same voice."

"You never told me that."

"You never told me about yours either," Fiona says, not unkindly, "and
I think that's rather the whole family tradition, isn't it. Everybody
carrying their own bit of her, separately, because nobody wanted to be
the one who brought it up first and made the other one sad."

We sit on the phone for a long time after that, not saying very much,
both of us listening to the specific quality of shared silence that
only exists between people who've lost the same person and are, for
once, allowing themselves to sit in that loss together rather than
apart. It doesn't fix anything. But it does, for the first time in six
years, feel like carrying something in company rather than alone,
which turns out to be most of what either of us actually needed.

·

I take the tape out of the box again the following Sunday, alone, in
the quiet of my own flat, and get as far as holding it in both hands
before I put it back, unplayed, for a second time.

I'm not ready for the whole of it yet. Four seconds cost me more than
I expected, and I've learned enough about myself this month to know
the difference between pushing through a feeling because it's brave
and pushing through it because stopping would mean admitting I'm not
ready, which isn't bravery at all, just another version of managing
something rather than actually sitting with it.

So I wait. I put the tape back in the box, and the box back on the
shelf, and tell myself, without much conviction, that there'll be a
better moment for the rest of it, sometime, though I have no idea yet
what that moment is going to look like, or who might be standing next
to me when it finally arrives.

·

I mention the loft, though not the tape itself, to Nadia a few days
later, mostly because the silence around it has started to feel like
its own small dishonesty, and I've been trying, since the envelope
conversation, to get better at naming the gaps rather than just
managing around them.

"There's a thing in a box in my loft I haven't told you about," I say.
"I'm not ready to tell you what it is yet. I wanted you to know there's
a thing, rather than just letting you notice I've gone quiet some
evenings and wondering why."

Nadia looks at me for a long moment, and I can see her deciding,
visibly, not to push, the same restraint she's shown me a dozen times
already this month.

"Okay," she says. "Thank you for telling me there's a thing. That's
different from telling me the thing, and I know the difference costs
you something to hold onto."

"It does."

"Take whatever time it needs," she says. "I'm not going anywhere while
you work out what to do with a box in a loft."

I don't say anything else about it that evening, but I notice, walking
home, that having said even that much — a thing exists, I'm not ready,
please don't ask — has lightened something in my chest that I hadn't
fully registered as heavy until it was carrying slightly less.

Priya, separately, catches me looking distracted at the desk that same
week and, with her usual bluntness, asks outright whether I'm alright.

"I'm fine," I say, automatically, and then, catching myself, the way
I've been trying to catch myself lately: "Actually. Not entirely. I
found something of my mum's. I'm still working out what to do with
it."

Priya doesn't say anything clever or comforting. She just says, "that's
allowed to take as long as it takes," with a gravity that surprises me
coming from a seventeen-year-old, and goes back to the desk, and lets
me have the rest of the shift in a version of quiet that doesn't feel
like being managed, which is, I am learning, an entirely different
thing from being left alone.

·

I find myself, in the days after, noticing my mother in small,
unexpected places around the building — a turn of phrase Sandra uses on
a Thursday call that sounds, for a second, exactly like something my
mother used to say about the weather; the specific way the kettle's
handle has worn smooth in one particular spot, the same spot worn
smooth on the kettle in our childhood kitchen, as though some habits of
hand simply pass themselves down regardless of who's holding the
handle.

I mention this to nobody, at first, because it feels too small and too
strange to hand over as a proper sentence. But I write it down, in the
same notebook I've started using for everything I can't quite say out
loud yet, alongside the four minutes I allowed myself on the loft
ladder and the fact of a cassette that says, on its label, in
handwriting I don't recognise, MUM — DON'T DELETE.

It occurs to me, writing it down, that grief postponed doesn't
actually go anywhere in the meantime. It just waits, patiently, in the
worn handle of a kettle and the cadence of a stranger's phone call,
for the one moment you're finally ready to let it back into the room
properly, and that moment, I am slowly starting to suspect, might not
be very far away at all.

·

Nadia notices the notebook before I've said anything about it — catches
me writing in it one evening at the overnight desk, in the specific
unhurried way she notices most things, and doesn't ask what's in it,
just asks, gently, whether writing it down is helping.

"I think so," I say. "It's not fixing anything. It's just making the
carrying a bit less shapeless."

"That's usually enough, to start with." She doesn't push for more, the
way she's learned not to, the way I'm learning not to with her in
return. "For what it's worth — and I don't offer unsolicited comparisons
often — grief and the podcast thing aren't the same shape, but they
rhyme, a bit. Both of them are about something private getting handled
in public before you're ready, except yours is entirely self-inflicted,
which is its own particular kind of hard."

"How do you mean, self-inflicted?"

"You're the one who won't let yourself grieve on your own schedule,"
Nadia says. "Nobody's doing that to you. You're doing it to yourself,
the same reflex that had you covering a three a.m. shift instead of
waking anyone up. It's not a criticism. I think it's just useful to say
out loud, the way it was useful when you said the true thing about
London instead of managing it."

I sit with that for a long while after she's gone back to the desk,
turning it over the way I turn over most of what she says that lands
harder than she probably intends it to, and find, walking home later, that
she's right, again, in the specific way she generally is — not unkindly,
just accurately, which somehow always lands more gently than kindness
alone ever quite manages to.

·

I ring Fiona again, later that same week, to tell her I've decided,
finally, on a day for the tape — not tomorrow, not a specific date I've
picked out of pressure, just a decision that it's coming, soon, on my
own terms rather than avoided indefinitely.

"Do you want me there," Fiona asks, "when you play it properly?"

I think about the question longer than it probably needs, because it's
a generous offer and deserves a considered answer rather than a
reflexive one.

"I don't think so," I say, eventually. "Not because I don't want you
there. Because I think this particular one, I need to do with Nadia,
and I think you'd understand why, even though I can't fully explain it
yet myself."

"I do understand," Fiona says, gently. "I'm glad you've found someone
you trust with it. That's not nothing, Robyn, after six years of doing
every hard thing entirely on your own out of some misplaced idea that
needing help made you less reliable."

"I'm working on that idea too."

"I know you are," Fiona says. "I can hear it, actually, in how you talk
now. You sound like someone who's finally decided being reliable and
being alone were never the same requirement in the first place," and
rings off before I can find a response to that which doesn't risk
undoing me slightly, leaving me to sit with the observation the rest
of the evening, turning it over the way I turn over most of what she
says that lands harder than she probably intends.

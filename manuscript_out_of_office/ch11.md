## Chapter Eleven — Not For the Show

We end up on a wall outside a chip shop on Byres Road on a night with
no rundown, no contract requiring it, no reason at all except that
Nadia said she was hungry and I, for once, didn't turn it into a task.

Before I see her face I hear the street: the fryer roaring through the
open door behind us, a bus somewhere up the hill changing gear with a
sound like a held breath let go, rain starting three closes away and
taking its time getting to us, the specific Glasgow weather that
announces itself before it arrives. Only then, turning, do I actually
look at her — properly, off duty, a chip halfway to her mouth, laughing
at something small I've said that wasn't even trying to be funny.

We talk about nothing, in the specific way that ends up meaning
everything. She tells me about a tour she did once through the Baltic
states with a company doing a two-hander that ran ninety minutes with
no interval, and how she used to time the whole thing from a booth at
the back and could tell, by minute forty, exactly how the audience
was going to feel about the ending. I tell her about the first thing I
ever presented, aged twenty-two, a graveyard-shift music show with an
audience she assures me, kindly, must have been at least four people,
and she laughs at the right places, and asks a follow-up question,
which — for a woman who has spent three years failing to make small
talk to save her life — I clock immediately and don't mention, because
mentioning it would be exactly the kind of thing that makes a person
stop doing it.

Nothing about the show comes up for forty minutes. I check, afterward,
lying awake, and I'm fairly sure it's a record.

Walking back, our hands are near enough that the back of hers brushes
the back of mine twice, and neither of us closes the gap, on purpose,
by an agreement neither of us has said out loud, and somehow that not-
touching does more work than an actual touch would have.

·

She's quiet back at the studio, in a way I've learned to read as
different from her ordinary quiet, and when I ask if she's alright she
doesn't deflect it into a level or a timing, which is itself the tell.

There's an envelope on the overnight desk I haven't noticed before —
a fringe theatre company's letterhead, SOUND DESIGNER, six months,
touring, unopened, with three weeks of dust settling into the crease
where she's clearly folded it and refolded it without ever getting as
far as the seal.

"I keep telling everyone I don't want it," she says. "I've told you
that. It isn't true. I don't want to *want* it, which isn't the same
thing, and I know it isn't."

"Why haven't you opened it?"

"Because touring puts me in front of people," she says, "and being in
front of people is what the podcast turned into something I still
can't talk about all the way through." She says it at the envelope, not
at me, which I understand, this once, not to push against. "There's a
reason to stay that isn't the roof."

She doesn't finish the sentence. She doesn't have to; I hear the shape
of the rest of it anyway, sitting in the room with us, unspoken and
entirely unmissable.

I don't ask her to say the rest. I've learned that much, at least. I
sit with her, in the quiet, while the level meter does its small green
work in front of us both, and let the unopened envelope stay exactly
where it is.

"Do you want it," I ask, eventually, meaning the job, not the reason.

"I don't know," Nadia says, which from her — a woman who answers
almost every question with a fact, a timing, a number — is nearly a
whole confession on its own. "I know what I'm supposed to want. Six
months, good money, a company I respect, work I'm good at. I know
what a sensible person does with that envelope."

"What does the sensible person do?"

"Opens it. Says yes. Goes back to being someone who's good at a job
and invisible while she does it, in a different city, where nobody's
heard of the podcast and nobody's going to ask."

"And what do you actually want?"

She doesn't answer that one. She looks at the envelope instead, for a
long moment, and then puts it in the desk drawer, not the bin,
which I notice, and don't say anything about, because there's a
version of this conversation where I push and a version where I let
her carry it at her own pace, and I am trying, this once, very hard,
to be the second kind of person.

We sit a while longer without talking, the level meter's small green
work the only thing moving in the room, and I find myself doing the
arithmetic anyway, quietly, the way I do everything: six weeks left on
the show, an unopened envelope with a six-month contract in it, a
woman who has just told me, as plainly as she's told me anything, that
there's a reason to stay that isn't the roof. I don't say any of that
out loud. I just sit with her, and let the desk drawer stay shut, and
try to be someone worth staying for rather than someone who simply
asks her to.

·

Priya, entirely without meaning to, hands me the exact framing I need
for it a few days later, in the kitchen, apropos of a school essay
she's meant to be writing about, of all things, market forces.

"The thing about touring work," she says, not looking up from her
laptop, in the specific tone of someone thinking out loud rather than
making conversation, "is that it always pays better than staying
somewhere does. That's just supply and demand. Staying's cheap because
everyone assumes you'll do it for free, out of loyalty, and loyalty
doesn't show up on anyone's invoice."

"That's very cynical for an essay about market forces."

"It's not cynical, it's economics." She finally looks up. "Why, is
this relevant to something?"

"No," I say, far too quickly, and Priya goes back to her essay with
the specific, satisfied silence of a teenager who knows exactly when
she's landed something and has decided, generously, not to make me say
so out loud.

I think about her sentence for days afterward. *Loyalty doesn't show
up on anyone's invoice.* It's the truest thing anyone's said to me all
month, and it isn't even about the two of us, not directly — and yet I
can't stop turning it over, wondering what it would actually take to
make staying somewhere worth as much, on paper and off it, as leaving
would be.

·

I finally ask Nadia properly about the touring years the following
weekend, over a pot of tea that's gone cold twice while we talk,
because I've decided that understanding the shape of what she's
weighing matters more than my own discomfort at asking.

"What was it like," I say, "before all this. The company you toured
with."

"Good," she says, without hesitation, which surprises me. "Properly
good. Small houses, mostly — village halls, community centres, the
odd proper theatre if we were lucky. I did the sound for a two-hander
about a lighthouse keeper that toured for three years and I could
still, today, tell you every cue in it from memory. There's a
particular kind of satisfaction in a small room that's completely
silent because you've built the silence properly, on purpose, out of
speakers and a mixing desk and about four years of learning exactly
how a room breathes."

"You miss it."

"I miss the work. I don't miss the life around the work." She turns
her mug slowly on the table. "Digs in other people's spare rooms.
Never being anywhere long enough to know which bench is dry after
rain. Being, for six months of every year, entirely and only defined
by the job, because there wasn't time to be anything else." She looks
up. "The overnight desk gave me somewhere to actually live. That's
worth more to me now than any lighthouse keeper's cue."

"So the contract—"

"The contract's good work, properly paid, genuinely tempting on every
professional measure I've got." She says it plainly, no drama in it.
"And I keep not opening it, and I think, if I'm honest with both of
us, it's because for the first time in about six years I've got a
reason to stay that has nothing to do with being too tired to leave."

I don't say anything to that. I just reach across the table and take
her hand, briefly, and she lets me, and neither of us needs to say
anything further about an envelope that's still, for now, sitting
unopened in a drawer.

·

The theatre company itself rings the following week, chasing an
answer, and I happen to be in the room when the call comes through,
which means I get to watch Nadia handle it in real time rather than
hear about it afterward secondhand.

She's kind about it. Genuinely kind, not performing kindness for my
benefit — she thanks them properly, tells them the work sounds
exactly like the sort of thing she'd have leapt at three years ago,
and asks, without any drama in it at all, for another fortnight to
decide, which they grant, sounding faintly puzzled that anyone would
need that long to say yes to what is, by any reasonable measure, an
excellent offer.

"You could have just said no," I say, once she's hung up.

"I could have. I didn't want to, not yet, because saying no felt like
deciding something before I'd actually let myself feel the whole
weight of it." She sets the phone down. "I spent two years assuming
the only two options available to me were touring, in front of
people, or hiding, behind a desk. I hadn't considered there might be
a third one, until recently, where I get to be visible on my own
terms, in one place, with people who already know me. I want to sit
with that a bit longer before I close a door on the other version."

"Take as long as you need."

"I will," Nadia says, and looks, for a moment, less like a woman
deciding between two jobs and more like a woman finally being allowed
to choose something for herself, properly, without anybody's deadline
attached to it but her own.

·

I mention the fortnight to Priya, in passing, not the details, just
that Nadia's got a decision to make and has bought herself some room
to make it properly, and Priya, for once, doesn't push for the whole
story, just nods slowly in the specific way she does when she's
filing something important away rather than gossip.

"Good," she says. "People should get fortnights. Everyone always wants
answers immediately, like a decision that takes two weeks instead of
two minutes is somehow a worse decision, when it's usually just a more
honest one."

"That's very wise for someone who complained about the wait for her
own exam results for a solid month."

"That's different. Exam results are outside my control. This is
inside hers. You should always take longer over things that are
actually yours to decide, and less time over things that are just
going to happen to you regardless." She says it with the specific
flat certainty of a teenager who has clearly thought about this more
than her age would suggest, and goes back to whatever she was doing
before, leaving me, again, faintly astonished at how much wisdom keeps
turning up in this building from a person who isn't legally old enough
to buy a lottery ticket.

I think about her distinction for the rest of the week — things that
are yours to decide, versus things that simply happen to you — and
find myself sorting my own recent choices into the two piles more
carefully than I have in years. The London job: mine to decide, and I
took my time over it, eventually. The panel's renewal: not mine, not
really, however much energy I spend managing it. Nadia: entirely,
frighteningly, mine to decide, every single day, in small ways, and I
notice, doing the sorting, that I've started taking my time over that
one too, rather than rushing to secure an answer the way I used to
rush to secure everything else.

·

Fiona rings partway through that same week, and I tell her about the
envelope, in outline, the way I've started telling her most things
about Nadia now — not the full story, because most of it isn't mine to
share, but enough that she understands the shape of what's happening.

"She sounds like someone who's been burned by moving too fast before,"
Fiona says, once I've explained the fortnight.

"I think she has."

"Then let her have the fortnight, properly, without checking in on it
every day to see how she's getting on. I know that's not your natural
setting. Do it anyway."

"I am doing it."

"Good," Fiona says, sounding genuinely pleased, in the specific tone
she reserves for moments where she's watching me manage to do
something she once doubted I could. "You know, this whole thing started
as a favour to a funding panel, and I remember thinking, when you first
told me, that it sounded like the single worst idea you'd ever had.
I want that on the record too, since we're keeping records of
everything else this autumn."

"Duly noted."

"I'm allowed to have been wrong," Fiona says, "loudly, and often, if
it means being right about something this good in the end," and rings
off, leaving me smiling at the phone in the specific way that's become,
this month, considerably more common than it used to be.

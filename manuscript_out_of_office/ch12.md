## Chapter Twelve — London, Again

There's a calendar reminder on my phone that says, simply, *answer
London*, and it is five weeks old, and I set it myself, and I have
been walking past it the way you walk past a bill you've decided to
open later.

The job is real. Senior producer, national reach, a salary that would
make the roof fund look like a rounding error, at a station I used to
believe, at twenty-six, was the entire point of doing any of this. I
read the email again for the fourth or fifth time — I've stopped
counting — standing in the office with the door shut, and I notice,
this time, a detail I'd skated past before: the sign-off is from a
name I half-recognise, someone who used to be junior when Kirsty and
I did breakfast together, who is apparently now senior enough to be
courting me on somebody else's behalf.

Eight years. Everyone moves except the people who stay.

Kirsty texts, unprompted, *just checking in about the format, no
pressure :)*, which manages to be, somehow, the most pressure available
in the English language, four words and an emoji doing the work of an
entire committee meeting.

I don't tell Nadia it's resurfaced. I notice myself not telling her,
which is new, and which I don't examine too closely, because examining
it would mean admitting there's now a category of thing I keep from
her on purpose, and I am not ready for that category to have a name
yet. I tell myself it's not relevant to the show. I tell myself there's
nothing to tell until I've actually decided something. Both of those
are true and neither of them is the reason.

·

Broadcast three, and the rundown has a slot for a pre-screened caller
with a nice, safe question about anniversaries.

What we get instead, patched through by an error nobody will ever fully
explain, is a real one — unscreened, live, a woman's voice slightly
shaking, asking: "How did you know you could trust each other
completely?"

She means it. Her name's Fiona — a different Fiona from the one on my
midnight list of candidates, a coincidence I clock and immediately
discard, because there's no room in this moment for anything except
the actual question. She's waiting for an answer, the way people wait
when the question actually matters to them, breath audible even
through a phone line, and the scripted line is right there on the
rundown, four words away from my mouth — *communication is a muscle*,
something Priya and I workshopped over a whiteboard three weeks ago —
and neither of us reaches for it.

What comes out instead is true and entirely un-cued: something about
an overnight shift, a fader slid across without being asked, a mug of
tea that arrived unrequested and meant, without either of us saying
so, *I noticed*. None of it makes sense to a stranger listening in.
All of it is real, and for about four seconds — half a beat, less than
it takes Priya to reach for the next sting — the fake thing and the
true thing touch, live, in front of an audience, before either of us
catches it and I cue the next segment on pure instinct.

Off air, in the corridor, both of us are shaking slightly, and neither
says why.

Priya, mercifully, does not comment on it directly, though she watches
us both with the specific narrowed attention of someone doing sums she
isn't ready to say out loud yet either. She busies herself instead
with the automation queue for the next segment, giving us both, I
realise afterward, exactly the kind of privacy a seventeen-year-old
has no obligation whatsoever to extend to her employer and extends
anyway, because she is, under the gossip and the spreadsheets, kinder
than she generally lets on.

I go home that night and don't draft any texts at all. There doesn't
seem to be a sentence small enough to hold what just happened, and for
once I don't try to build one anyway.

·

The London decision doesn't get any easier for being deferred. If
anything, deferring it makes the deferring itself into its own small
daily task — the calendar reminder, snoozed rather than answered, the
recruiter's second email arriving exactly a week after the first with
the specific patient persistence of someone who's been told, somewhere
up the chain, that I'm worth waiting for.

I read the second email standing at the kettle, the same kettle, the
same four minutes, and notice, this time, that my first reaction isn't
excitement or dread but something closer to irritation — the
particular irritation of being interrupted mid-thought by a decision
I've already, somewhere below the level I'm willing to admit to, more
or less made.

I don't answer it that day either. But I notice the irritation, and
file it, alongside everything else this month, for later examination,
in the growing queue of things I am, however slowly, finally starting
to actually look at rather than simply carry.

·

The caller from broadcast three — Fiona, the one whose voice shook,
the one who asked the question neither of us could script an answer
to — writes in a few days later, care of the station's general
inbox, a short message thanking us for the answer and saying, almost
in passing, that she'd gone home that night and had the actual
conversation with her own partner that the call had been circling
around for weeks.

I read it out to Nadia at the desk. She doesn't say anything for a
moment, just looks at the screen for a long time, and then says,
quietly, "that's the whole job, isn't it. Not the panel. Not the
metric. Just — that."

"Just that," I agree.

Neither of us says anything else about London, or the panel, or the
particular category of thing I've started keeping from her. But I
notice, watching her read Fiona's message a second time, that whatever
I decide about that job, it's going to have to survive being weighed
against a moment like this one, and that the maths, when I finally do
it properly, is not going to be close.

·

Kirsty texts again that same evening, a short message this time,
about the broadcast three clip rather than about London: *heard about
the four seconds. People are talking about it in a good way, for
once. Well done, both of you.*

It's a small, ordinary kindness, and I let it be exactly that — a
colleague passing on some cheering news — without reading anything
further into it, because there isn't, as far as I can tell, anything
further to read.

·

Douglas, for his part, is considerably less charmed by broadcast
three than the general mood in the building would suggest he ought to
be. His email arrives two days later, addressed to me rather than the
whole panel distribution list, which I've learned by now to read as
its own small warning sign.

*While the content continues to perform well on engagement metrics,*
it reads, in the specific bloodless register I'm coming to recognise
as his native tongue, *the panel would want assurance that the format
maintains appropriate professional boundaries, particularly regarding
unscreened caller content going forward. Recommend tightening screening
protocols ahead of the next review point.*

"He's worried about the four seconds," Priya says, reading over my
shoulder, uninvited, as ever. "The bit everyone else loved. He's
worried about the exact bit that made the show good."

"He's worried about liability, not quality. Those aren't the same
thing to him."

"They should be the same thing to everyone," Priya says, with the
specific moral clarity available only to a seventeen-year-old who
hasn't yet had to sit across a table from a funding panel and learn to
speak their particular dialect of caution. "That was the best four
seconds of radio this station's ever produced and he wants to make
sure it never happens again."

I don't disagree with her, not really, but I also recognise, reading
the email a second time, the specific shape of a warning that's going
to matter later, in ways I can't yet fully see the edges of — a
funding review that has decided, somewhere behind its bloodless prose,
that the show's real strength is also its real risk, and that those
two facts are going to keep sitting uncomfortably close together for
as long as the renewal remains undecided.

I don't reply to Douglas that day. I sit with the email a while
instead, the way I've started sitting with things rather than
immediately managing them, and notice, with some discomfort, that the
version of me who'd have fired back a reassuring response within the
hour is, these days, considerably harder to locate than she used to
be.

·

I do eventually reply to Douglas, two days later, once I've had time
to draft something that doesn't read as either capitulation or
confrontation, and I show it to Nadia before I send it, the way I've
started showing her most things that touch the show now, regardless of
whether they technically fall under her job description.

"You've been reasonable," she says, reading it over. "Possibly too
reasonable. You've agreed to review the screening protocol without
actually agreeing to change anything about how the segment runs."

"That's deliberate."

"I know it's deliberate. I'm just checking you know it's deliberate,
rather than it being an accident of you being too tired to hold a
firmer line." She hands the laptop back. "It's good. Send it."

I send it, and the reply that comes back from Douglas a day later is,
by his standards, almost warm — a single line acknowledging the
response, no further demands, which I've learned by now to read as
roughly the temperature of an actual thaw rather than merely an absence
of complaint.

"He's not a villain," Nadia says, later, when I mention the reply.
"I know it's easy to make him one. He's just a man whose entire job is
covering the panel's exposure, and covering exposure and caring about
the actual work aren't always the same instinct, even in someone who
isn't secretly malicious about it."

"When did you get so generous about Douglas?"

"I'm not being generous. I'm being accurate," Nadia says, which I've
come to understand, by now, is simply how she experiences most acts of
fairness — not as generosity extended, but as a fact correctly stated,
regardless of how much warmer it might look from the outside.

·

Priya, who has clearly been keeping her own private ledger of Douglas
interactions since the clipboard incident, greets the news of the
thaw with visible disappointment.

"That's it? One reasonable email and he's just — nice now?"

"People are allowed to be won round, Priya."

"I had a whole campaign planned. Months of strategic clipboard
sabotage." She sighs, the specific put-upon sigh of a general robbed
of her war by an early peace treaty. "Fine. I suppose grudging thaw is
still a victory. I'll update the spreadsheet to reflect a diplomatic
resolution rather than the glorious rout I was hoping for."

"You wanted a rout against a funding panel member."

"I wanted *justice*," Priya says, with enormous dignity, "and I'm
choosing to interpret a one-line acknowledgement email as a partial
delivery of it." She closes her laptop with the specific finality of
someone declaring a subject closed, at least for today, and goes to
put the kettle on, leaving me to reflect, not for the first time, on
how much of this building's actual morale runs on a seventeen-year-
old's capacity to turn even a bureaucratic thaw into a small personal
triumph worth celebrating properly.

·

The caller from broadcast three — the other Fiona, the one whose
question neither of us could script an answer to — writes in again a
few weeks later, a longer message this time, thanking us properly and
mentioning, almost in passing, that she and her partner have decided to
get married in the spring.

I read it aloud to Nadia at the desk, and she goes quiet in the
particular way that means she's genuinely moved rather than merely
processing information.

"We didn't do that," she says, eventually. "Whatever got them there.
We just happened to be on air at the right four seconds. They did the
actual work themselves."

"I know. But we were the four seconds."

"That's a strange thing to have been," Nadia says, "for two people who
started this whole show as a favour to a funding panel." She looks at
the message again. "I keep thinking about what Priya said. That there's
no difference left to tell, between the segment and whatever's actually
happening underneath it. I think that's exactly why this landed the way
it did. If we'd been faking it, that caller would have heard four
seconds of performance. She heard something real, by accident, and it
apparently helped her decide something enormous about her own life."

"Does that frighten you? How much weight a few unscripted seconds can
carry?"

"A bit," Nadia admits. "Mostly it makes me want to be more careful with
the unscripted seconds, rather than less generous with them. There's a
difference between being frightened of something and deciding it
deserves more care because it matters. I think I've spent most of my
life doing the first one when I should have been doing the second."

I don't have anything to add to that, so I let it sit between us,
the two of us reading and rereading a stranger's happy news at a desk
in a basement that started this whole autumn as a funding-panel
gambit and has become, somewhere along the way, considerably more.

·

I tell Fiona about the wedding message that same night, and she goes
quiet on the line for a moment, the specific quiet she reserves for
news that means more to her than she immediately lets on.

"That's the kind of thing you don't get to plan for," she says,
eventually. "You set out to save a roof. You end up changing the
actual course of a stranger's life, by accident, because you happened
to say the true thing on air instead of the scripted one. I hope you
let yourself feel how enormous that is, rather than just filing it
under good outcomes and moving on to the next problem."

"I'm trying to let myself feel it properly."

"Good," Fiona says. "That's new for you, and I like it," and we talk a
while longer, about nothing much, before ringing off, leaving me
sitting at the kitchen table with a stranger's happy news and a
growing sense that this whole strange autumn has turned out to matter
in ways considerably larger than a funding renewal ever could.

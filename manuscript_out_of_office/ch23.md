## Chapter Twenty-Three — Terms Renegotiated

The voicemail box is full by nine the next morning, which has never
once happened in the entire history of this station, not even the year
we accidentally aired eleven minutes of dead air during a council
byelection.

Most of the messages are listeners, and most of them are kind, and
several of them are people naming, in their own words, their own
version of being loved as a bit rather than as a person, glad to hear
someone say it back to them at last. A regional paper runs two hundred
and twenty words under the headline COMMUNITY RADIO'S HONEST FINALE,
which is not, in my experience, the kind of coverage a manufactured
stunt generally earns.

Priya's spreadsheet gets a third tab, unprompted, titled, in full,
GOOD NEWS.

The panel's silence, for once, does not read to me as a threat. It
reads like an institution quietly working out how to change its own
mind without saying so directly, which — I am learning, six weeks into
what started as an entirely fictional relationship and turned real
somewhere around week four — is roughly how institutions do
everything.

Nadia doesn't come down to the office to see the coverage. She's back
on her ordinary shift by the Saturday, both headphone cups doing their
usual thing, one on and one off, and when I ask how she's doing she
doesn't reach for a level or a timing.

"Tired," she says. "Relieved. Frightened, a bit, that it's not over
yet, because it isn't, not really — there'll be more clips, more
people who find the segment because of the coverage rather than
because they were already listening at midnight. I'm trying to decide
if that's alright."

"Is it?"

"Ask me again next week," she says, which I recognise now, gratefully,
as the actual answer rather than an evasion of one.

·

Day forty-four, exactly where the clock always said it would land: the
committee room, the same strip lighting, the same folder of numbers,
though this time I haven't rehearsed a single word of what I'm going
to say.

Douglas opens, predictably, with a version of *reputational exposure*,
though he's had three weeks to soften it and it comes out closer to
*learnings* than to anything resembling an actual objection. Someone
from the finance side asks a genuinely fair question about whether
funding an anonymous segment sets an awkward precedent, and I answer
it properly, at length, without once managing myself into a smaller
shape to make the room more comfortable — the first time, I realise
partway through, that I've ever done that in this specific room.

The renewal is approved. Not on the original metric — not on
*audience-identifiable relational content* — but on a rewritten one:
Static, named outright, funded as its own line item, no longer
something that has to hide inside a stunt in order to survive.

Kirsty argues for it in the room, plainly, on the record, in a way
that costs her something with the rest of the panel, and afterward, in
the corridor, she says the thing she should have said eight years ago
and has finally found the nerve to say over a folder of committee
minutes instead.

"I should have said what I said in that coffee shop a long time ago,"
she says. "For what it's worth — I'm glad you stayed."

"I'm glad you're saying it now," I say, and mean it, and that's the
whole of the repair either of us needs. Not a reconciliation with a
bow on it. Just two people, finally, saying a true thing out loud, on
the record, where it can be heard.

The show survives changed, not merely spared, which turns out to be
the better outcome by every measure that actually matters.

Priya, waiting in the corridor outside the committee room with a
coffee she's clearly bought specifically for this moment, hands it to
me the second I'm out the door.

"Well?"

"Renewed. Rewritten. Static's on the books, properly, with a fee."

Priya makes a sound I have never heard her make before, something
between a cheer and a sob, and hugs me, briefly, fiercely, before
remembering that she is seventeen and has a reputation for composure
to maintain, and stepping back, straightening her jacket, entirely
unconvincing.

"I'm very calm about this," she says.

"I can tell."

"I'm going to go build a spreadsheet."

"Of course you are."

She goes, at something close to a run, and I stand in the corridor of
the Chambers annexe for a moment, holding a coffee I didn't ask for,
in a building I've walked out of feeling defeated more times than I
can count over six years, and let myself, properly, feel the opposite
of that for once.

·

I ring Nadia from the corridor before I've even left the building,
because some news doesn't survive the walk back intact if you don't
say it the moment you have it.

"Renewed," I say, the second she picks up. "Rewritten. Static's on the
books, named, funded, properly credited. It's yours, in writing, by
the end of the week."

There's a silence on the line, longer than I expect, and for a second
I think the call's dropped.

"Nadia?"

"I'm here." Her voice does something I've only heard it do a handful
of times, something that isn't quite steady. "Sorry. I just — I've
spent two years assuming the safest version of me was the invisible
one. I'm still getting used to the idea that the visible one gets to
be funded too."

"She does," I say. "Properly. In writing."

"In writing," Nadia repeats, like she's testing the shape of the
phrase, the way you test a floor before you trust your whole weight to
it, and I let her have the silence that follows, because some
sentences need room to actually land.

·

The formal paperwork takes another fortnight to arrive, in the way
these things always do — a licence variation, a budget line, a letter
from Douglas that manages, even now, to sound faintly put-upon about
the whole arrangement, though I notice he signs it without a single
further objection. Nadia frames the letter, eventually, not out of
sentiment exactly but because she says she wants to be able to look at
it on a bad day and remember that somebody, somewhere, put something
real about her in writing and meant it.

It hangs, in the end, in the overnight studio, just above the desk,
where she can see it every single shift, a small rectangle of official
council letterhead doing considerably more work than its size would
suggest.

·

Priya insists on marking the renewal properly, in the way she insists
on marking everything she considers significant, which turns out to
mean cake, bought from the good bakery on Byres Road rather than the
supermarket, and a hand-lettered banner strung across the kitchen that
reads, in full, KELVIN ROW: STILL HERE, STILL BORING, STILL FUNDED,
which she considers, correctly, the accurate summary of the entire
last two months.

We eat cake standing up in a kitchen that smells of damp and
transmitter oil, all four of us — me, Nadia, Priya, and Dev, who has
fully recovered from his food poisoning and has, in the intervening
weeks, become something of an unofficial fifth member of the group
chat, still called, at Priya's insistence, OPERATION COUPLE, though
the operation part has long since stopped meaning what it originally
meant.

"To the roof," Priya says, raising a paper cup of orange squash like
it's champagne, "which is still leaking, and will presumably always be
leaking, and which none of this changes even slightly."

"To the roof," we all agree, and drink to a leaking roof with the
specific sincerity of people who have learned, this autumn, exactly
what a building like this one is actually worth keeping standing for.

·

Malcolm turns up that same afternoon, unannounced as ever, using the
key he was never asked to hand back, and takes in the banner, the cake
crumbs, the general state of a basement that has clearly just been
celebrating something, with the specific slow satisfaction of a man
watching a project he handed over eleven years ago finally arrive
somewhere he'd hoped it might.

"Heard you got the renewal," he says.

"Rewritten, not just renewed."

"Better," he says, lowering himself into the good chair without
asking, same as always. "Renewed means they let you keep doing the
same thing. Rewritten means they finally understood what the thing
actually was." He looks round the room once more, at Priya's banner,
at the wall of fame now visibly overflowing its corkboard. "Don't let
it become boring," he says, the way he always says it, "and don't let
it close."

"Working on both."

"I can see that." He accepts a slice of cake from Priya, who presents
it to him with the specific ceremony she reserves for the station's
founder, and eats it slowly, looking round the basement one more time.
"I'll tell you the thing I never told you, eleven years ago, handing
you the keys," he says, eventually. "I didn't think you'd manage it.
Not because I doubted you specifically — I didn't know you well enough
yet to doubt you specifically — but because nobody manages it, usually.
Stations like this one fold within a couple of years, most places. I
handed you the keys mostly because there wasn't anybody else standing
in the room, same as you've told me happened to you."

"Why are you telling me now?"

"Because you've just done the thing I didn't think was possible, and I
think you should know that I noticed, and that it mattered, and that
I'm not in the habit of saying so, which is probably a fault of mine
rather than a virtue." He finishes the cake, sets the plate down. "Nine
years. Not bad, for someone I handed a coal store and a set of keys
to, with no plan beyond two rules."

"Two rules were enough."

"Apparently so," Malcolm says, and gets up to leave, slower than he
used to, and pauses at the top of the basement stairs to look back
down at the four of us still standing in the kitchen. "Don't let it
become boring," he says once more, "and don't let it close," and this
time, for the first time in eleven years, I understand it isn't an
instruction at all. It's a blessing, dressed up as one, the only kind
he's ever known how to give.

·

I ring Fiona from the corridor outside the committee room before the
news has even had time to properly settle in me, because some things
need saying to the person who's carried the worry alongside you before
they're said to anyone else.

"Renewed," I say, the second she picks up. "Rewritten, actually. Better
than renewed."

There's a whoop down the line loud enough that I have to hold the
phone slightly away from my ear, entirely unlike Fiona's usual
composed register, and it takes her a moment to recover enough to form
actual words.

"I'm coming down this weekend," she announces, once she's got her
breath back. "I don't care what's on. I want to see the place properly,
finally, now that it's not about to disappear out from under you."

"You've seen the place plenty of times."

"I've seen the place when it was something you were fighting to keep.
I want to see it now it's something you've actually won." A pause,
softer. "I'm proud of you. Properly. Not just about the renewal. About
all of it — the whole autumn. You've done more growing up in two
months than I watched you do in the previous decade, and I include
myself in the category of people who should have pushed you toward
some of it sooner."

"You can't make yourself responsible for my decisions, Fiona."

"I know. I'm just saying it anyway, because it's true, and because
you've spent your whole life being the one who says the true, difficult
things to other people, and I think it's time somebody said one back
to you, for a change." She laughs, a little. "Now go and celebrate
properly, and tell Nadia I'll see her this weekend, and that I already
like her enormously based entirely on secondhand reports, which I
recognise is not a high bar for her to clear, but I intend to be
thoroughly won over regardless."

I promise to pass it on, and ring off smiling in a corridor of the
Chambers annexe I've walked out of feeling defeated more times than I
can count, finally, for once, feeling nothing but the opposite.

·

Fiona does come down that weekend, exactly as promised, and meets
Nadia properly for the first time over cake in the station kitchen,
the two of them sizing each other up with the specific caution of a
sister and a partner meeting for the first time, both aware that
whatever's said in the next hour will set the tone for years of future
gatherings.

"So you're the overnight engineer," Fiona says, by way of opening.

"I'm the overnight engineer," Nadia agrees, entirely unbothered by the
scrutiny, in the specific flat register I've come to find endlessly
reassuring rather than off-putting. "Also, apparently, the reason your
sister's stopped ringing you every Tuesday sounding like she's about
to collapse under the weight of a leaking roof single-handedly."

"I noticed that too," Fiona says, visibly recalibrating her opening
line, warming immediately. "I like you already. Most people meeting me
for the first time try very hard to be charming. You've just stated a
fact."

"I find facts generally land better than charm," Nadia says. "Charm
runs out. Facts don't."

Fiona laughs, properly, the specific laugh she reserves for people
she's decided, quickly and permanently, that she approves of, and the
rest of the visit passes easily, the two of them trading stories about
me — Fiona's from childhood, Nadia's from the last two months — with
the specific delighted cruelty only two people who both love you
properly can manage without it ever once feeling like an attack.

"I like her enormously," Fiona tells me privately, before she gets back
on the train. "Properly. Not just 'she seems nice.' She's exactly the
kind of person I'd have chosen for you myself, if anyone had ever
asked me, which nobody ever does, being your sister rather than your
actual decision-maker in these matters."

"You've made your opinion known plenty over the years regardless."

"That's simply due diligence," Fiona says, entirely unrepentant, and
hugs me at the platform, and gets on her train back to Aberdeen,
leaving me to walk back to the station with the specific settled
feeling of having watched two of the most important people in my life
meet properly, at last, and like each other exactly as much as I'd
hoped they would.

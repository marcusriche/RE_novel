## Chapter Five — The Backstory

Building a shared history that doesn't exist turns out to be one part
comedy and three parts logistics.

We agree on the true parts first, because Nadia insists on it, on the
grounds that a lie built on no true parts at all is a lie with nothing
to hold it up. So: we met at the station. That part's real, if you're
generous about the definition of *met*, which mostly consisted of me
saying good morning to a closed studio door for two years. Eight
months, backdated, because Priya assigns us an anniversary off the
volunteer rota like she's doing us a favour, which, structurally, she
is.

"What do we argue about," I ask, marker in hand, the whiteboard now a
genuine crime scene of crossed-out titles and half a diagram of a fake
relationship.

"The radio," Nadia says, without hesitating. "Safely. Because it's
real, so we'll never get caught out on a detail, and because everyone
who's ever worked in this building will believe two people who work
here argue about scheduling."

"That's very practical."

"I'm a very practical person."

We rehearse an argument about whose turn it is to do the washing-up —
a washing-up that does not exist, in a flat neither of us shares —
and I am, four lines in, surprised at how good she is at lying with
specificity. She invents a dishwasher that's been broken since March.
She invents a brand of fairy liquid she claims to hate. She does not,
however, know how to make small talk to save her life, and the two
things combined — devastatingly precise fiction, absolutely no ability
to ask how my weekend was — is so funny that I have to put the marker
down.

"What," she says.

"Nothing. You're just — you'd be a brilliant witness and a terrible
first date."

"I know," Nadia says, entirely unbothered, and goes back to the
dishwasher bit.

We're four minutes into the washing-up argument — properly into it now,
raised voices, me doing an exaggerated *you never asked me* while she
does something with her eyebrows that could win awards — when it turns
real for about four seconds. I say something about never being asked
what I want, meaning the bit, meaning the fictional dishwasher, and my
own voice does something underneath the joke that I don't authorise
and can't immediately take back.

Nadia notices. Of course she notices; noticing things is the entire
occupation. She doesn't say anything about it. She just lets the beat
sit there a half-second too long before delivering her next line, and
we both know, without either of us naming it, that something slipped
through that wasn't scripted.

I write it all on an index card afterward, because I am the kind of
person who needs an index card. Nadia reads it back once, flatly,
hitting every beat of the joke exactly right, which should not be as
unsettling as it is.

We add other things to the index card over the course of the
afternoon, the way you do when you're building a person out of spare
parts: a favourite film neither of us has actually seen (a French
thing Nadia claims to love specifically because nobody ever asks a
follow-up question about French things), a story about how I supposedly
talked her into applying for the overnight desk in the first place
(entirely fabricated; she applied because the ad was the only one that
didn't require her to be on camera), and a running joke about the
station's ancient, temperamental kettle that we agree to seed into
actual on-air banter because, Nadia points out, a joke a couple
actually has is a joke that survives contact with a live caller, and a
joke invented purely for the segment generally doesn't.

"You're better at this than you should be," I tell her, somewhere
around the fourth index card.

"I used to write show notes for a fringe theatre company," she says.
"You learn to build a person out of a paragraph. It's the same skill."

I don't ask which company, or when, or why *used to*, because I've
already worked out, four days into this, that the fastest way to get
Nadia to close a door is to knock on it before she's said I can.

·

Kirsty comes by on the Friday, "to see the format in person," which
means the panel sent her, which means somebody upstairs wants a report
that isn't just Douglas's euphemisms.

She stands in the foyer in a blazer that costs more than my monthly
transmitter bill and does the thing she's always done, which is be
warm in a way that is somehow also a closed door. "Robyn," she says,
in the tone you'd use for someone you used to know quite well and now
mostly know from a distance, which — I suppose — is exactly what I am
to her.

Eight years since we did breakfast together. Eight years since she
took the offer from national radio and I stayed, because somebody had
to, because the station doesn't run itself and I have never once, out
loud, said that I minded her leaving. I still haven't. I'm not about to
start in a foyer with a lanyard watching.

We were good together, back then, in a way I've never quite managed to
recreate with anyone since. She did the news and the banter; I did the
listeners, the phone-ins, the small human stuff that made people
actually ring in rather than just tune out. We used to finish each
other's links without trying to. When she left, it took me the better
part of a year to stop leaving a half-second gap in the schedule where
her voice was supposed to go.

She never once suggested I come with her. I've turned that fact over
more times than I'd like to admit, in the years since, trying to work
out whether it meant she didn't think I'd want to, or didn't think
I'd get in, or simply never thought of me at all once the bigger job
was in front of her. I have never asked. I'm not about to start now,
in a foyer, with a lanyard watching.

"It's looking good," Kirsty says, meaning the format, meaning the
folder she's presumably read, meaning nothing at all about the two of
us specifically. "Very — real. That's what the panel wants. Real."

"That's the plan," I say.

She glances past me, once, at Nadia, at the desk, headphones half on,
entirely uninterested in being introduced, and Kirsty doesn't ask who
she is, which I notice, and which I file away without knowing yet what
to do with it.

"Good to see you, Robyn," Kirsty says, and goes, leaving behind the
specific, professionally warm nothing she's always left behind, and
I stand in the foyer for a second longer than I need to before I go
back to the desk.

Nadia doesn't say anything about the exchange. Afterward, I notice
that she doesn't ask, and I don't offer, and something about the two
of us not asking each other things yet feels, for now, like the safest
arrangement available.

Priya, on the other hand, has opinions, delivered the moment Kirsty's
out the door and down the close stairs.

"That," she announces, "was extremely tense, and I would like to know
why, and I want to note that I asked Nadia the same question and she
just said *history* and went back to the fader, which I found
extremely unsatisfying as an answer."

"It's not a story, Priya."

"Everything's a story. That's the whole basis of my job here." She
folds her arms, settling in, the specific posture of a teenager who
has correctly identified that an adult is deflecting and has decided
to enjoy watching them do it. "You went very quiet. You're never quiet.
I've catalogued your entire range of behaviours over four years and
*quiet* isn't in it."

"We used to work together. A long time ago. It didn't end badly, it
just — ended."

"Did you want it to end?"

"I didn't get a vote," I say, which is truer than I meant to let it be,
and Priya, mercifully, for once in her life, decides not to push
further, and goes back to her spreadsheet, leaving me with the
specific relief of having said something honest to someone who chose,
this once, not to make me say more of it than I was ready for.

·

I spend that evening, after everyone's gone home, doing the thing I
haven't let myself do properly in eight years, which is pull the old
recordings.

The station keeps an archive going back further than it probably
should, on a hard drive Malcolm bought secondhand sometime in the
nineties and that nobody has ever quite dared to replace, for fear of
losing what's on it. I find a breakfast show from a Tuesday in
October, eight years and one month ago, and I sit in the dark office
with the amber bulb glowing through the wall and listen to two people
who sound, if I'm honest, considerably younger than I remember either
of us being.

We're good. I'd half convinced myself, over the years, that I'd
built the whole thing up in memory, made it better than it actually
was because loss tends to do that to things. We weren't. We were
genuinely, unreproducibly good together, in the specific way that only
happens when two people trust each other's timing completely, and
listening to it now, alone, in an empty basement, I let myself feel the
loss of that properly for the first time since it happened, rather
than filing it, as I have filed everything else that hurts, under
things there simply isn't time to deal with.

I don't play the whole show. I don't need to. I've got the shape of it
back now, and that's enough, for tonight, to be getting on with.

·

Fiona's down from Aberdeen that weekend for a wedding two streets
over, and turns up at my door afterward with a bottle of wine and the
specific look of a woman who has heard, from somewhere, that Kirsty
Lennox is now sitting on the licensing panel, and intends to extract
every detail of that fact before she leaves.

"You didn't tell me she was back," Fiona says, pouring two glasses
without asking whether I want one, which is, at this point, simply how
my sister operates.

"She's not back. She's on a panel. It's not the same thing."

"It's exactly the same thing, and you know it, because you've gone the
specific shade of quiet you go whenever her name comes up, which is a
shade I have been watching you go since we were nineteen and she
turned up at that first breakfast audition looking like she owned the
building."

"I don't go a shade."

"You go a *very specific* shade," Fiona says, entirely unbothered by
my objection, curling up on the other end of the sofa with the
practised comfort of someone who has spent thirty-odd years making
herself at home in whichever flat I happen to currently occupy. "I
watched you not go to London with her. I watched you spend the better
part of a year afterward leaving gaps in conversations exactly the
length of wherever her voice used to go. And now she's sitting across
a table from you in a blazer, judging your funding application, and
you're telling me it's not the same thing."

"It was eight years ago."

"Eight years isn't as long as you think it is," Fiona says, gentler
now, turning her glass by the stem. "I still think about the flat we
grew up in like I could walk back into it tomorrow. Eight years is
nothing, when it's a person, rather than a place."

I don't have an answer to that, and Fiona, mercifully, doesn't push
for one, just tops up my glass and changes the subject to something
easier — her nephew's disastrous attempt at a school play, a work
colleague's ongoing feud over the office thermostat — the ordinary
furniture of two sisters spending a Saturday evening together, and I
let myself be grateful, for a while, that at least one relationship in
my life doesn't currently require a written agreement to function.

·

Fiona stays the night, in the end, on the sofa she's claimed as hers on
every visit for the better part of a decade, and over breakfast the
next morning she brings up Kirsty again, more gently this time, having
apparently spent the intervening hours turning over whatever she
half-said the evening before.

"I don't want you to think I was having a go at you," she says, over
toast neither of us has properly buttered. "About Kirsty. I know it's
not simple."

"It's not simple."

"I just think," Fiona says, carefully, "that you've spent eight years
treating it like a closed file, and closed files still have weight,
even shut. You carry them exactly the same as open ones. Sometimes
more, actually, because you don't let yourself go back and check what's
actually in them."

"What would you have me do? Reopen it? She's on the panel now. There's
a professional line."

"I'm not saying reopen anything," Fiona says. "I'm saying stop
pretending the file's empty. It's not empty. You built a whole career
you never fully wanted around the shape of what happened with her,
without ever once saying so out loud, not even to me, and I'm your
sister, and I had to work most of it out from the outside."

I don't have a ready answer for that, and Fiona, mercifully, doesn't
push further, just butters her toast properly this time and changes
the subject to her own life, her own ordinary furniture of children
and thermostats and a husband who apparently cannot be trusted near a
barbecue, and I let the conversation move on, grateful, while quietly
filing away the phrase *a whole career you never fully wanted* to turn
over again later, alone, once she's back on the train to Aberdeen and
I no longer have to manage my own face while I think about it.

·

Fiona catches the train back on the Sunday, and I walk her to the
station rather than letting her get a taxi, mostly because I'm not
ready for the visit to end quite yet, and we stand on the platform for
a few minutes making the specific small talk two people make when
neither of them wants to be the one to say the actual goodbye first.

"Ring me after the first broadcast," she says again, the same
instruction from the phone call days before, "properly this time, not
just a text. I want to hear your actual voice tell me how it went,
not read three sentences about it."

"I will."

"And Robyn." She pauses, hand on the train door, in the specific way
she does when she's about to say something she's been holding back
the whole visit. "Whatever happens with Kirsty, with the panel, with
any of it — I meant what I said about the closed file. You don't have
to reopen it. But you don't have to keep pretending it's empty either,
not with me, not with yourself. That's all I'm asking."

"I hear you."

"Good," Fiona says, and hugs me, quickly, fiercely, the way she has
since we were children, and gets on the train, and I stand on the
platform until it's pulled fully out of the station, watching it go,
turning her sentence over the whole walk home in a way I suspect I'll
still be turning it over long after this particular six weeks has come
to whatever end it's heading toward.

## Chapter Eighteen — First, and Real

What happens next belongs to the two of us, and I'd like to keep it
that way, if it's all the same to you.

What I'll say is this: six weeks of performing a couple for an
audience, and then, finally, nobody watching at all. No cue sheet. No
rundown. No fifty-four-minute mark to hit. Just two people who have
run out of reasons to wait, and choose, plainly, in words, not to.

I will say, too, that she asks. Not once, at the start, and then never
again — properly, more than once, in the specific way she said she
would, checking rather than assuming, and that there is something in
being asked, gently and repeatedly, by someone who clearly means the
question each time, that I don't have adequate words for yet and may
never fully manage to.

Morning finds us still there, unhurried, neither of us first up to
leave, which — for two women who have spent a combined seven years
being the first one up in every room they've ever been in — feels like
its own small miracle. Somewhere around seven, with the rain starting
properly on the window and neither of us showing any sign of moving,
Nadia says, into my shoulder, not fully awake, "I keep waiting for you
to have somewhere else to be," and I say, "I don't," and she says,
"good," and that's the whole conversation, and it's the best one
either of us has had in years.

·

Tea, badly made, on a kitchen counter that has never once been
photographed for the show.

"Two more broadcasts," Nadia says, wrapped in a blanket that used to
belong, I gather, to a touring life she's mostly stopped mentioning.
"Then it's just us. We decide what anyone gets told, and when."

"Our timeline," I say. "Not the panel's."

"Not the panel's." She looks, for a second, genuinely happy in a way
I don't think I've seen from her before — not dry, not guarded, just
plainly, unguardedly pleased with the shape of her own life. "Two more
Thursdays and then we tell the truth on our own terms, once nobody can
call it a stunt."

"What do we say," I ask, "when we do tell people. What's the actual
sentence?"

Nadia considers this properly, the way she considers everything.
"The truth," she says. "In whatever order it makes sense to tell it.
Started as a favour, became something else somewhere around the
fourth broadcast, and neither of us noticed until it was too late to
call it anything but real."

"That's a good sentence."

"I've had six weeks to think about it."

"Since when?"

"Since about the second broadcast," Nadia admits, and it's such an
unguarded thing to say, so far from the eleven-words-in-three-years
version of her I first knocked on a glass door for, that I have to put
my mug down for a second before I trust myself to respond to it
properly.

I don't say anything about the fact that dramatic irony is, at this
exact moment, sitting quietly in the room with us, entirely unnoticed
by either party, because I don't yet know it's there. I just drink the
badly made tea, and watch the rain start on the kitchen window, and
let myself, for one whole morning, believe that the hardest part is
already behind us.

"There's a thing I want to ask you," Nadia says, later, once the tea's
gone cold and neither of us has moved to make more. "Not about the
show. About after it."

"Ask."

"When this is over — the six weeks, the panel, all of it — what do
you actually want your life to look like? Not the station's life. Not
the roof's. Yours."

Nobody has asked me that question in years, possibly ever, in those
exact words, and I find, sitting at her kitchen counter with cold tea
in front of me, that I don't have a rehearsed answer, which is itself
remarkable, because I have a rehearsed answer for nearly everything.

"I don't know yet," I say, honestly. "I think I want to find out."

"That's allowed," Nadia says. "You don't have to know already. You
just have to be willing to ask."

I think about that for the rest of the morning, and for a good part of
the following week, and I don't come to any tidy conclusion, which —
for a woman who has spent a decade being uncomfortable with anything
that doesn't resolve neatly by the end of a shift — I count, quietly,
as its own kind of progress.

·

We spend that whole week doing ordinary things, deliberately, as
though testing whether the extraordinary version of us can survive
contact with the mundane. Grocery shopping, once, at the big Tesco on
Byres Road, where Nadia reveals an entirely unexpected and deeply
held opinion about the correct brand of porridge oats that leads to an
argument conducted in hushed, furious whispers in the cereal aisle
and resolved only when we agree to buy both brands and settle it
properly, at home, with a blind taste test that Priya, informed of it
by group chat, insists on adjudicating remotely via video call.

Nadia wins. She always wins these things, in the small print, in the
detail nobody else bothered to check.

"I told you," she says, insufferably pleased, holding up the winning
tub.

"You didn't tell me. You simply were, apparently, correct, which isn't
the same as telling."

"It's the same as telling if you'd been listening properly the first
time."

I don't have a response to that, so I make the porridge her way, and
it is, infuriatingly, better.

·

The two remaining broadcasts, five and six, sit ahead of us that week
like something we're both trying not to look at directly, the way you
avoid looking straight at a deadline that's going to change everything
about how a story gets told. Nadia mentions it once, in the kitchen,
apropos of the rota. "Two more shows and then we tell the truth," she
says, and I catch, for the first time, a note of something underneath
the certainty — not doubt exactly, but the specific caution of someone
who has learned, the hard way, that plans made in a kitchen on a
Tuesday morning don't always survive contact with a live studio.

"You alright?" I ask.

"I'm fine," she says, and then, correcting herself, because we've
both got better at this over six weeks: "I'm nervous. Telling people
on our own terms is still telling people. I've spent two years making
sure nobody got to do that to me again, and I'm about to do it to
myself, on purpose, live, twice."

"We don't have to do it live."

"Yes, we do," Nadia says. "If it's not live, it's not really ours.
It's just another recording someone else could get their hands on."

I don't argue with that, because she's right, the way she is about
almost everything, and because I've learned, by now, that the things
worth doing properly are rarely the comfortable ones.

·

Broadcast five happens, in the end, almost entirely without incident,
which in hindsight I understand as the calm the whole book has been
saving up its weather for. A gentle hour. A caller with a genuinely
funny complaint about a mother-in-law's Sunday roast schedule that
gets resolved, on air, with a compromise nobody in the studio expected
to actually work and that, according to the messages afterward,
apparently did. Nadia and I fall, for the full thirty minutes, into
the easy rhythm we've built over five weeks without either of us once
thinking about the rundown.

Afterward, in the corridor, she says, quietly, "one more."

"One more," I agree.

"And then it's just us."

"And then it's just us," I say back.

·

We spend that evening at my flat, for once rather than hers, and Nadia
looks around it properly for the first time — the good armchair, the
lack of anything on the walls, the specific bareness of a place built
by someone who never expected to need to explain it to a guest.

"You could put something on the walls, you know," she says, turning
slowly on the spot in my living room. "It wouldn't kill the aesthetic.
There is, currently, no aesthetic to kill."

"I never got round to it."

"You've lived here nine years."

"I've been busy."

"You've been avoiding making anything look permanent," Nadia says,
with the specific gentleness she uses for observations she knows will
land somewhere tender, "in case you had to leave it in a hurry. I know
that particular decorating philosophy. I've practised it myself, for
considerably longer than nine years."

I don't argue with her, because she's right, and because I'm slowly
learning that the correct response to her being right isn't defence,
it's just sitting with it until it stops stinging.

"What would you put up," I ask, "if it were your flat?"

She considers the question properly, walking the room once more.
"Something from before. Not a photo — I don't have many of those I'd
want on a wall. Something that just meant a thing, once, to someone,
and still does, even if nobody else looking at it would ever guess
why."

"Like what?"

"I'll show you sometime," she says, which isn't a no, and isn't quite
a yes either, and I let it sit exactly where she's left it, because
I've learned by now that some doors open slowly, on their own
schedule, and pushing on them only ever makes them stick.

Priya, in the meantime, has been quietly rebuilding relationships of
her own — specifically with Dev, whose recovery from food poisoning
has left him with a slightly overcorrected sense of duty toward the
station, and who has taken to arriving early for every single shift,
checking every cable twice, in a way that Priya finds both touching
and, by her own admission, mildly exhausting to manage.

"He's apologising to the building now," she tells me, with the
specific weary fondness of someone describing a friend's endearing but
tiresome habit. "Not to me. Not to you. To the actual building, out
loud, like it's got feelings about the shortbread incident. I told him
it's fine. He said fine isn't the same as forgiven. I said in this
building it usually is. He didn't believe me."

"He'll come round."

"He will," Priya agrees, "once he stops treating every single shift
like a redemption arc. Some of us just want him to check the cable and
go home at a reasonable hour, redemption arc optional."

I laugh at that, properly, and file it away alongside everything else
this small, strange, over-loyal community has taught me this autumn —
that most of the actual work of running a place like this isn't the
roof, or the panel, or even the show, but the steady, unglamorous
business of people forgiving each other for small things quickly, so
that when a large thing eventually comes along, there's enough goodwill
already banked to survive it.

I ring Fiona that same week to tell her, finally, in full, that the six
weeks have turned into something real, and that we're planning to say
so publicly once the last broadcast is done.

"I knew it," Fiona says, with enormous satisfaction. "I knew it before
you did, probably, which I recognise is an obnoxious thing to say, but
I'm going to say it anyway because I've earned the right after years
of watching you not have this."

"You didn't know it. You were worried I was building a surveillance
file on a woman I'd never spoken to."

"That was week one. This is week six. People change their minds when
the evidence changes," Fiona says, entirely unbothered by the
correction. "I'm coming down for the last broadcast, by the way. I've
already booked the train. I want to be in the room, or as close to the
room as they'll let a member of the public get, when you two finally
say it properly, on the record, to everyone."

"That's very sentimental of you."

"I contain multitudes," Fiona says, echoing a phrase she's clearly
picked up secondhand from somewhere in my own retelling of this whole
autumn, and rings off before I can ask her where she got it, leaving me
smiling at my phone in a way that's become, this month, something of a
theme.

Priya, told the news properly rather than via the group chat this
time, insists on marking it with a hand-drawn certificate, presented
with enormous ceremony in the kitchen, that reads, in full: THIS
CERTIFIES THAT ROBYN AND NADIA ARE, AS OF THIS WEEK, ACTUALLY REAL,
WITNESSED BY PRIYA (AGE 17, WHO KNEW ALL ALONG).

"You didn't know all along," I say. "You suspected. There's a
difference."

"I knew," Priya says, with the specific unshakeable confidence of a
teenager rewriting her own history in real time, "from approximately
the third broadcast, and I would like that documented for posterity,
alongside this certificate, which I have laminated, because I take
my documentation seriously."

"You laminated it."

"I laminated it at the library, on my lunch break, because some
occasions deserve permanence." She hands it over with genuine, if
slightly overwrought, solemnity, and I take it, and it goes, eventually,
onto the fridge in my own flat, next to nothing else at all, the first
thing I've put up on a wall in nine years that I didn't immediately
regret making permanent.

We fall asleep that night with the radio on, low, tuned to nothing in
particular, just the specific hush of a station between programmes,
and it is, I think afterward, the most ordinary and the most
extraordinary night of the entire six weeks — nothing happening in it
at all except two people who have run out of reasons to be anywhere
else, and neither of us has the faintest idea, falling asleep to
static and rain, how completely wrong that particular piece of
optimism is about to turn out to be.

## Chapter Four — On the Level

The kettle in the small meeting room takes four minutes, which I know
because I have now, over the course of one Tuesday afternoon, watched
it complete an entire cycle three times, and each time I have thought,
*surely this is not a load-bearing detail of my life*, and each time I
have been wrong.

We have a whiteboard. We have forty minutes before Priya needs the
room back for something she's calling "admin" and which I strongly
suspect is homework. We do not, forty minutes in, have a show.

"Two of Us," I say, writing it up, then immediately regretting the
handwriting, which slants downward in a way that makes it look like a
threat. "Or — The Chat. Or—"

"No," Nadia says, not looking up from the fader she's cleaning with a
cotton bud, an activity she appears to find more interesting than
either of my titles.

"You haven't heard the third one."

"I don't need to. You're doing the thing where you make it sound like
a segment on a breakfast show that got cancelled in 2011."

"That's oddly specific."

"I've engineered a lot of segments that got cancelled in 2011."

I write CALL IT SOMETHING on the whiteboard, mostly to give my hands
something to do, and try a different angle. "Okay. What do you call a
signal that's telling the truth?"

"On the level," Nadia says, immediately, still cleaning the fader,
clearly not registering that she has said anything at all. "That's
what the desk says when a signal's honest. On the level. No distortion,
no clipping, just—" She looks up, finally, and sees my face, and
stops. "What."

"That's the name."

"That's a technical term."

"That's the name," I say again, already writing it on the whiteboard
in letters that, this time, slant upward, because apparently my
handwriting has opinions about content. "On the Level. It's got
everything. It's got the pun. It's got the promise. It sounds like
something people would actually tell their pals to listen to."

Nadia looks at the whiteboard for a long moment, the specific stillness
of someone recalculating something they thought was already settled.

"I didn't mean it as a name," she says.

"I know. That's why it's a good one."

She doesn't argue with that, which from her, I am learning, functions
as a form of agreement. She goes back to the fader. I stand there
looking at four words on a whiteboard that used to be a phrase about
signal quality and is now, apparently, the name of my career-saving,
completely fictional relationship, and something about that — the
fact that the whole premise of the thing got built out of her
vocabulary and my desperation, in equal parts, without either of us
deciding it on purpose — makes me strangely, disproportionately happy.

We spend the rest of the forty minutes on the actual bones of the
thing, which turns out to be the part Nadia has genuinely strong
opinions about, once you get her past the naming. Thirty minutes,
she says, not sixty — a hard-out, no matter what's happening on air,
because a caller with a genuinely unsolvable problem will happily eat
your whole runtime if you let them and the show needs to feel finite
to feel safe. Three call slots, screened, never more, because a fourth
slot is where shows like this start performing crisis instead of
having conversations. And music beds under everything except the
actual advice, because — and she says this like it's obvious, the way
she says most things that turn out to be the smartest thing said in
the room — "silence makes people perform sincerity. A bed under it
lets them just be sincere."

I write all of it down. I am aware, writing it down, that I am taking
dictation from a woman I asked to be my fake girlfriend four days ago
and who has, in the intervening ninety-six hours, developed and
delivered an entire theory of live radio intimacy without once being
asked to.

"You've thought about this a lot," I say.

"I've engineered a lot of shows that got this wrong," she says, which
is, I am starting to understand, her version of *yes, extensively,
and it mattered to me*.

·

Save the cat, if you want to call it that, which nobody in this
building would, happens three nights later at three in the morning,
when a volunteer called Dev texts the group chat to say he's got food
poisoning and cannot, physically, be trusted near a live mic for the
next several hours.

I do not wake anyone up. I do not ask Priya, who is seventeen and
should, by any reasonable standard, be asleep. I get in the car and I
go and I sit down at a desk I have not properly operated in four years
and I am, immediately and comprehensively, terrible at it.

I hit the wrong cart. I ride a fader the wrong way and get a wall of
silence that runs eleven seconds too long, which on overnight radio
feels, I promise you, like an hour, like the station has quietly died
and nobody has told the two listeners currently awake to notice.

I want it understood that I used to be good at this. Before I was a
manager, before the roof and the spreadsheets and the licensing
panel, I was a presenter, properly, for six years, and I could ride a
desk in my sleep. It is a specific kind of humbling, at half past
three in the morning, to discover exactly how much of that skill has
quietly atrophied while I was busy being indispensable at everything
else instead.

Through the glass — because of course she's there, she's always there,
it's her actual shift and I am the one intruding on it — Nadia reaches
over from the next desk and fixes it without a word. Slides a level
across. Fires the next cart at exactly the right second. Doesn't look
at me like I'm an idiot, which I am, in this specific and narrow
professional context, being.

We sit together in the dead hour after, two mugs of tea gone properly
cold, and don't talk about the show at all, which — I realise, holding
the mug for warmth it no longer has — is the first time that's
happened since this whole thing started.

"You didn't have to do that," Nadia says, eventually, meaning the
shift, not the fix.

"I know," I say. "I don't know how to not."

She looks at me for a second in the particular way she has, head
tilted a few degrees off centre, listening to something that isn't
sound.

"I noticed," is all she says, and goes back to the desk, and I sit
there in the three a.m. quiet turning that word over — *noticed* — like
it's a fact I've been handed and haven't worked out yet what to do
with.

Priya finds out about it the next afternoon, from Dev, who is
mortified and apologetic and brings in a box of shortbread by way of
penance, which Priya accepts with the specific graciousness of a
seventeen-year-old who has already decided the real story here isn't
Dev's stomach.

"You covered a three a.m. shift," she says to me, in the tone of a
woman assembling a case, "instead of calling me, or Marcus, or
literally anyone else on the volunteer list, at an hour when you
could have called any of us and we'd have understood completely."

"It was three in the morning. I wasn't going to wake anyone up."

"You woke *yourself* up."

"That's different."

"It is not different," Priya says, with the weary patience of someone
explaining a very simple fact to someone who should, by rights, already
know it. "It's the same thing, except you did it to the one person in
this building who's allowed to be tired without anybody minding."

I don't have a good answer to that, so I eat a piece of Dev's
shortbread instead, and let her be right about it, which is, I am
starting to notice, becoming something of a pattern this month.

·

Dev, for what it's worth, recovers fully within two days and turns up
to his next shift with a second box of shortbread and a story about
the specific takeaway he now believes was responsible, delivered with
the wounded pride of a man who has never once in his volunteering
career let the station down and is finding the experience of having
done so, even accidentally, even for a stomach bug entirely outside
his control, close to unbearable.

"You've apologised four times," I tell him. "It's a stomach bug, Dev.
Nobody's angry."

"I know that. I'm angry on the station's behalf."

"The station isn't angry either."

"Well, it should be a *bit* angry," Dev says, with the specific
stubbornness of a man who would clearly rather be blamed for something
than let something go unblamed at all, and I leave him to it, because
some people process a small guilt best by being allowed to hold onto
it a while longer than is strictly necessary, and Dev, bless him, is
one of them.

I mention the whole thing to Nadia later, expecting a shrug. Instead
she says, "he's not wrong to feel bad about it, even if the reason's
daft. It's a good instinct to have around here. People who don't feel
bad about letting the desk down are usually the ones who end up
letting it down properly, eventually, when it actually matters."

I file that thought away too, along with everything else she's said
this week that turns out, on reflection, to be smarter than it sounded
at the time.

·

The format document itself, once Nadia's finished with it, is a
genuinely impressive piece of work — four pages, properly structured,
with a rundown template, a call-screening protocol adapted from
something she clearly learned on tour, and a section titled TONE that
reads, in full: *warm, not saccharine. Honest, not cruel. If in doubt,
underplay it — the audience will do the rest of the emotional work
themselves if you give them room to.*

I read that section three times before I fully absorb it, because it
is, without question, the single best piece of editorial guidance
anyone has given me in nine years of running this station, and it's
come from a woman who, four weeks ago, I'd have sworn had never once
thought about broadcasting philosophy in her life.

"Where did you learn to write like this," I ask her.

"I didn't learn to write like this. I learned to listen to a room and
work out what it needs, and then I wrote down what I noticed." She
shrugs, entirely unbothered by the compliment, in the way she's
unbothered by most compliments, treating them as data rather than
gifts. "It's the same skill as sound design, really. You're not
adding anything. You're just getting out of the way of what's already
true in the room."

I think about that sentence for a long time afterward — *getting out
of the way of what's already true* — and wonder, not for the first
time this month, how much of my own management style has been the
precise opposite: adding, managing, filling every silence with
something, rather than trusting a room, or a person, to hold its own
truth without my intervention.

·

Malcolm turns up on the Wednesday, unannounced, the way he always
does, using the key he was technically supposed to hand back eleven
years ago and that nobody's ever had the heart to ask for, walking
stick first down the basement stairs, moving slower than he used to
but with exactly the same look on his face when he clocks the state of
BIG LEAK's bucket.

"Still not fixed the roof, then," he says, by way of greeting.

"Working on it."

"You've been working on it since I handed you the keys." He lowers
himself into the good chair without asking, which Priya, for once,
allows without comment, out of what I can only assume is inherited
deference to a man who ran this place for eleven years on pure
stubbornness before she was born. "Heard you've promised the panel a
love story."

"Word travels fast."

"Word's always travelled fast in this building. It's the one piece of
broadcast equipment that's never once needed maintenance." He looks at
me properly, the way he used to when I was nineteen and certain I was
about to be told I'd done something wrong. "Is it real?"

"Define real."

"That's not an answer, Robyn, that's a dodge, and I taught you better
dodges than that." He nods toward the studio, where Nadia's shape is
visible through the glass, headphones half on. "Is she going to get
hurt by this?"

"I hope not. I've tried to make sure not."

"Trying's not nothing," Malcolm says, "but it's not the whole job
either." He's quiet for a moment, looking round the basement at
everything that's changed and everything that hasn't in eleven years —
the same cracked ceiling tiles, the same hand-painted sign upstairs,
the same amber bulb over the studio door that's never once been
replaced with a proper red one because, as he told me once, a long
time ago, *amber suits a place that never quite finished becoming
anything.*

"I told you once," he says, "don't let it become boring, and don't let
it close. I never once told you what to do about anything in between
those two instructions, because I didn't know either, and I still
don't. But I'll tell you this for free, since I'm here and you've got
tea on: whatever you're building with that woman in there, real or
otherwise, don't let it become the third thing on the list. Don't let
it become boring, don't let it close, and don't let it become a job.
You've only got the two rules to actually go by. Don't invent a third
one just because you're frightened of the second."

He leaves twenty minutes later, having drunk exactly half a cup of tea
and eaten three of Priya's biscuits without asking, the same as he's
done every visit for eleven years, and I stand in the basement for a
while after he's gone turning his instruction over, wondering how a
man who ran this place on pure stubbornness managed, in the space of
one visit, to say the truest thing anyone's said to me all week.

·

Priya, who has been listening to the whole exchange from the corridor
under the thin pretence of sorting cue cards, corners me the moment
Malcolm's gone.

"He likes her," she announces. "You should know that. Malcolm doesn't
ask questions like that about people he doesn't already like."

"He's met her twice."

"Malcolm decided he liked me within about four minutes of meeting me,
and I was fourteen and extremely rude to him about the tea rota," Priya
says, with the specific authority of someone citing established
precedent. "He doesn't need long. He just needs to watch someone do
one true thing, and he's made his mind up. I'd say bringing tea to the
overnight desk unasked probably counted, if word's got round to him
already, which, in this building, it obviously has."

"Word travels fast in this building."

"It's the one piece of broadcast equipment that never needs
maintenance," Priya says, echoing Malcolm's own line back at me with
such precise mimicry of his gravelly delivery that I laugh out loud for
the first time all week, properly, the kind of laugh that surprises
you on the way out.

"You've been rehearsing that impression."

"I've been rehearsing it for years," Priya admits, entirely unashamed.
"I do a very good Douglas too, if you ever want to hear it, though I
save that one for occasions that really call for it."

"When would that be?"

"Committee meetings, mostly," Priya says, "in my own head, since
nobody's ever going to let a seventeen-year-old into an actual
committee meeting to do it there," and goes back to the cue cards,
leaving me standing in the basement with the specific warm feeling of
a place that, whatever else happens with the panel, has never once, in
nine years, run short of people who care about it properly.

## Chapter Nineteen — Hot Mic

Here is how it happens, because it matters, afterward, that it happens
exactly this plainly and this stupidly and not in any of the ways
either of us would have braced for.

Priya is updating the automation queue on the Wednesday afternoon —
the ordinary admin that keeps the overnight feed running itself between
midnight and six, stings and idents and the archive loop nobody
listens to — and she drags the wrong file into the wrong slot. That's
the whole of it. No malice, no carelessness even, really, just the
single most ordinary kind of accident there is: a folder with two
similarly named files in it, a Tuesday afternoon, a seventeen-year-old
trusted with more of this building than anyone her age should
reasonably be trusted with, doing the job the way she always does it,
and getting, this once, unlucky.

The file that airs is a recording made weeks ago in the tech cupboard,
while Nadia was testing a new mic, never meant to leave the machine it
was made on. It has the two of us on it, talking — really talking,
not for anyone, about the podcast, about being loved as a bit rather
than as a person, about things Nadia has never once said to another
living soul.

It plays out over the automated overnight feed at half past midnight,
with a timestamp on it, to however many people happen to be awake and
listening.

·

I find out at seven in the morning, from Priya, who is crying before
she's finished the first sentence, which tells me everything I need to
know about how badly this has already gone before I've heard a single
detail.

"I dragged the wrong file," she says. "I checked the name, I swear I
checked the name, and it was—" She can't finish it. She doesn't have
to. I've already opened the automation log myself, and I can see
exactly which file went out, and exactly how long it ran, and exactly
how many listens it's had before anyone thought to pull it.

"How many people were listening at half past midnight," I ask, mostly
because I need a number to hold onto, something with edges, before I
can deal with anything that doesn't.

"Forty-one," Priya says. "That's what the log says. Forty-one people."

Forty-one people is nothing, in the scheme of things — less than a
busy Thursday broadcast, less than a tenth of what On the Level pulls
in on a good week. It is also, I understand immediately and
completely, not nothing at all, because it is forty-one people who
heard something Nadia has spent two years making sure nobody heard,
and the number of listeners has never once, in the whole history of
this, been the actual measure of the damage.

Priya is still talking, in the specific rapid, unstoppable way people
talk when they're trying to outrun their own guilt with words. "I've
already pulled it from the loop, it can't play again, I checked the
file three times before I put it in and I don't know how I got it
wrong, I've done that queue a hundred times, I don't understand how
I—"

"Priya." I make myself say it gently, because whatever else this
morning is, it is not her fault in any way that deserves this much of
her crying alone in a corridor about it. "It's an accident. I need you
to hear that from me, clearly, before anything else happens today.
You didn't do this on purpose. Nobody thinks you did this on purpose."

"Nadia might."

"Nadia won't," I say, with a confidence I don't, in this exact moment,
actually have.

I call Nadia. She doesn't answer. I call again. She doesn't answer
that either, and by the third call I am standing in the office with my
hand shaking in a way I don't remember deciding to let happen.

I find her at the overnight studio door, which is shut, in a way I've
never once seen it shut during her actual shift, not even on the
worst night either of us has had.

"Nadia."

Nothing.

"Nadia, I know what happened. Priya's told me. It was a mistake, it
was an accident, nobody did this to you on—"

"It doesn't matter that it was an accident."

Her voice, through the door, is the flattest I have ever heard it — not
the professional flat, the working flat, the flat she uses to say true
things. This is a different flat entirely. This is the sound of
someone who has gone somewhere I can't follow her to yet.

"I told you," she says. "The podcast. Being a story instead of a
person. I told you what that costs, and I told you I needed it to stay
off the record, and it didn't. It's out there. With a timestamp. With
my actual voice on it, saying actual things, and I don't get to decide
who heard it or what they do with it, and that's — that's the entire
thing, Robyn. That's the exact thing."

"I promised you—"

"I know what you promised me," she says, and for the first time there
is something in her voice besides flatness, something that sounds,
horribly, like it might be the beginning of tears she is not going to
let me hear finish. "I know it wasn't you. I know it was an accident. I
believe every single word of that, and none of it changes what's
sitting on the internet right now with a timestamp on it."

I don't have an answer for that, because there isn't one. There is no
sentence available to me that makes an accident stop being true.

"Can I do anything," I ask, which is, I recognise even as I'm saying
it, the single most useless question available, the caretaker's
reflex firing uselessly against a door that has nothing behind it for
me to fix.

"No," Nadia says. Not cruel. Just accurate. "That's the whole of it,
Robyn. There isn't a thing to do. That's what this is. You can't do
anything, and I can't undo forty-one people having heard my actual
voice say actual things, and neither of those facts is going to change
no matter how long either of us stands on either side of this door."

I stand there anyway, for a long time, because leaving feels like a
second kind of abandonment on top of the first, even though staying
accomplishes precisely nothing that she's just told me, plainly, it
would accomplish. Eventually the silence on the other side of the
door goes on long enough that I understand she isn't coming out, not
this morning, maybe not today at all, and I make myself walk away
from a door I have never once, in six years, walked away from while
someone was still upset on the other side of it.

·

Within the hour, the panel's office calls.

"Manufactured content becoming a liability," is the phrase the admin
uses, reading it, I suspect, off a prepared line someone more senior
gave her. "The panel will need to reconsider the renewal in light of—"

I don't hear the rest of the sentence properly. I'm still standing
outside a shut studio door that has not opened once, not for me, not
for Priya, not for anyone, and somewhere behind it is a woman whose
worst and most specific fear I have just watched come true, by
accident, through nobody's malice, which turns out to make it no
easier to bear at all.

·

I spend the rest of that day doing the only thing I know how to do
under pressure, which is manage the parts of the catastrophe that will
hold still long enough to be managed.

I call the panel's admin back and get, for my trouble, twenty minutes
of hold music and then a further conversation with Douglas himself,
who uses the phrase *optics* four times in ninety seconds and never
once asks how Nadia is. I draft a statement for the station's own
social accounts and delete it three times, because every version
reads like an institution protecting itself rather than a workplace
that has genuinely hurt somebody, and I am not willing to put my name
to that, whatever it costs the renewal.

I check the automation logs again, properly this time, all the way
through, and confirm what I already suspected: the file sat in a
folder with eleven other similarly named recordings, all of them test
files from the mic upgrade in July, none of them ever meant for
broadcast, an entire folder that should have been archived and deleted
weeks ago and wasn't, because archiving old test files was a task on
a list that, like most such tasks, kept losing to more urgent things.
It is, in the end, my failure as much as Priya's — a system I should
have had properly tidied months before a seventeen-year-old ever had
to touch it under pressure.

I find Priya in the kitchen, still red-eyed, staring at a mug of tea
she hasn't drunk.

"It was an old test file," I tell her. "It should have been deleted in
July. That it wasn't isn't your fault, it's mine — I run this
building, and the folder structure was mine to fix, and I didn't fix
it, because I was too busy fixing everything more visible instead."

"That doesn't make me feel better."

"It isn't supposed to make either of us feel better," I say. "It's
supposed to be accurate. Feeling better can come later, once we've
actually said the true thing about who let this happen and how."

Priya doesn't say anything for a while. Then: "Is she going to be
alright?"

"I don't know," I say, which is the truest thing I've said all day,
and the hardest one to leave sitting in a kitchen with a seventeen-
year-old who wants, more than anything, to be told it will all be
fine.

·

I go back to the overnight studio door twice more that day, and both
times leave without knocking, because there is a version of persistence
that becomes its own kind of pressure, and I am, for the first time in
my adult life, genuinely trying to tell the difference before I cross
it.

The clip, meanwhile, does the specific ugly thing recordings do once
they're loose in the world. Somebody's cut it down to ninety seconds
and shared it in a Glasgow community group, with a caption that reads,
with the particular cruelty of people who mistake curiosity for
entitlement, *is this the real story behind Kelvin Row's "couple"?*
By evening it's had several hundred views, which is nothing by the
standards of the actual internet and everything by the standards of a
woman who has spent two years building a life specifically designed to
never again be several hundred people's evening's entertainment.

I don't share any of this with Nadia directly — she'll have seen it
herself, I have no doubt, and the last thing she needs from me is a
running commentary on exactly how bad it's got. I do, however, spend
an hour I don't really have drafting a polite, entirely reasonable
message to the group's admin, asking that the clip be taken down out
of basic respect for a private individual, and am mildly astonished
when it actually works within the hour, a small, unglamorous act of
internet housekeeping that does nothing to undo what's already been
heard but at least stops the number from climbing any further.

It's not a fix. Nothing about that day has a fix. But it's something,
and something, on a day with nothing else available, turns out to
matter more than I'd have guessed.

·

I don't sleep at all that night, and around two in the morning I give
up pretending I might, and sit at the kitchen table with a notebook,
doing the thing I do when a problem refuses to resolve, which is
write down everything I actually know rather than everything I'm
afraid of.

What I know: it was an accident. What I know: Nadia's fear was never
about accidents specifically, it was about control — about who gets
to decide when her voice becomes public and on what terms — and an
accident is, in its own way, the purest possible violation of that,
worse in some respects than malice, because there's no one to be
angry at, nothing to push against, just a fact that has already
happened and cannot be made to un-happen by any amount of apologising.

What I know: I cannot fix this by managing it. What I know: I am
going to want to try anyway, repeatedly, for days, because managing
things is the only language I have ever been fluent in, and learning
a new one under this much pressure is going to be slow and clumsy and
I am going to get it wrong more than once before I get it right.

I write all of that down, in full, at two in the morning, and it
doesn't make the night any shorter, but it does give me something to
hold onto besides the specific helplessness of standing outside a
closed door with nothing useful to offer the person behind it.

·

Fiona rings at seven, having seen some version of the clip herself —
it's reached Aberdeen too, apparently, in the specific way bad news
travels faster and further than good news ever manages — and doesn't
waste time on preamble.

"How bad," she says.

"Bad."

"Is she going to be alright?"

"I don't know," I say, the same honest, unmanaged answer I'll give
Priya a few hours later, because it's the only true one currently
available to me. "I don't think 'alright' is even the right question
yet. Right now it's just about getting through today without making
it worse."

"What do you need from me?"

"Nothing, actually. I just needed to say it out loud to someone who
isn't in the building, before I have to go back in and be steady for
everyone who is."

"Then say it," Fiona says, gently. "I'm listening. Take as long as you
need."

I do, in the end, say more of it than I'd planned to — the automation
log, the forty-one listeners, the specific flatness in Nadia's voice
through a shut door — and Fiona listens the whole way through without
once trying to fix any of it, which I understand, now, having spent two
months learning the difference from a woman with headphone cups and a
level meter, to be the single most useful thing anyone can offer
another person on a morning like this one.

"I love you," Fiona says, at the end of it. "Go back in. Be steady.
Ring me tonight, whatever's happened by then."

"I will."

I put the phone down and sit for a moment longer in the grey early
light, gathering whatever version of steady I can currently manage,
before going back out to a building that, today more than any other
day since I've run it, needs every scrap of it I've got.

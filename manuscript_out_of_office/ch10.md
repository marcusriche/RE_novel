## Chapter Ten — The Brief for Broadcast Two

We're prepping broadcast two off a caller's letter that's actually
tricky — a couple arguing about a shared bank account, one of them
wanting to keep saving separately, the other reading that as a door
half-shut — and for the first time neither of us has to fake having an
opinion about it.

"You're wrong about the joke on page two," Nadia says, not looking up
from the rundown.

"I am not."

"It's a good joke. It's wrong for the caller. She's not going to want
to laugh at the account thing, she's going to want someone to say the
account thing is reasonable and the door thing is also reasonable and
that they're not the same argument." She crosses it out with the
station biro, the one on the string. "Cut it."

I cut it. She's right, which is becoming a genuinely inconvenient
pattern, one I've started privately cataloguing: Nadia's right about
the joke on page two. Nadia's right about which caller to screen out.
Nadia was right, three weeks ago, about the thermostat, the one thing
in this building I'd been prepared to argue about to the death, and
she settled it in one sentence by pointing out that the radiator in
the corridor runs off the same circuit and nobody had ever checked.

"You could have told me that a year ago," I say, apropos of the
thermostat, apropos of nothing, apropos of the general pattern.

"You never asked."

"I've been managing this building for six years. I don't get to ask
the engineer basic questions about wiring, I'm meant to already know."

Nadia looks at me properly for the first time all afternoon. "That's
possibly the most Robyn sentence you've said to me yet," she says,
"and I want you to notice that I mean it as a diagnosis, not a
compliment."

Somewhere in the second hour of prep, a bit forms that neither of us
builds on purpose: Nadia mutters the actual, technically correct answer
into the talkback under her breath, and I repeat it on air two minutes
later as though it were my own wisdom, and it works, every single
time, and by the third instance we're both laughing about it in a way
that has stopped feeling like homework and started feeling like
something closer to a language — one with its own grammar, its own
private jokes, built entirely out of six weeks of forced proximity and
neither of us noticing, until now, quite how fluent we've become in
it.

·

Second broadcast, kept mercifully brief on the page, because there are
only so many times you can describe a red bulb and a level meter
before the reader starts wondering if anything else happens in this
basement: the account caller gets a real, useful answer — Nadia's,
mostly, delivered in my voice — and there's no stunt beat this week,
deliberately. We agreed, off mic, that a kiss-of-the-week format would
be worse for everyone, us very much included.

There is one moment, though, that I want on the record properly,
because it's the first time a caller has made either of us go quiet
rather than clever. A man rings in — older, careful with his words,
the kind of caller who's clearly rehearsed the call three times on his
own landing before dialling — and asks, simply, whether it's normal to
still be a little frightened of your own wife after thirty years of
marriage, not frightened of her exactly, frightened of getting it
wrong, of the account, the door, the whole architecture of a life you
built together starting to show a crack you can't fix on your own.

Nadia answers that one. Not with a level, not with a timing. She says,
"It's not about being frightened of her. It's about being frightened
that you'll stop being someone worth staying with. Those are
different fears, and only one of them is actually about your wife."

There's a silence on the line, and then the caller says, quietly,
"aye, that's it exactly," and hangs up before either of us can say
anything else, and Priya has to fade the next sting in eleven seconds
early because none of us, for a moment, trusts our own voice.

A small crowd of regulars has started phoning in just to hear the two
of us argue about the studio thermostat, which nobody scripted and
nobody can explain, and Priya's spreadsheet gets a second tab, titled,
with the specific bluntness of a seventeen-year-old who has stopped
pretending she doesn't have opinions about our fictional relationship,
REGULARS WHO ARE ACTUALLY HERE FOR THEM.

I ask her, once, whether she thinks the audience can tell the
difference — between the segment and whatever's actually happening
underneath it. Priya looks at me the way you look at someone who has
asked a question they already know the answer to and are hoping,
against the evidence, that you'll disagree with them.

"Robyn," she says, "I don't think there's a difference left to tell."

I don't have an answer for that, so I don't try to give her one, and
go back to the rundown, and don't think about it again for at least
several hours, which — for a woman who thinks about everything, at
length, usually at four in the morning — counts, this week, as
enormous restraint.

·

Priya's own life, I am slowly starting to learn, is considerably more
complicated than the version of her that runs a desk with such
frightening competence four nights a week would suggest.

She lives with her mum in a flat off Maryhill Road, one of three
children, the only one still at home, and does two Highers alongside
the station work, which is more than most adults I know manage to
balance without something giving. Her mum works nights at the Queen
Elizabeth, which is, Priya mentioned once, in passing, entirely without
self-pity, the actual reason she ended up at Kelvin Row in the first
place — an empty flat on a Tuesday evening and a radio advert for
volunteers that happened to be on the one time she was listening.

"I like it here better than at school," she told me once, months ago,
apropos of nothing, stacking cue sheets. "At school I'm seventeen. Here
I'm just good at something. Nobody checks my age before they let me
run a desk. They just check whether the desk's been run properly."

I think about that sentence more than I probably should, given
everything else currently occupying my attention. There's a version
of this whole strange autumn where Priya is the actual heart of it —
not the fake couple, not the panel, not the roof, but a seventeen-year-
old who found the one room in the city where being good at something
mattered more than being a particular age, and who has, without ever
being asked to, made it her whole personality.

I don't tell her any of this. She would, correctly, find it
mortifying. I just make sure, more carefully than I used to, that the
rota never runs her past what a seventeen-year-old should reasonably
be asked to do, even when she insists, loudly and often, that she's
fine.

·

Nadia clocks the pattern before I've fully admitted it to myself —
that I've started, without deciding to, protecting Priya the way
nobody protected me at that age, filling in the gaps I fell through
myself at nineteen with nobody standing close enough to catch me.

"You know you can't fix your own nineteen through her, don't you," she
says, one evening, gently, in the specific tone she reserves for
observations she's fairly sure will land badly and has decided to make
anyway because she thinks they matter more than the discomfort of
saying them.

"I'm not doing that."

"You're doing a version of that. It's not a bad version. She's lucky
to have it. I just want you to notice you're doing it, because the
version of care that comes from an old wound and the version that
comes from actually looking at the person in front of you aren't
always the same thing, even when they look identical from the
outside."

I sit with that for longer than I'd like to admit. She's right, of
course — she generally is — but there's something in the noticing
itself that feels, for once, less like an accusation and more like
being handed a tool I didn't know I needed. I start trying, after
that, to ask Priya what *she* actually wants from this place, rather
than simply assuming I already know, having once been seventeen and
unasked myself.

I ask her properly, one quiet evening, sitting on the good chair while
she runs the desk from the spare stool, a reversal of the usual
arrangement that she clocks immediately and finds, I think, more
significant than I'd intended it to be.

"What do you actually want out of this place," I say. "Not what you
think I want to hear. The real answer."

Priya doesn't answer straight away, which is unusual for her, and I
let the silence run rather than filling it, the way I've been slowly
learning to.

"I want to run something," she says, eventually. "Not this exact
thing, necessarily. But something like it. Something where nobody
checks whether I'm old enough before they let me be good at it." She
turns a fader idly, not really adjusting anything, just needing
somewhere to put her hands while she says something true. "Everyone at
school talks about university like it's the whole of what happens
next. Nobody talks about the version where you just get very good at
something real, early, and build outward from there."

"You could do both."

"I could. I probably will, if I'm honest — Mum would never forgive me
if I didn't at least try for it properly. But I wanted you to know
that this isn't a stopgap for me. It's not something I'm doing until
my real life starts. This is my real life. I'd like that taken
seriously, rather than treated like something charming a teenager does
on Saturdays."

"It's taken seriously," I say. "It has been for a while. I don't
think I've said that to you properly, though, so: it is. You're not
a volunteer who happens to be good. You're one of the best producers
this station's ever had, and I include several decades of paid adults
in that assessment."

Priya doesn't say anything for a moment, and when she does, her voice
has lost some of its usual armour. "Nobody's ever said that to me
using the word *producer* before. Everyone says *helper.* Or *the
young one.*"

"You're not the young one here. You're Priya, and you run a desk
better than most people twice your age, and I should have told you
that a long time before this week made me actually stop and look at
you properly."

She goes back to the fader, visibly working to compose herself, and I
let her have the moment without commentary, because some things, once
said, don't need to be said again to be believed — they just need
time to actually land.

·

I catch Nadia, later that same evening, looking at an envelope on the
side table by the overnight desk that I've never seen before and don't
recognise — heavy paper, a return address I can't read from where I'm
standing, postmarked somewhere that isn't Glasgow.

She sees me clock it and moves it, unhurriedly, into her bag, without
comment, in the specific unhurried way that is somehow louder than if
she'd snatched it away.

"You don't have to tell me what that is," I say.

"I know I don't."

"I'm not asking."

"I know you're not." She zips the bag, finally, and looks at me
properly. "It's from a theatre company. The touring one. They want an
answer about something, and I haven't given them one, and I'm not
ready to talk about why, and I'd like you to let that be enough for
now, the way I let the London thing be enough for you, without
either of us actually explaining it to the other."

I go very still at that, because I hadn't told her about London — not
directly, not in so many words — and yet here she is, naming it
plainly, in a tone that makes it clear she's known for a while and
has simply been waiting, the way she waits for most things, for me to
either bring it to her myself or not.

"How long have you known?"

"Since the reminder went off on your phone in the kitchen and you
turned it face-down so fast you nearly knocked over your tea," Nadia
says. "I'm an engineer, Robyn. I notice things people try to hide.
It's the whole job."

"I should have told you."

"You should have," she agrees, without any particular heat in it,
"but I understand why you didn't, because I'm currently doing the
exact same thing to you with an envelope, so I'm not in a strong
position to be angry about it." A pause. "Same rule as the podcast.
I'm not owed your London decision on my timeline. You're not owed my
envelope on yours. We tell each other when we're ready, and until
then, we don't pretend the not-telling isn't happening. That's the
part that actually matters — not the secret itself, just being honest
about the fact that there's one."

I think about that distinction for a long time afterward — the
difference between a secret kept and a secret denied — and find,
turning it over on the walk home, that it's possibly the most useful
piece of relationship architecture anyone's ever handed me, real or
fake, and that I intend to hold onto it considerably longer than the
six weeks currently on the contract.

·

I tell Fiona about the envelope rule that same week, in broad strokes
rather than specifics, mostly because I want to say it out loud to
someone and hear whether it sounds as wise repeated back as it did in
the moment, or whether it was simply Nadia's particular gift for
sounding wise about anything she says plainly enough.

"That's actually rather good," Fiona says, after a pause, sounding
faintly begrudging about it, the specific tone of an older sister
conceding a point she'd rather have made herself. "I wish someone had
told me that fifteen years ago. Your brother-in-law and I spent the
first two years of our marriage assuming secrets and honesty were
opposites, when actually the opposite of honesty is just pretending
there's nothing there at all."

"You have secrets from each other?"

"Everyone has secrets from each other, Robyn. The healthy version
isn't no secrets. It's not pretending the gaps don't exist." A pause.
"I still haven't told him about the money I lost on that terrible
investment his cousin talked me into, for instance. He knows there's
something. He's chosen not to push. I'll tell him eventually, when I'm
ready, and he'll be annoyed, briefly, and then he'll get over it,
because the not-telling was never actually about him."

"That's a very specific example to have ready."

"I've had a while to think about it," Fiona says, dryly, "same as you
apparently have now, with your envelope woman and her mysterious
theatre contract. Sounds like you've found someone who's already
worked out something it took me a decade of marriage to learn. Hold
onto her."

"I intend to," I say, and mean it more than the three words let on,
and Fiona, for once, doesn't push for anything further, just lets the
sentence sit there between us, satisfied, apparently, that she's said
what needed saying.

·

I think about the envelope rule for days afterward, turning it over at
odd moments — waiting for the kettle, walking down Dumbarton Road,
lying awake doing the arithmetic I've stopped being able to help doing
about anything that actually matters to me. It occurs to me, somewhere
around the third day of turning it over, that I've spent six years
running this station on a version of the opposite principle: manage
every gap before anyone notices it's there, close every silence before
it has the chance to become a question. Nadia's rule is the first
piece of relationship architecture I've ever encountered that assumes
the gaps are allowed to simply exist, unmanaged, as long as everyone's
honest that they're there.

I mention as much to her, eventually, once I've had proper time to sit
with it. "I think I've spent my whole adult life trying to eliminate
every gap instead of just naming them."

"I know," Nadia says. "I noticed that about you roughly a week in."

"You could have said something."

"I did say something. I gave you the envelope rule." She almost
smiles. "I don't tend to explain things twice, Robyn. I say them once,
properly, and trust you to do the rest of the work yourself, in your
own time. It generally lands better that way."

I don't have an answer for that except to admit, quietly, that she's
right, which by now has become something of a running theme in this
particular education.

## Chapter Three — Terms

She comes and finds me.

That's the part I didn't expect, and the part I'd think about for
weeks afterward: I had gone home, properly given it up as a no, and at
half past midnight my phone lights up with a text that says, in full,
*come back*, from a number I'd had saved for three years under NADIA —
OVERNIGHT and had never once used for anything except the rota.

I come back. Of course I come back. I would come back for considerably
less than this.

The walk from my flat to the station at half past midnight is fifteen
minutes I could do with my eyes shut, and mostly do — down past the
chip shop with its shutters down, along the stretch where the streetlights
go orange-then-white-then-orange again for no reason anyone's ever
been able to explain to me, over the little humped bridge where you can
just about see the Kelvin below, black and moving fast after a day of
rain. I don't let myself hope on the way there. Hoping, at half past
midnight, on a fifteen-minute walk, felt at the time like tempting
something.

She's at the desk when I get there, and she doesn't stand up, and she
doesn't say hello. She holds up a cue sheet — the paper kind, the ones
we still print for the automated overnight log because the software
crashes if you look at it wrong — and turns it over to show me the
back, where she's written three things in block capitals with the
station's own biro, the one on a string that nobody has ever managed
to lose.

"I've got conditions," she says.

"Okay."

"One." She doesn't look at the paper. She knows it already; she's
reading it to me from memory, the paper just there so I have something
to look at instead of her while she says it, which I understand, later,
is its own kind of kindness. "Nothing physical that isn't discussed
first. On air or off. I don't care how small. A hand on an arm gets
discussed before it happens, not apologised for after."

"Agreed."

"Two. My actual job doesn't get mentioned. Not as colour, not as
backstory, not as a fun fact for a caller. The overnight desk stays the
overnight desk. The theatre thing stays off the record entirely." A
beat. "There's a reason for that and I'm not explaining it tonight."

"You don't have to explain it at all."

"I know I don't," Nadia says, and something about the way she says it
— quick, almost defensive, like she'd braced for me to push and is
recalibrating now that I haven't — makes me think this is the first
time in a while anyone's taken *no explanation needed* at face value
rather than as an opening bid.

"Three." She finally looks up. "If I say stop — on air, off air, mid
sentence, doesn't matter — the segment ends there and nobody argues
about it live. Not you, not the panel, not a caller. It ends. Same
minute."

I look at the three lines on the back of the cue sheet, in a stranger's
handwriting that I am about to spend six weeks getting to know very
well, and I have the sudden, entirely inconvenient thought that I have
never once in my adult life had anyone hand me terms like this — not a
partner, not a landlord, not the panel, nobody — and that the fact
they're arriving now, from a woman who has said eleven words to me in
three years, is doing something to my chest that I do not have time to
examine tonight.

"Say roughly eleven words to me in three years," I say, "and then hand
me a legal document."

"Four of them were about a cable."

It's the first thing resembling a joke either of us has ever made in
each other's direction, and it lands so precisely and so drily that I
laugh before I've decided to, an actual laugh, the kind that surprises
you on the way out.

Nadia doesn't smile. She does something very close to it, around the
eyes, that I will learn to read as the real thing, the actual signal
under everything else.

"Is that a yes," I say.

"That's a yes," she says, "with three conditions and a fourth one I
haven't said out loud yet, which is that if this is going to work at
all, you need to stop apologising for asking me for things. You asked.
I said no. You asked again, worse. Then you told me the truth, which
was that you'd run out of people, and that's the only version of the
ask I was ever going to say yes to. So say things like that. Don't
manage me into it."

I don't say anything for a second, because I am doing the thing where
I file that away to take out again later, at four in the morning,
and turn over from every angle.

"Can I ask why," I say, instead of *understood*, because the question
has been sitting behind my teeth since I got the text. "Why you
changed your mind. You don't have to answer that either, obviously,
given everything you've just said about not owing me explanations."

Nadia looks at the cue sheet for a second, not at me. "Because you
came back," she says, eventually. "You went home. I watched you go on
the monitor, actually, out the front, and I thought — she's not going
to ask again, she's decided it's a no and she's letting it be a no.
And then I texted you two words and you were back inside twenty
minutes, in the rain, without asking what I wanted first." A small
shrug, more in the shoulders than anywhere else. "That's the version
of you I could work with. Not the one who explains. The one who
shows up."

"Understood," I say, eventually, and mean considerably more by it than
I did thirty seconds ago.

"There's one more thing," I say, before she can turn all the way back
to the desk. "Money. I haven't worked out what I can actually offer
you, but I'm not going to pretend this is a favour that costs you
nothing and expect you to do it for the good of community broadcasting."

Nadia considers this properly, in the way she considers everything.

"A cut of whatever the renewal secures," she says, eventually. "Not a
number now. A percentage, agreed in writing, once you know what
there is to cut. I don't need it. I'd like it on paper anyway, because
things that stay unwritten have a way of becoming favours, and I've
done enough unpaid favours for one lifetime."

"Agreed. In writing. This week."

"Good." She turns back to the desk, picks the headphone cups back up,
one on, one off. "Six weeks. Thursdays. I'll want the format by
Tuesday."

"You'll want the—"

"I'm producing this," Nadia says, without turning around, in the tone
of a woman informing me of a fact rather than negotiating one. "You're
presenting it. I am not doing this badly, and you don't know how to do
it at all yet, so: Tuesday. Format. Go home."

I go home. I do not, for the first time in about six years, spend the
walk doing arithmetic about the roof.

I spend it thinking about four words about a cable, and what it might
take, over six weeks, to collect the rest of them.

I text Priya from the bridge, halfway home, because I know she'll
still be awake and because somebody has to be told. *She said yes.
Conditions attached. Format due Tuesday.*

The reply comes back in under a minute: *OF COURSE SHE SAID YES. I
TOLD YOU. Also I am going to need to know the conditions, this is now
my business too.*

*It's not your business.*

*I do the gossip AND the desk. It's structurally my business.*

I don't reply to that, mostly because she's right, and partly because
I'm smiling at my phone on a bridge in the rain at one in the morning
in a way that feels, for the first time in longer than I'd like to
admit, like something other than management.

·

I draft the written agreement the next morning, at the kitchen table,
before I've had any tea at all, because I want it done before the
version of me that overthinks everything gets a chance to complicate
it. It ends up being one page: a percentage, defined against whatever
the renewal secures, payable within thirty days of it being confirmed,
signed by both of us, witnessed by Priya, who takes the responsibility
with such gravity that she insists on using her best handwriting for
the date.

"This is very official," she says, examining the document with the
scrutiny of a woman twice her age. "You've even put a clause about
what happens if the renewal falls through entirely."

"I have."

"That's very you. Planning for the version where it all goes wrong
before you've even started the version where it goes right."

"That's called being prepared, Priya."

"That's called being frightened of hoping properly," Priya says, with
the specific bluntness only a seventeen-year-old can deploy without
it landing as cruelty, and hands the paper back before I can construct
a defence, and goes to put the kettle on, leaving me standing there
with a legally sound document and the uncomfortable sense that she's
just diagnosed something about me more accurately than any of the
document's actual clauses manage to.

·

Word gets round the volunteer network faster than either of us
intends, mostly because Priya cannot, structurally, keep a piece of
good news to herself for longer than the time it takes to text it, and
by Thursday lunchtime the corkboard by the kettle — the one Malcolm
put up his first year and nobody's ever taken down, papered thick with
listener postcards and a decade of scrawled thank-yous, which everyone
who's ever worked here simply calls the wall of fame — has acquired a
new addition: a torn-off strip of paper in Priya's handwriting reading
ROBYN + NADIA, COUPLES HOUR, WATCH THIS SPACE, pinned dead centre where
nobody could possibly miss it.

"Take it down," I tell her.

"I will not. It's morale."

"It's premature."

"It's *accurate*," Priya says, with the specific stubbornness of
someone who has already decided this argument is over. "You're doing
the show. The show is happening. The board records things that are
happening. I'm not putting anything on there that isn't true."

Sandra, one of our longest-running regulars — a woman who's rung in
faithfully every single Thursday for four years, mostly to complain
gently about the music selection — corners me by the kettle that
afternoon with the specific delight of someone who's been handed
gossip she didn't have to work for.

"Is it true," she says, "that you've finally got yourself a girlfriend,
after all this time worrying about everybody else's love life instead
of your own?"

"It's a radio segment, Sandra."

"It's about time is what it is," Sandra says, entirely unmoved by my
attempt at professional distance, patting my arm with the specific
firmness of a woman twice my age who has decided she's allowed to. "I've
watched you run yourself ragged for this place for years. Somebody
ought to be running you ragged back, for a change, and I don't mean the
roof."

I don't have a good answer for that either, and I'm starting to
suspect, standing by a kettle with a scrap of paper above my head
announcing a relationship that doesn't technically exist yet, that I'm
going to spend the next six weeks not having good answers for quite a
lot of things.

·

I draft the announcement for the station's own social accounts that
evening, alone in the office, and delete it four times before I settle
on something that doesn't oversell what we've actually got yet, which
turns out to be a harder needle to thread than any funding application
I've ever written.

*Big news from Kelvin Row*, the final version reads, *starting
Thursday — a brand new live segment, On the Level, hosted by two of our
own. Tune in and find out.* Deliberately vague. Deliberately short. I
have learned enough, in three weeks of dealing with a woman who
distrusts anything oversold, to know that the version of this
announcement that would have satisfied me a month ago — breathless,
over-adjectived, doing entirely too much work to convince people of a
feeling before they'd even had the chance to have it themselves — is
exactly the version Nadia would have vetoed on sight.

I show it to her before I post it, which I didn't have to do, and
which I do anyway, because I've started to understand that the
partnership only works if she gets to see things before the public
does, not after, regardless of whose account it's going out under.

She reads it twice. "It's fine," she says, which from her, I am
learning, is close to a rave review.

"You could sound more enthusiastic."

"I could," she agrees, "but then it wouldn't sound like me, and the
whole point of doing this properly is that it has to sound like both
of us, or the audience will clock the seam within a week."

I post it as written. It gets, by station standards, an enormous
response within the hour — shares, comments, Sandra typing an entire
paragraph of exclamation marks that somehow manages to convey more
genuine warmth than most properly punctuated sentences I've ever
received — and I sit there afterward, watching the notifications climb,
feeling the specific vertigo of having set something in motion that is
now, entirely, out of my hands.

·

Fiona rings that evening, having apparently seen the post already
through some mutual connection I've never fully understood the
mechanics of, and doesn't bother with hello.

"Nadia Quinn," she says. "The overnight one. That's who said yes."

"That's who said yes."

"Robyn." A pause, the specific pause of a sister recalibrating a
mental file in real time. "I don't think I've ever heard you say two
words about her in nine years. Not one. And now she's your fake
girlfriend, on the record, with a public announcement and everything."

"I know how it looks."

"I'm not saying how it looks. I'm saying it's interesting that the
person you picked, out of an entire building of volunteers, is the one
person you've apparently never once mentioned to me. That's not
nothing, Robyn. That's actually rather a lot, dressed up as nothing."

I don't have a ready answer for that, because I suspect she's
identified something before I've fully identified it myself, and Fiona,
sensing she's landed the point cleanly, doesn't push further, just
lets it sit there between us on the phone line, satisfied, the way she
generally is once she's said the true thing and doesn't need to grind
it in any further.

"Keep me posted," she says instead, gently, "on all of it. The show.
Her. Whichever parts you're ready to tell me," and rings off, leaving
me to sit with the specific unsettled feeling of having been seen more
clearly by my own sister, from several hundred miles away, than I've
quite managed to see myself.

## Chapter Sixteen — The Tape

She turns up at my flat with a cassette player from the station's store
cupboard, unasked, because I mentioned the tape once, in passing, weeks
ago, in the specific offhand way I mention things I'm not ready to have
a conversation about, and Nadia — whose love language, it turns out, is
doing the practical thing rather than saying the soft one — has clearly
been carrying that mention around ever since, waiting for the right
evening to put it down.

"You don't have to," I say, looking at it on my kitchen table like it's
live.

"I know," she says. "I brought it anyway."

I play four seconds of my mother's voice. That's all I manage — four
seconds of a woman I haven't heard in years saying something entirely
ordinary about the weather — and then I turn it off, and Nadia doesn't
fill the silence, and doesn't fix it, and doesn't tell me it's alright.
She just sits there, present, for as long as it takes, which turns out
to be considerably longer than four minutes.

"You don't have to be the reliable one in this room," she says,
eventually. "I'm not going anywhere because you cried at a cassette
player."

I don't have a clever answer for that. I don't think one exists. I
just sit with it — with her, in the quiet — and something that has been
held very tightly in my chest for a long time loosens, not all the way,
but enough that I notice the difference.

"Tell me about her," Nadia says, after a while, carefully, in the
voice she uses when she's checking whether a door is actually open or
just unlocked.

So I do. Not the funeral, not the last year, which I've told
approximately nobody in any real detail — but the ordinary things, the
ones that don't hurt in the acute way, only in the low background way
that never quite goes: that she taught me to read a rate card the way
some parents teach fractions, at the kitchen table, with a red pen;
that she used to ring the station every single Tuesday at exactly the
same time, not to check up on me, just because Tuesday was Tuesday
and she liked the routine of it; that the last thing she ever said to
me, in person, not on a tape, was an instruction about the insurance
that I still, six years later, follow to the letter, as though
following it keeps some part of the conversation open.

Nadia doesn't try to make any of it neater than it is. She just
listens, the way she listens to everything, head tilted a few degrees
off centre, and somewhere in the telling I stop performing being
alright and start actually being, briefly, not alright, in front of
another person, on purpose, which — I am realising, as I say it — may
be the single hardest thing I have done in this entire strange autumn.

·

Later that week, a quiet night at the desk, both of us working late for
no practical reason except that neither of us wants to be the one who
leaves first.

The room narrows the way a room does. My hand goes to her jaw, slow,
no plan behind it, and she leans into it before either of us has said
a word, and for one entire second I am certain of exactly what's about
to happen.

Priya's key in the front door lock — badly timed, entirely innocent,
completely unaware of what she's just interrupted — ends it before it
becomes anything more than that.

We both laugh about it, too loudly, too fast, in the specific way of
two people who were about a second from something neither of us has
said out loud yet, and the not-saying, by now, is doing more work than
any single sentence could.

"For the record," Priya says, dropping her bag on the good chair,
entirely oblivious, "I need the desk for twenty minutes, there's an
admin thing." She looks between the two of us, at whatever's left on
our faces, and adds, with the specific weary patience of a teenager
who has clearly decided this is simply how adults behave now, "I'm
going to keep interrupting this, aren't I. Forever. Until you sort it
out."

"Probably," I say.

"Great," Priya says, and sits down at the desk, entirely unbothered,
and neither Nadia nor I says another word about it, though I catch her
eye once, across the room, and something passes between us that isn't
disappointment at all — closer, if anything, to relief, that whatever
this is, it isn't going anywhere, interruption or not.

We end up on the fire escape again, later, once Priya's admin thing
has swallowed the desk properly and there's nowhere else quiet enough
to stand. The rain's stopped for once, which feels, after six weeks of
almost constant weather, like the city taking a breath.

"Do you ever think," I say, "about how much of this we've built out of
places that aren't actually private? A fire escape. A corridor with
buckets in it. A stool that keeps moving six inches at a time."

"I think about it," Nadia says. "I think it's because neither of us
has ever had anywhere private that felt safe enough to use properly.
So we've been building it out of the scraps instead — the bits of the
building nobody else wants."

"That's a very bleak way of putting something quite nice."

"It's not bleak," she says. "It's just accurate. You can build
something real out of scraps. People do it all the time. It's just
slower, and it looks strange from the outside, and you have to trust
the scraps a lot more than you'd have to trust something that started
out whole."

I think about that for the rest of the evening — about scraps, and
what you can build out of them if you're patient and mean it — and
find that I don't mind, particularly, that ours has been built this
way. Some of the sturdiest things I know were never meant to hold this
much weight, and hold it anyway.

·

We start, without ever formally agreeing to it, spending most evenings
together that neither of us is scheduled to work — not always at
either flat, sometimes just walking, down along the river path where
the Kelvin runs fast and shallow over its stones, sometimes as far as
the Botanics, where Nadia turns out to have an entire private
taxonomy of which benches are dry after rain and which only look dry.

"You've mapped this whole park," I say, the third time she steers us
away from a bench that looks, to my eye, perfectly fine.

"I've had a lot of evenings to fill," she says, which I understand,
by now, to be doing a great deal of quiet work underneath its
simplicity — three years of evenings, mostly alone, mapped out street
by street and bench by bench, the specific loneliness of someone who
built herself a whole private geography rather than ask anyone to
share the ordinary kind.

I don't say anything about that. I just remember which benches are
dry, going forward, and start noticing, myself, which ones aren't,
until the mapping becomes something we do together rather than
something she carries alone.

·

Priya, predictably, notices the pattern of these evenings before
either of us has said anything about them out loud, and produces, one
Monday morning, a printed calendar with our unofficial routine
plotted onto it in colour-coded blocks, labelled, with no attempt at
subtlety whatsoever, THINGS THAT AREN'T FOR THE SHOW.

"I've noticed a pattern," she announces, sliding it across the desk.

"Priya."

"I'm not saying anything about the pattern. I'm just documenting it.
For posterity." She taps a Tuesday. "This one's the Botanics. This
one's the chip shop. This one I couldn't work out, so I've just
written QUIET NIGHT, which could mean anything, and I've chosen not to
speculate further, out of professionalism."

"Your professionalism is doing a lot of work today."

"It usually does," Priya says, entirely unbothered, and goes back to
whatever she was doing before, leaving the calendar on the desk where
it stays, pinned up eventually, next to the wall of fame, an
accidental record of a life quietly assembling itself out of ordinary
Tuesdays.

·

We have, by this point, a routine, which is a thing I have never
once in my adult life had with another person outside a work
schedule, and which I find myself guarding, privately, with a
fierceness that surprises me.

Tuesdays are the Botanics, weather permitting, or the good chip shop
if it isn't. Wednesdays are usually quiet — Nadia's longest shift of
the week, and I let her have the evening to herself without treating
the absence of contact as anything other than exactly what it is.
Fridays, after the broadcast, we cook badly together in one flat or
the other, taking it in turns, an arrangement that has produced, so
far, one inedible curry, one entirely acceptable pasta, and one
memorable disaster involving a kitchen timer neither of us thought to
set.

"We're bad at this," I say, surveying the wreckage of the timer
incident, a smoke alarm still ringing faintly somewhere down the hall.

"We're new at this," Nadia corrects, fanning smoke away from the
hob with a tea towel, entirely unbothered by the chaos in a way that
tells me, more than any single conversation could, how much safer she
feels in a burnt kitchen with me than she's felt in a great many
tidier rooms with other people. "Give it time. Everything gets easier
with practice, even the bad cooking."

I believe her. I've started, this month, believing most things she
tells me, on the grounds that she has yet to be wrong about a single
one of them.

·

Priya, inevitably, has opinions about the burnt-dinner incident, having
heard about it secondhand from the smoke alarm's continued and
apparently permanent grudge against the kitchen, which now goes off
whenever anyone so much as toasts bread.

"You've broken the smoke alarm," she announces, the following Monday.
"It's traumatised. It goes off if I so much as look at the grill."

"That's not how smoke alarms work, Priya."

"It's exactly how this one works now. I've named it. It's called
Kevin, after the incident, and Kevin does not trust either of you near
an open flame ever again." She says this with the specific gravity of
someone who has genuinely decided a domestic appliance has developed a
personality disorder as a direct result of witnessing our cooking.

"We'll buy a new alarm."

"You will not replace Kevin. Kevin has been through something. Kevin
deserves better than being discarded the moment he becomes
inconvenient." She pauses, softening slightly. "Also I've noticed you
two are cooking together at all now, regularly, which four months ago
would have seemed about as likely as the transmitter fixing itself, so
I'm choosing to interpret Kevin's trauma as evidence of genuine
progress rather than a problem to solve."

"You've built an entire philosophy around a smoke alarm."

"I contain multitudes," Priya says, echoing, without any apparent
awareness that she's doing it, almost exactly my own line back at me
from weeks earlier, and goes back to her spreadsheet, leaving me
standing in the kitchen wondering, not for the first time, how much of
my own vocabulary has quietly become the building's shared dialect
without my ever noticing the transfer taking place.

·

Sandra, our long-serving widow-caller, drops by the station in person
one Friday afternoon — the first time she's ever visited rather than
simply rung in — bearing a tin of shortbread she insists is "nothing
special, just a thing I do," which turns out, on tasting, to be
considerably better than anything Dev's ever brought in penance.

"I wanted to see the place," she says, looking round at the buckets,
the whiteboard, the wall of fame now three rows deep in printed
messages. "You talk about it every week like it's a person. I wanted
to meet the person properly."

"It's mostly damp and cold tea," I tell her, honestly.

"It's not, though," Sandra says, with the specific firmness of someone
who has clearly rehearsed the correction. "It's the thing that's in my
kitchen every Thursday at seven, keeping me company when the flat's
too quiet to bear on its own. That's not damp and cold tea. That's
considerably more than that, whatever it looks like from where you're
standing."

I don't have a ready answer for that, so I offer her more tea instead,
and she stays the better part of an hour, telling Priya and me stories
about her late husband that have nothing to do with the show at all —
a holiday in Millport gone comically wrong, a wedding-day mishap
involving a borrowed car that wouldn't start — and it occurs to me,
listening, that this is the part of the job Nadia was right about
weeks ago: you rarely see where the work lands, and then, occasionally,
it turns up at your kettle with a tin of shortbread, and reminds you,
plainly, exactly what all of it was ever actually for.

·

Fiona rings that evening, mostly to hear about Sandra's visit, having
apparently decided that the ordinary, unglamorous details of the
station's regulars are, at this point, as interesting to her as
anything happening between me and Nadia directly.

"I like that you've got a whole cast now," she says. "Sandra. Grant.
Priya. That teenager Dev with his apology shortbread. You used to tell
me about the roof and the numbers, mostly, when you rang. Now you tell
me about people."

"I suppose I do."

"It's a good change. It means you're actually living in the place
rather than just managing it from slightly outside." A pause. "I think
that's rather what's happened with Nadia too, if I'm honest. You've
gone from managing a relationship to actually being in one, and it
shows in every story you tell me now, even the ones that aren't
directly about her."

I don't have an immediate response to that, so I let it sit, turning
it over the way I've learned to turn over most of what she says that
lands harder than a simple observation probably should, grateful, in a
way I don't fully examine, for a sister who's watched this whole
transformation from several hundred miles away and somehow still
manages to see it more clearly than I can from directly inside it.

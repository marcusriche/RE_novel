## Chapter Twenty-Four — Out of Office

Priya, formally paid now out of the renegotiated budget, hires two more
volunteers off her own initiative and hands me a finished roster
instead of a problem, the following week, without once asking me first.

"You didn't have to run this past me," I say.

"I know," Priya says. "That was the point. Try it. Not asking."

I try it. It's uncomfortable, in the specific way that letting go of a
thing you've held too tightly is always uncomfortable, and I do it
anyway, which is, I think, the entire remaining shape of the thing I
needed to learn.

Nadia, at the desk, headphone cup off on one side as always, doesn't
say anything about it. She just catches my eye across the room, in
exactly the way she did in week one, except everything about what it
means between us has changed completely.

The theatre contract, in the end, goes back in its envelope, unsigned,
not because she decided against it in one dramatic conversation but
because the six months on offer clashed with a season of Static she'd
already, without telling anyone, started planning properly for the
first time — segments with themes, a loose arc across the autumn,
the unmistakable behaviour of someone building something she intends
to still be building next year.

"You could still take work like that," I say, once, carefully, not
wanting the question to sound like a test. "Touring. If you wanted
to."

"I know I could," Nadia says. "I'm choosing not to, this year, because
I want to see what happens if I stay somewhere on purpose instead of
because leaving felt too loud to risk." She looks at me for a second.
"Ask me again next year."

"I will," I say, and I mean it exactly as literally as she'd want me
to.

·

Kirsty comes by, once, on a Thursday with nothing scheduled, just to
see the place properly for the first time in eight years — not for the
panel, not with a lanyard, in an ordinary coat like anyone else who
might wander down Dumbarton Road and in through a basement door.

"It hasn't changed," she says, looking round at the buckets, the ON
AIR bulb, the whiteboard still faintly ghosted with six weeks of
crossed-out titles nobody's bothered to wipe clean.

"It has, actually. New roof fund. New line item for Static. New
volunteer roster that doesn't fall over every time somebody gets food
poisoning."

"I meant it hasn't changed in the way that matters," Kirsty says. "It's
still the place people actually need. I'd forgotten what that looked
like, up close."

I don't know what to say to that, so I offer her tea instead, badly
made, in a station mug with a chip in the rim, and she takes it, and
stays longer than either of us expects her to, and it is, in its own
small unremarkable way, its own kind of repair.

·

An ordinary Thursday, some weeks later, with nothing scheduled at all.

The amber bulb is still not fixed. The kettle still takes four minutes.
Priya runs a roster that no longer needs me in it, which I am,
slowly, learning to be glad about rather than threatened by.

"How are you," I ask Nadia, at the desk, not for the show, not for
anyone.

And she answers the actual question, for once, in words, rather than
in levels or timings or durations. "Good," she says. "Better than good.
I keep waiting for the segment to end, and it doesn't."

I write a sign for the studio door, half as a joke and half not: OUT
OF OFFICE, in Priya's careful hand, propped against the amber bulb
whenever the two of us are actually off the clock, which is a category
that exists now in a way it never quite did before.

It was never about being away from the desk. It's the one hour a week
— sometimes considerably more than an hour — that belongs to nobody's
audience but us.

No proposal. No five-years-later. Just two people who have stopped
performing anything for anyone, including, at last, each other,
choosing, on an ordinary Thursday, to come back to the same room on
purpose, with nothing at all left to broadcast.

Priya finds the sign a few weeks in and adds a second line underneath
it, in the same careful hand, without asking permission, which I've
come to understand is simply how she operates now: BACK TOMORROW. LEVELS
ARE FINE.

I leave it there. It's true, as far as I can tell, on every register
that matters — the desk's, and the other one, the one neither of us
had a word for six weeks ago and now, mostly, don't need one for at
all, because it's simply what's happening between us, plainly, out
loud, on and off the record, every ordinary Thursday since.

·

If you want the tidy version — the one the regional paper never quite
managed to print, the one Priya's spreadsheet doesn't have a tab for
— it goes something like this.

Static runs Tuesdays and Fridays now, properly funded, properly
credited, though Nadia still won't say her surname on air and nobody
who matters has ever once asked her to. The wall of fame by the kettle
has run out of corkboard twice and been rebuilt twice, wider both
times. Sandra, the widow from week two, still writes in most Fridays.
Grant from Maryhill sorted out the cutlery drawer situation and now
sends in updates nobody asked for and everybody reads anyway.

Kirsty comes down to the station more often than either of us
predicted, usually with no excuse at all, which I've come to understand
is itself the excuse — she doesn't need a reason to be somewhere she
wants to be, and she's slowly, visibly, relearning how that works,
same as I am.

Priya starts university in two years with, I have absolutely no doubt,
a media studies degree and a plan for the station that will make my
own five-year plan look like a shopping list. I've already told her
the manager's job is hers whenever she wants it. She's already told me
she's not taking it until I've finished, in her words, "learning how
to have a life outside this basement," which is either the kindest or
the most insufferable thing anyone in this building has ever said to
me, and possibly both.

And Nadia and I are, on any given Thursday, simply two people who work
together, and live four streets apart, and have stopped needing a
rundown to know what the other one means.

I ring Fiona in Aberdeen the week after the renewal comes through,
mostly to tell her properly, in full, the whole of what I only gave
her in fragments back when it was still a secret worth keeping.

"So it's real," she says, once I've finished. "Actually real. Not
just for the show real."

"Actually real."

"Good," Fiona says, with the specific satisfaction of a sister who has
waited a long time to be allowed to say *I told you so* about
something. "You've been lonely since you were nineteen years old and
decided somebody had to be the reliable one. I'm glad you finally
found someone who noticed."

I don't have an answer to that, so I don't try to build one. I just
sit with it, the way I've learned to sit with true things this
autumn, and let her be right about it, and go back downstairs to a
basement that smells of damp and diesel and, underneath it all, cold
tea, where somebody is waiting for me who noticed a long time before
I ever worked up the nerve to ask.

I take the tape out of the box in the loft, finally, on an ordinary
Sunday some weeks after the renewal, and this time I don't put it back
unplayed.

I play the whole of it, sitting on my own kitchen floor with the box
open beside me, and Nadia sits with me through the whole four minutes,
not fixing anything, not filling the silence, just present, the way
she was the first time, except this time I let the entire message
play — the bit about the transmitter insurance, the bit about the
weather, and then, right at the end, a bit I'd forgotten was even
there, four seconds I somehow never got to on the loft ladder that
first Sunday: *"Tell that station of yours I'm proud of it. And of
you. I don't say that enough. Don't let it become boring, hen, and
don't you dare let it close."*

I sit with that for a long time afterward, the coincidence of it —
my mother, unknowingly, years before Malcolm ever said the words to
me directly, using almost exactly his phrase, as though the whole
building had simply always run on the same two rules, passed hand to
hand, mother to founder to me, without any of them ever comparing
notes.

"She sounds like she knew you very well," Nadia says, quietly, once
the tape's finished and the room's gone back to ordinary quiet.

"She did," I say, and for the first time in six years, saying that
sentence doesn't cost me anything at all. It just feels true, and
settled, and finally, properly, grieved.

·

Fiona comes down from Aberdeen again a few months later, for an
ordinary weekend rather than a wedding, and meets Nadia properly for
the first time, over a dinner that Nadia insists on cooking despite
her well-documented culinary limitations, which produces, predictably,
one slightly burnt but entirely edible attempt at something involving
chicken.

"I like her," Fiona tells me afterward, in the kitchen, drying dishes
while Nadia and Priya argue good-naturedly in the other room about
whether the automation queue needs a fourth safeguard or whether three
is, as Priya insists, already excessive. "I like her a lot, actually.
She listens to you properly. I've watched people talk at you for years,
Robyn. She's the first one I've watched actually listen."

"I noticed that too. Eventually."

"You always were slow with the important things," Fiona says, not
unkindly, nudging me with her shoulder the way she has since we were
children, "and remarkably fast with everything else. Roofs. Rotas.
Buckets. Just terribly slow with anything that mattered to you
personally."

"I'm working on it."

"I can see that," Fiona says, and looks through to the other room,
where Nadia's just won whatever the automation argument was about, and
Priya's conceding with enormous, theatrical bad grace, and something in
my sister's face settles, the specific look of someone who has spent a
long time worrying about a person and has finally, gladly, been allowed
to stop.

·

Priya does, in the end, start university two years later exactly as
predicted, media studies with a minor she invents largely on the spot
during her interview, and hands the manager's job back to me with the
specific ceremony of someone bestowing a great honour rather than
returning a burden, on the grounds that she's "not ready to run the
whole building yet, only most of it."

"You already run most of it," I tell her.

"I know," she says. "I wanted it on the record officially, though,
before I go off and let some university lecturer tell me things I
already learned properly in a basement." She hugs me, briefly and
fiercely, exactly the way she did in the corridor outside the committee
room two years earlier, before remembering her reputation and stepping
back to compose herself. "I'll be back every summer. Kevin the smoke
alarm still doesn't trust either of you, and somebody needs to keep an
eye on that situation."

"We'll manage."

"You'll manage badly, and cheerfully, the way you always do," Priya
says, "which is, I've come to understand, rather the entire operating
philosophy of this building, and I wouldn't change a single thing
about it."

She goes off to her first term with a list of instructions for the
volunteer rota she insists on leaving behind despite nobody asking for
it, and the station runs on, exactly as it always has, one leaking
roof and one amber bulb and one very good couple at a time.

I still ring Fiona most Sundays, the same as I always have, except now
there's considerably more to tell her than a roof fund and a rota, and
she still says the same thing she's said since we were children,
half joke and half genuine instruction, at the end of every call: don't
do anything she wouldn't do. I have, by now, an actual answer for that,
which I give her, most weeks, and which never once stops making her
laugh: "Too late. Already did."

·

Kirsty asks me once, months later, over a coffee that's become
something of a habit between us now rather than a rare, careful
event, whether I ever regret how it all started — the lie to the
panel, the whole elaborate scaffolding of a fake romance built to save
a leaking roof.

"Not for a second," I tell her. "I regret that it took a crisis to make
me finally ask her. But I don't regret the shape it took. I think if
I'd asked her plainly, out of nowhere, in year two or three, she'd
have said no, and rightly, because neither of us was ready yet for
what it actually turned into. The six weeks weren't the lie. They were
the only door either of us knew how to walk through at the time."

"That's a generous way of looking at it."

"It's an accurate way of looking at it," I say, borrowing, without
meaning to, exactly Nadia's own preferred phrase, and Kirsty laughs,
recognising it immediately, having heard it enough times now from both
of us that it's become something of a running joke between the three
of us whenever anyone tries to dress up a hard truth as a kindness.

"You two have started sounding alike," she says. "I mean that as a
compliment, for what it's worth. I don't think I've ever watched two
people become so completely fluent in each other's way of seeing the
world without either of them losing their own voice in the process."

I think about that observation for a long while afterward, the way I
think about most things Kirsty says now that she's finally decided to
say the true things rather than the careful ones, and find that she's
right, in the way people who've known you a long time are sometimes
uniquely positioned to be — not about the show, not about the panel,
just about the plain, ordinary fact of two people who've built
something real enough that it's started reshaping the language they
use to describe the whole of their lives.

That's the whole of it. It was never going to be a bigger story than
that. I've come to think that's rather the point.

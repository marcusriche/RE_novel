## Chapter Two — The Only Candidate

I run the actual list at midnight, sitting in the office with a mug of
tea I made forty minutes ago and have not touched, because running lists
is a thing I do instead of sleeping, and midnight is when the lists get
honest.

Sanjay presents well but tells everyone within a week, because Sanjay
has never once in his life kept a secret that wasn't actively illegal.
Fiona would do it, and would be good at it, and would also treat it as
an audition for something bigger, which is fair enough — Fiona wants a
bigger thing, and good for her — but is not what a six-week format needs
from its second lead. Marcus would overact it so hard the panel would
think we'd hired actors.

I've run through worse lists than this one, if I'm honest. I ran a
version of this exact exercise nineteen years old, having just arrived
at the station as a volunteer with nothing to offer except that I
never once missed a shift, working out which of the six other
volunteers I could trust to cover for me if my mum had a bad week.
There is a version of me that has been running this particular kind of
list — who's reliable, who's discreet, who can be trusted with
something that matters — since before I was legally allowed to sign
for the equipment I was operating, and there is a tired, four-in-the-
morning part of me that recognises exactly what it says about a life
that the skill has transferred so cleanly from *who can cover the
Tuesday slot* to *who can pretend to love me on the radio*.

I don't examine that thought properly either. There's a lot, this
particular week, that I am choosing not to examine properly, and I am
building, without noticing it, quite a substantial queue.

And then there's Nadia, who engineers the overnight slot, who has said
roughly eleven words to me in three years.

I know this because I counted, once, out of the specific boredom that
sets in around three in the morning when you're the only two people
awake in a basement and one of you is behind glass. Eleven words.
Four of them were about a cable.

The case for Nadia, run coldly, the way I run everything when I'm
tired enough to be honest about it: she's here six nights a week
already, so the schedule solves itself. She has no on-air persona of
her own to protect, no reputation as a presenter that a fake romance
could tarnish. She is, by a very wide margin, the most discreet person
on the payroll, in the sense that she has said, cumulatively, less to
me in three years than Priya says to me in a single shift.

I do not, at midnight, examine why that last part might be a problem
rather than a qualification. I will have plenty of time to examine it
later. Later is coming for me at a pace I have not yet appreciated.

I stand in the corridor outside the overnight studio for a count of
ten before I knock, if you can call it knocking when the door is glass
and she can see me the entire time I'm working up to it.

Through the glass: Nadia at the desk, one headphone cup on and one off,
the way she always sits, listening to the room the way other people
listen to music. There's a level meter in front of her doing something
gentle and green, and she is watching it the specific way you watch a
thing you trust completely.

She's compact, sturdy in a way I've always associated, probably
unfairly, with people who've spent a decade lifting flight cases in
and out of vans, forearms that look like they know things my own
don't. Grey's coming in at her temples early and she's made no attempt
at all to do anything about it, which I find, standing here working up
the nerve to ask her a genuinely unreasonable favour, unaccountably
reassuring. A woman who has stopped negotiating with her own grey
hairs is a woman who has, at minimum, made peace with something.

She doesn't look up. She knows I'm there. Of course she knows I'm
there — the whole point of her, professionally, is that she notices
everything and says almost none of it.

I knock anyway, because standing in a doorway working up the nerve to
ask someone to be your fake girlfriend is not something you get to do
silently.

·

I over-explain. I want that in the record too, because I am going to
spend a great deal of this book learning not to do it, and it would be
dishonest to start the story with a version of myself who already
knows how to stop.

I explain the panel. I explain the roof, again, though she was in the
building for the first explanation and does not, technically, need the
second. I explain Douglas's face when he said *audience-identifiable*,
and Priya's spreadsheet, and the transmitter contract.

"There's a woman on the panel now," I say, somewhere in minute four,
apropos of absolutely nothing Nadia has asked me, "who used to co-host
breakfast with me. Kirsty. She left for national radio eight years ago.
I mention this only because it's relevant to nothing and I appear to
be telling you anyway, which is a symptom of something."

Nadia says nothing. She doesn't even look up from the fader.

"The point is the show," I say, correcting course, or trying to,
"which needs to be believable, which means it needs two actual people
in it, and I have thought about this at length and there is, currently,
exactly one person on this payroll who fits the profile, discretion-
wise, and I recognise that puts you in a strange position, and I want
to be upfront that I've thought about the strangeness of the position
and I don't think that makes the position less strange, it just means
I've thought about it, which I suppose is its own kind of—"

"Robyn."

"Yes."

"You're doing the thing."

"What thing."

"The thing where you explain a request until the request stops sounding
like a request and starts sounding like weather. Something that's
simply happening to both of us, that neither of us decided."

I stop talking, mostly out of shock at being caught doing something I
didn't know I had a name for, and — because apparently there is no
natural end to over-explaining once it starts and stopping mid-flow
leaves an actual silence behind it that has to be filled with
*something* — I explain my own five-year plan for the station instead,
unprompted, entirely irrelevant, purely because I have run out of
relevant things to say and the silence where Nadia would normally
answer is doing something to me that talking seems, incorrectly, like
it might fix.

Nadia lets me finish. She has both headphone cups off now, folded
around her neck, which I will learn later is the equivalent, for her,
of putting a phone face-down on a table.

"No," she says.

I keep going. I am aware, dimly, distantly, the way you're aware of
traffic while you're crossing against a light, that I am doing the
thing where being told no is treated as information about the *shape*
of the ask rather than information about the ask itself, and I say it
again, differently — smaller stipend increase this time, a mention of
flexibility, the word *hypothetically* deployed twice in one sentence.

"You're not hearing me," Nadia says. Flat. Not unkind. Just entirely,
completely flat, the way a level meter is flat when there's nothing
coming through the desk at all. "I heard you the first time. I engineer
that segment because nobody looks at me while I do it. You're asking me
to be looked at. On purpose. By strangers, live, every Thursday, for
six weeks."

I go quiet.

This is, I want to note, an extremely rare event. I do not, generally,
go quiet. Going quiet is not a skill I have cultivated, professionally
or otherwise; I am a person who fills silences the way water fills a
bucket labelled BIG LEAK, immediately, and without being asked.

But there isn't anything left to fill it with, because she's right, and
I know she's right, and the only true thing available to me is smaller
than everything I've already said.

"I know," I say. "I'm asking anyway. Because I've run out of people
I'd rather ask."

Nadia looks at me for a long moment — the kind of look I will come, in
time, to recognise as her actually thinking, rather than her simply
waiting for me to stop talking — and doesn't say anything at all.

"For what it's worth," I say, standing in the doorway, coat already
half on, "I'm sorry. For asking. Properly sorry, not the version of
sorry where I then explain why I had to."

Nadia looks up at that — actually looks up, headphone cups still round
her neck — in a way she hasn't done through the whole of the previous
five minutes.

"Don't be sorry for asking," she says. "Be sorry if you ask again the
same way."

I don't have anything to say to that, so I don't try to manufacture
something. I just nod, and leave, and stand in the corridor for a
second afterward turning the sentence over, because it is, I am
starting to suspect, the single most useful piece of feedback anyone
has given me about myself in about a decade, delivered by a woman who
has, as far as the official record shows, said fewer than a dozen
words to me in three years.

I take the silence as a no, because it is, currently, the only
sensible thing to take it as, and I go home in the rain, past the
overnight studio's external door — which I don't stop at this time,
having already learned my lesson about that once tonight — not
knowing that the actual answer is going to arrive in about ninety
minutes, on the back of a cue sheet, in block capitals.

I think, walking home, about how little I actually know about her, for
someone I've just asked to be my girlfriend on public radio. I know
what shift she works. I know she takes her tea with milk, no sugar,
from three years of half-noticed observation rather than any actual
conversation. I don't know where she grew up, or what she studied, or
whether she has family in the city, or whether the reason she's so
good at being unseen is a skill she was born with or one she built,
deliberately, brick by brick, for a reason I haven't earned the right
to ask about yet.

It occurs to me, letting myself into my flat, dripping onto a mat that
has given up trying to cope with six years of Glasgow weather, that I
am about to spend six weeks constructing an elaborate public fiction
about a woman I know almost nothing true about at all, and that this
should probably worry me more than it currently does.

·

I do the thing I always do when a problem won't resolve, which is
make a list, on paper, at my own kitchen table, of everything I
actually do know about Nadia Quinn, assembled entirely from three years
of passive observation rather than a single direct question.

She takes her tea milk, no sugar. She arrives at the station eleven
minutes before every shift, never ten, never twelve, always eleven,
with a regularity I'd always assumed was coincidence and now suspect
is deliberate. She has a habit of touching the door frame of the
overnight studio on her way in, once, briefly, which I've never asked
about and have always found, without knowing why, quietly moving. She
laughs at almost nothing, and when she does, it's brief and completely
unguarded and gone again within a second, as though laughing for
longer than that would cost her something she isn't willing to spend.

It is, I realise, looking at the list, an inventory of a person built
entirely out of the outside of her — habits, timings, small
consistencies — with nothing at all about what any of it actually
means. I have catalogued a woman the way you'd catalogue a piece of
equipment you rely on but have never once opened up to see how it
works.

I fold the list and put it in a drawer, mostly out of embarrassment at
having made it, and go to bed telling myself, without much conviction,
that six weeks of proximity will surely fill in some of the blanks
whether either of us means it to or not.

·

I ring Fiona before I go to bed, which is against my own rule about not
calling her after ten, because I need to say the plan out loud to
someone who isn't Priya and isn't Nadia and isn't currently deciding
whether to be part of it.

"You want to what," Fiona says, in the specific flattened voice she
reserves for the moments when I have genuinely surprised her, which
does not happen often, because she's spent our whole lives assuming
she's seen every version of me there is to see.

"Ask the overnight engineer to be my fake girlfriend for a funding
panel."

"Robyn."

"I know."

"Do you even know this woman?"

"Not really," I admit, which costs me something to say out loud,
because I have spent the last hour composing an inventory of a person
built entirely out of tea preferences and doorframe habits, and hearing
myself describe the actual scale of what I don't know about her is
worse than I expected it to be. "I know her shift pattern. I know how
she takes her tea. I've counted the words she's said to me over three
years and it's not many."

"That's not a relationship, Robyn, fake or otherwise. That's a
surveillance file."

"It's temporary."

"Everything you've ever called temporary has lasted at minimum four
years," Fiona says, with the specific accuracy of a sister who has
been keeping her own quiet tally. "The station was temporary. The
manager job was temporary. You've been not-officially-dating this
station for nine years under the heading of temporary."

"This is different."

"Is it?" A pause, softer now. "I'm not trying to talk you out of it. I
think if you've decided to do it, you'll do it properly, because
that's the only setting you have. I just want you to notice, out loud,
to me, right now, before it starts, that you've built your whole life
out of being useful to institutions that can't love you back, and now
you're about to spend six weeks being visibly, publicly loved by a
woman you've never actually spoken to, and I don't think you've
thought once about what it might cost you to want that to be real."

I don't have an answer for that. I sit with the phone against my ear
for long enough that Fiona, eventually, lets me off the hook, the way
she always does once she's said the true thing and doesn't need to
grind it in further.

"Ring me after the first broadcast," she says. "I want the full
report. Every detail. I'm invested now, apparently, whether either of
us wanted that."

"You're allowed to be invested."

"I know I am," Fiona says. "I'm your sister. It's the one job I never
had to apply for," and rings off before I can answer that, leaving me
alone with a phone gone quiet and a great deal more to think about
than I had an hour ago.

·

I go in early the next morning, before Priya, before anyone, and sit at
the desk in the empty studio with the amber bulb dark and the whole
basement holding the specific hush it only has at that hour, before
the boiler's kicked in and the pipes have started their daily
complaint about the cold.

I run the list one more time, on paper this time rather than in my
head, because I've learned over six years that a problem written down
behaves differently to a problem merely turned over — it gets smaller,
usually, or at least it gets honest about its actual size rather than
whatever size worry has inflated it to overnight.

Nadia's name sits at the bottom of the page, underlined once, and I
look at it for a long time in the grey early light, trying to work out
whether underlining it was an accident of the pen or a decision I made
without quite noticing I was making it. I decide, eventually, that it
doesn't matter which. The name's underlined either way, and I'm not
going to un-underline it just because admitting to a decision after
the fact feels more exposed than pretending it happened on its own.

I fold the page and put it in my pocket rather than the drawer this
time, which I notice, walking down to put the kettle on before anyone
else arrives, is itself a small change in behaviour I don't examine
too closely, in case examining it talks me out of whatever I'm
currently, quietly, deciding to do.

The kettle takes its usual four minutes. I stand there watching it,
the way I'll come to stand and watch it a great many more times before
this particular fortnight is over, and think, not for the first time
this week, that some decisions arrive not as a single moment of
clarity but as a long accumulation of small ones — an underlined name,
a pocket instead of a drawer, a kettle you wait for instead of setting
and walking away from — until one morning you look up and realise
you've already decided, some time back, without ever holding a formal
vote on it with yourself.

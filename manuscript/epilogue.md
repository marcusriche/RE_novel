## Epilogue — Thaw

By the middle of May the river had forgotten ice entirely.

It ran green-brown and businesslike past the north bank windows,
carrying a barge and a rental kayak and a great deal of pollen,
and the bridge lights came on at eight-forty now instead of
four-fifteen, which Elena took personally, as a dividend.

The glass had been lettered in April, in gold leaf, by an old man
from Bridgeport who did three shops a year and charged like a
surgeon:

ELENA'S — BOOKS BALANCED · DINNER AT SIX

Inside, at four in the afternoon, the small dining room stood in
its pre-service hush. Twenty covers. White paper over the cloths,
because Lucia had ruled — with the finality of a woman who has
survived a winter and is not interested in further discussion —
that fine linen was for people with something to prove. From the
kitchen came the sound of a partner in her own name running her
own pass: Ferro & Moretti, LLC, filed in March, articles framed by
the coat hooks where anyone in the room could audit them.

Danny came through with the water pitchers, apron on over a
CPA-review textbook he refused to be caught studying, studying it.
He'd taken the waiter job "temporarily" in April and had
reorganized the walk-in by the second week — sorted by turn rate,
not by category, with a laminated map on the door that Lucia had
threatened twice to burn and had not burned. Out of spite, he
said, when anyone asked why he was doing the review course. It ran
in the family, the spite. So, it turned out, did the aptitude. He
still counted on his fingers. He counted everything.

On the corner table — her table — the green banker's lamp burned
over ruled paper.

The day's post lay beside it: two invoices, a wine rep's
brochure, a check from a bakery on Grand whose books she now kept
and whose owner had come to her specifically, in April, and said,
*you're the one who reads handwriting, yeah?*

And on top of the pile, the envelope from the US Attorney's
office, opened, read twice, refolded along its own crease.

Trial calendar. *United States v. Moretti, S., et al.* Her
appearance confirmed for the third week of June, four days
estimated, direct and cross.

Sal's lawyers had stopped calling her lawyers in March. The et al.
did not include a certain enforcer, who remained — per the last
anyone had heard, which had come by way of a cousin in Kansas City
and had the ring of truth — weather in another state. There had
been no reprisal. There had been no contact of any kind. Page four
hundred and six sat in a government warehouse in a numbered box,
unread by anyone but a woman who had no further use for it and a
man who had left town on the arithmetic.

She would testify in June, under her own name, and sleep in July.

She read the calendar the way she read weather now — something
that would arrive, be endured, and pass — in a city that held her
whole life and no longer held a single lien on it.

At 5:55 the bell over the door went, and she didn't look up,
because she didn't need to.

Big man. Moving like he'd planned the route. Off duty since five,
reading glasses already on, which meant he'd been at the
training-division drafts on the train again — red-penning some
poor recruit's evidence log with the zeal of the reformed. The
module was called *Watch the Hands* and had been oversubscribed
twice. He never told them why it was called that. Some of them
worked it out.

Cole hung his coat on the third hook — his hook; there had been no
ceremony about it, the hook had simply become his, the way true
entries post — and took the second chair at the corner table, and
set a small paper bag by her elbow.

Two cannoli from the place on Grand he maintained was superior to
Lucia's. A heresy he committed weekly, on the record, in writing
if pressed, because the argument was the point and both parties
knew it and Lucia had twice sent one back with a note.

"Findings," he said, nodding at the trial calendar.

"Scheduled. Survivable." She squared it under the lamp. "Yours?"

"Two trainees can now spot a forged invoice in under a minute
without a machine." He stole her pen, which was also weekly. "One
of them asked if it was true I learned questioned documents from a
hostile witness."

"And?"

"I declined to correct the record on *hostile*."

"Smart. It'll hold up better in the memoir."

Upstairs — they still said *her place*, though the bottom drawer
had been his since March and lately the closet was undergoing what
she classified in writing as adverse possession — dinner would
happen at whatever hour the last table left. Quality time, his
ledger called it. Acts of service, said hers: she cooked on
Sundays now, badly, with total command of the costings and no feel
for salt, and he ate every plate like sworn testimony, and Lucia
had twice threatened to file an intervention under the
partnership's health-and-safety authority.

At six Lucia called service, and the room filled the way small
rooms do, all at once: neighbors, two regulars from the bakery,
Marco Reyes-Vega and his girlfriend and the baby, who had a
standing table and a bad reputation with breadsticks. Two of
Danny's study group. A woman who came alone every Thursday with a
paperback and had never once been made to feel like an
inconvenience about it.

Elena worked her floor with an eye on the pass and an ear on the
till, and now and then, crossing behind the corner table, let her
hand rest one second on a gray wool shoulder.

*Anywhere*, said the scope. She exercised it daily, the way you
exercise any hard-won line of credit — to keep it warm, and
because the terms permitted it, and because she had spent thirty
years booking her life like contraband and had retired the
practice in writing.

Near close, when the room had thinned to coffee and the river
outside had gone to black glass and bridge lights, she sat at last
in the lamp's circle and pulled the day's ruled sheet toward her.

Receipts squared. Kitchen closed out. Two lines left.

Her father had ended every day of his working life with the same
discipline. One line for the money. One line for the truth. He had
told her, at this same lamp, when she was fourteen and impatient
with him: *a book that carries only money goes false by spring,
Elena. The money line keeps you honest with other people. The
other line keeps you honest with yourself, and only one of those
is optional, and it is not the one you think.*

For thirty years she had kept everyone's first line and no one's
second.

*Covers: 38. Till: balanced,* she wrote, in the narrow upright
hand, sevens crossed.

Then, beneath it, the second line — the true one, the entry the
whole green book of her life had been trying to reach:

*Two cannoli, one lamp, dinner at six. Nothing owed.*

She looked at it for a moment, the way you look at a figure you've
been carrying so long you half expect it to be wrong.

It wasn't. It footed.

She turned off the lamp and went up to dinner.

**THE END**

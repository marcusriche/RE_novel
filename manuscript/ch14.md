## Chapter Fourteen — Disposition

Nico came for the ledger on Sunday night, alone, through the alley
door of a shuttered restaurant, and Elena was waiting for him,
because she had run out of rooms to be found in and had decided to
choose the last one herself.

Police tape on the front glass. The dining room dark, chairs up,
the federal boxes gone. Only the back office lived — one green
circle of lamplight on the bare desk, and the bookkeeper behind it
with her hands folded, and no witness in the building for the first
time in eleven years of careful arithmetic.

"The family's done," Nico said, without preamble. The manners were
gone; what stood in her doorway was the service department itself,
coat open, hands loose. "Sal's lawyers are pricing surrender. The
crews are calling cousins in other cities. You know what I have,
after twenty-two years? A forecast: indictments by Thursday." He
came a step in. "And you know what fixes my weather. The book,
Elena. The federals will trade a man like me for a book like that.
Wherever it sleeps, it leaves with me tonight."

"You're right," she said. "It's here."

That stopped him — truth, the one tool he had no counter for. She
lifted the ledger from her lap and set it on the desk, in the green
light, three pounds of everything. His eyes went to it the way eyes
went to open vault doors.

"Sit down, Nico. Page four hundred and six."

He should not have sat. He sat. She opened the green boards, turned
the leaves with a teller's economy, and rotated the book to face
him, her finger on a column in her father's aging hand that became,
mid-page, her own younger one.

"June, eleven years ago. The vending route's take, gross, in Papa's
figures. And here — " her finger moved one line " — what you kicked
up to Sal that month, in mine. The difference is four thousand
dollars. Here's July. Here's every month since. You've been
skimming the route since before my father's hands went, and he
priced it, and I kept pricing it, because that's what the book is
for. It prices everything." She turned the page. "Eleven years,
Nico. Compounded, it buys the marina you think nobody knows about,
in your sister's husband's name, in Michigan City."

The office was so quiet she could hear the lamp's small electric
heart. Nico Gravano looked at eleven years of his own theft,
rendered in two generations of Moretti penmanship, and for the
first time in their long acquaintance his hands, resting on the
desk's edge, were the still ones.

"Here's the disposition," Elena said. "Tonight this book goes to
people who'll be very busy with Sal and with the crews — busy for
years. Whether they ever get curious about page four hundred six
depends on whether anything happens to me, to my brother, to Lucia
Ferro, or to a certain federal investigator. Nothing happens: you're
a footnote who fled. Anything happens: you're the appendix, and Sal
gets a copy in discovery, and you can explain the marina to
whichever of them finds you first." She closed the boards, folded
her hands on top. "Walk out of Chicago tonight, Nico. The math is
kind in exactly one direction."

He stood for a long moment in the doorway on his way out — she let
the silence run, one beat past comfort, hers again at last — and
what he finally said, without menace, almost professionally, was:
"Your father undercharged you people for sixty years. You were the
only one of them who ever collected full price."

Then the alley door clicked, and the enforcer was weather somewhere
else, cornered by arithmetic, not courage, which was the only kind
of cornering that ever held his kind.

Elena sat in the lamplight with her hands flat on the closed boards
and shook, thoroughly, at last, having budgeted for it.

·

The proffer room at the US Attorney's office had bad carpet, good
light, and a court reporter, which was all Elena had ever really
asked of a room: witnesses and a record.

Monday, ten a.m., daylight, per a clause spoken once in her
hallway by a man not present. Present: AUSA Priya Raman. Farrah
Okafor, for her and for Danny. A case agent she'd never met. And
in the file in front of Raman, doing its quiet work — Elena could
read paper upside down at four feet, it was her one inheritance —
a memorandum in dense block capitals titled DURESS AND REFUSAL,
authored by an investigator whose name was conspicuously absent
from the meeting.

"Ms. Moretti. Your counsel represents that you have a document."

"I have my father's ledger, and mine." She lifted it from her bag
and set it on the table, and the room changed pressure the way
rooms did around it. "Single physical original. No copies exist —
copying it was always the one entry I refused. I'll want that
noted."

Noted. Inventoried. Photographed where it lay. An evidence
technician read the number aloud — *Government Exhibit 1* — and
Elena signed the transfer form in the narrow upright hand that had
started all of this, and it was done, on the record, in daylight:
the disposition of the old book, resolved on the page.

Then she spent four hours decoding herself for them. The tribute
schedules. The vending route. The catering corrections and the one
she'd stalled — Raman slid the invoice across; Elena identified
her own coding and, in six sentences, said what it was: refusal
dressed as paperwork, the memo in the file already knew, check the
orphaned cash against the drawer inventory. And the entries that
mattered most, the ones she'd have traded all the rest for: the
transactions charged to Daniel G. Moretti, run through his name in
another's authorization code, his signature borrowed the way the
family borrowed everything, with intent to repay never.

"The coding proves the borrowing," she finished. "My brother's
name was stationery. The weight lands where it always lived —
Salvatore's chair, and the enforcer's route."

Raman set down her pen. "And your terms, Ms. Moretti?"

"Use immunity, full scope, my counsel has the draft. The
government joins Danny's release motion this week. And I testify
under my own name." She heard the room stop. "No number, no
program alias. Elena Moretti, of the Morettis, on the stand,
reading her family's true books aloud. You get a witness no jury
will ever forget, and I get the only clean exit that was ever
actually for sale. The kind where you don't leave."

It cost her the anonymity she'd spent six years pricing tickets
toward. She watched the cost post, felt the account close, and
signed.

·

He was at the riverwalk railing at dusk, under the Kinzie Street
bridge, where the ice had been. She had known he would be. His
coat, his stillness, no badge on him anywhere — a man subtracted
from his case, standing where their first refusal happened,
watching the river run dark and clear.

She stopped six feet away. The distance was itself an entry.

"I heard a rumor," Elena said, "that a memorandum walked into my
proffer file with no author attached."

"Authorship's under review." The old dry government cadence, worn
soft. He didn't turn. "Elena. You'll get the full accounting.
Every date. What I knew September fourteenth, what I knew at the
tablecloth, what I hid, when, and what it's cost so far. On paper,
in my hand, no craftsmanship. You can audit it against anything."
He looked at her then. The streetlight found what the last week
had done to his face. "No ask attached. It's not a payment. It's
just the true books. You were owed them from the first day, and I
paid in the wrong currency, and a wrong currency on time is still
a default."

The river moved. Upstream a bridge groaned with a train.

"One honest number," she said. "That's what my father called it.
The one entry that keeps a whole ledger from going false." She
came to the railing, not touching him, and looked at the water
where the ice used to be. "Brennan. The 6:40 to Carbondale. Gate
F. Six years I priced that ticket." A breath. "I never bought it.
Even the week the window stood wide open. I want that on your
record, whatever your record is now."

He was quiet for a long time. One beat past comfort. Two. Learned.

"Noted," he said, unsteady, "for the file."

Neither reached across the six feet. Nothing had been re-granted;
both kept the law of it exactly, and the exactness was itself the
tenderness — two people standing in the ruins of their withdrawn
terms, holding only the future tense between them, which for the
first time since September had a balance in it.

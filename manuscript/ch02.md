## Chapter Two — Regards from the Family

The suppliers loved her. That was the first thing Cole learned on the
canvass, and by the fourth stop it had begun to feel less like
information and more like weather.

Fulton Market at dawn, forklifts and frost-smoke, and every vendor who
did business with La Bilancia had paper so clean you could dress a wound
with it. The produce man showed him invoices going back three years,
every one initialed *EM* in the corner like a hallmark. The linen
service manager said, unprompted, "She pays net-ten. You know what
net-ten means in this town? Means you'd take a bullet for the account,"
and then heard himself and amended it to "figure of speech."

At the fish house a woman in a chef's coat was signing for crates —
Ferro, the kitchen chief from the restaurant, the one personnel had
flagged as *closest to subject*. He introduced himself and got nothing,
courteously, in two languages. She had a way of answering in Italian
precisely when the answer mattered, then apologizing for her English,
which was flawless.

"You've known Ms. Moretti a long time," he tried.

"Da una vita." A lifetime. She looked at him with dark, sorry eyes, like
he was something that had washed up on a beach she kept clean. "You want
to know about Elena? She covers other people's shortages. Write that in
your book."

He wrote it in his book.

Driving back, he assembled the picture the paper made. Every route into
the family's money ran through one set of hands, and the hands never
shook. He was starting to admire the discipline of the person deflecting
him — the way you admire a locked door as a door — and to trust her
less, which was the correct professional response, and simpler than the
other thing the interview had left him with. She'd read his worst day
off him in forty minutes and priced it in one sentence.

*That kind of entry never balances.*

He'd run six wiretaps and two informants at this family and the truest
thing anyone had said about his life came from the subject of the file.

·

Nico Gravano came on a Tuesday, carrying two espressos on a saucer like
an apology, which meant it wasn't one.

Elena watched him cross her dining room at four in the afternoon — soft-
footed for a big man, suit too good for his job — and set both cups on
the office desk, one turned toward her, handle at four o'clock, the way
her uncle took it. A message before he'd said a word: *I pour for Sal. I
am Sal, for present purposes.*

Lucia materialized in the doorway with a case of San Pellegrino she had
no reason to be carrying and began, slowly, to shelve it. Nico glanced
at her. Lucia did not leave. Eleven years in this building and she had
never once left Elena alone with Nico Gravano. Some arithmetic you did
early and never redid.

"Your uncle sends his love," Nico said. "The feds came to see you."

"They came to see the tablecloths. Everyone gets a subpoena, Nico. It's
Chicago. It's practically a parking ticket."

"He says you were wonderful." He tried the espresso, made a small
approving sound. "Stay wonderful."

"Tell him thank you."

"Danny asks about you," he said, gently, setting the cup down. Not
*Danny's lawyer says.* Not *the case.* Just her brother's name, set on
the desk between them like a small weight on a scale, and his eyes came
up to see what it moved.

Her hands lay flat on the blotter and stayed there. "Tell my uncle,"
she said, "that his books are the cleanest in the city, and the man who
keeps them company is his niece, and both of those things stay true no
matter who buys me coffee."

Nico smiled like a man reading a menu. He left the second espresso
untouched, which was also a message. Everything with these men was a
message. It was the language they'd all been raised in and it was why
she wrote things down instead.

That night, in her apartment with the deadbolt turned, she took Cole
Brennan's card out of the drawer where she'd meant to forget it. Heavy
stock. Direct line, cell handwritten under it — he'd added the cell at
the door, she remembered, citing some regulation about designated
points of contact while doing something the regulation had never
intended.

Burn it, said six years of training.

She read the name twice and put it back in the drawer.

·

The visitation room at the MCC smelled like nickels and floor wax.
Danny had lost weight in forty-six days, and he'd decided to play it as
a bit, patting his stomach through the jumpsuit like a man succeeding
at a diet. Twenty-six years old. Through the smeared glass he looked
nineteen.

"You look like Ma," he said into the handset. "That's how I know you're
worried. You get the forehead."

"I get the forehead from the ninth. Arraignment's moved up. Abruzzo
tell you?"

"Abruzzo tells me to say nothing, sign nothing, and trust the family."
His grin thinned out. "So I'm saying nothing, signing nothing."

"And the third thing?"

Danny looked at her through the glass for a while. Behind the bit, her
little brother was down there somewhere, scared in a way she recognized
from the year their father's hands went, when the whole house learned
what it cost to owe.

"Don't trust anybody, Lena," he said. "Not in here, not out there.
Whoever tells you they got me — that's the one holding the ticket on
me. That's how it works. I finally figured out the business we're in."
He tried the grin again. "Some accountant I'd have made, huh?"

"You'd have been terrible. You count on your fingers."

"I count on *you*." Fast, no bit at all. "That's my whole plan. That's
been my whole plan since I was nine."

The handset was slick under her grip. She put her free hand flat on
the counter and said what she had said to him once before, the week
they took him, the words she'd chosen the way she chose entries,
because they'd have to hold.

"You walk out of there owing nobody. I keep the books — I know exactly
what's owed, and it isn't you."

"Yeah," Danny said, quiet. "Say it again sometime. It's better than
Abruzzo's version."

Outside, on the steps, the cold came down off the lake like a verdict.
There was a payphone bank on Van Buren, one working handset, and she
used it the way she'd planned the route home — precisely, and without
letting herself think about why she wasn't using her cell.

He answered on the second ring. "Brennan."

"Hypothetically," she said, "if a pretrial detainee had a protective-
status request pending, and the request kept getting lost, who would
have to notice for it to stop getting lost?"

A pause. She could hear him decide not to say her name on an open line,
and something in her chest moved half a point on that alone.

"The duty magistrate," he said. "Or a federal officer with a reason to
ask. Per the detention standards, section —" He stopped himself. "I'd
notice," he said instead. "I'm telling you I'd notice."

She hung up on the answer because the answer was working.

He didn't log the call. She knew he wouldn't, the way she knew a column
would foot before she added it. Neither of them examined why. There
were entries you left unposted at your own risk, and they had opened
one, and it was already accruing.

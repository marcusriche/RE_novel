## Chapter Thirteen — Smaller Questions

Interview room B had a table, three chairs, a camera in the corner
dome, and a recorder, which an agent named Okafor-no-relation set
down between them with a click Elena felt in her back teeth.

Material witness. Uncharged. Free to leave once the scene was
processed, and processed, it turned out, was a word with hours in
it. At 1 a.m. they sent in the mistake: they sent Brennan, because
Brennan had the rapport, said the file, and the file was current as
of a coat hook in her hallway.

He sat across from her. The recorder ran. The room mirrored a
white tablecloth one season and one lifetime ago so exactly that
she nearly laughed at the craft of it — the world, that great
auditor, running the same transaction back through the books with
the signs reversed. He needed her to talk now. She had a tape
running now.

"Ms. Moretti. This interview is voluntary. You may end it—"

"—at any time," she said. "I remember your terms."

He flinched at *terms*, one millimeter, camera-proof. She catalogued
it without pleasure. The professional in her noted the shadows under
his eyes, the raid grit still on his sleeves, the fact that he had
squared his chair not to the door, for the first time in her
knowledge of him, but to her.

He asked the mandated questions. The premises, the records, custody
of documents. She answered each one with a smaller one — "Which
records?" "Whose inventory?" "Is that the manifest date or the memo
date?" — and watched that last needle go in, and hated that it still
cost her something to hurt him.

Then she asked her only real question, and let it stand on the tape
forever.

"September fourteenth." She kept her voice ledger-flat. "The case
memo that named my father's book. Did you know about that memo the
first day — the tablecloth, the recorder, your two combined
statutes? Did you sit across from me knowing your case already had
its hand in my safe?"

The pause was four seconds. She counted them the way she counted
everything. On tape, four seconds is an era.

"The case knew," Cole said.

The bookkeeper in her admired the craftsmanship: not a lie, load-
bearing, survivable in front of any review board. The woman in her
watched the right answer — *yes, I knew, and I should have told you
in daylight, and I chose the room instead* — die behind his teeth,
where all his true entries lived.

"Wrong answer," she said softly.

"Elena—"

"One word stops everything," she said. "Any time. No grievance
carried. Those were the terms." She leaned in so the recorder would
be sure to have it, clear, dated, witnessed. "Withdrawn."

He went white along the cheekbones. She watched the word land with
its full legal weight — everything voided, every standing yes,
every room, at the moment of utterance — and watched him accept it
without one syllable of appeal, both hands coming down flat on the
table, her gesture, learned, kept, too late.

Farrah Okafor — the relation this time; Danny's new counsel, the one
Elena had retained in secret with her own clean money in January —
arrived at 1:40 with a court order and a voice like a closing bell.
Material witness, no charges, her client was leaving. Elena stood,
took her coat, and walked out past the evidence room where forty
covers' worth of her life sat in numbered boxes, through a metal
detector that read wool and paper as wool and paper, with her
father's ledger flat against her spine, out into the black federal
morning.

Behind her, in room B, Cole Brennan sat alone with the recorder
still running and his hands still flat on the table, until the tape
ran out.

·

He was at Whitfield's door when the building opened, before coffee,
before counsel, before sense. He put three things on her desk in a
row, aligned to the edge, because even now his hands kept their
liturgy: a folded duplicate invoice. A memorandum titled DURESS AND
REFUSAL. And his credentials.

He told it in order. The intercept, the annex, the coding only he
could read. The margin note in his own hand, *dup. not located*,
which was false when he wrote it. The two a.m. phone call in
February, unlogged. The riverwalk. The terms. He gave dates. He
cited the sections he'd broken by number, one after another, a man
reading his own indictment aloud in a steady voice, and he watched
Dana Whitfield's face go through fury into something more
dangerous, which was arithmetic.

"The memo," she said finally, tapping DURESS AND REFUSAL. "This
holds? Handwriting-pattern analysis, the stalled correction, the
orphaned cash?"

"It holds. She jammed their laundering for five months, alone, with
her brother's ribs as the collateral. The invoice reads as
obstruction only if nobody can read her books." He kept his eyes
level. "I can read her books. That's the asset and the conflict.
Both go in the file."

"You're off the case as of this sentence. OPR gets all of it —
that's not mine to soften. The credentials stay on my desk until
they say otherwise." Whitfield looked at him a long moment. "You
came in on your own feet before OPR called you. That's the one page
of this that reads well. Why?"

He had spent two years assembling answers made of sections and
subparts, and none of them survived the question anymore.

"Judgment," Cole said. "Write that. It was judgment."

He signed the receipt for his own surrendered badge with the pen
from his breast pocket, the one that had initialed a napkin on Ohio
Street, and walked out of the federal building into the first
morning in nine years that did not belong to a case. The proof had
begun costing. He intended to let it.
